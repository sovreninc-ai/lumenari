# Joke Structures, Bit Development, Packets, Social-First Beats

The reference doc. Five sections.

---

## 1. Nine joke structures, with worked examples

### A. Setup → Punch
Two-line classic. Setup primes an expectation; punch breaks it.
> "I started therapy last year. My therapist said I have a hard time finishing things. I disagreed."

### B. Setup → Punch → Tag
Tag escalates or twists the punch. Fed by live laughs.
> "I started therapy last year. My therapist said I have a hard time finishing things. I disagreed."
> (Tag) "She said that wasn't an argument. I said it was a draft."

### C. Rule of three with a turn
Two on-pattern items, third breaks the pattern.
> "Three things have been hard about turning 40: my back, my knees, and the fact that I now consider 9:45 PM 'too late' to do anything."

### D. Misdirection
Audience reads a word one way; punch reveals the second meaning.
> "I told my dad I was thinking about going back to school. He asked what for. I said because I miss naps."

### E. Act-out
Joke is a voice or character. Reads thin on the page; kills live.
> "My toddler doesn't say 'I'm hungry.' He stands very close to your face and says, [low confident voice] 'we should eat.' Like a partner. Like we're in this together."

### F. Escalation
Each beat bigger than the last. Punch is the impossible final.
> "When I was 22, I'd recover from a hangover in 6 hours. At 28, a day. At 34, two days. At 41, my entire personality. I'm not hungover. I am the hangover now."

### G. List with a turn
Like rule of three, longer. The turn comes 3-5 in.
> "Things I've cried at this year: a coffee commercial. A dog I don't know. The first 8 minutes of Toy Story 3. A friend's wedding. My own laundry. Two of those I expected."

### H. The "but actually"
Setup is a common belief; punch reveals the contradicting truth.
> "Everyone says marriage takes work. It doesn't. Marriage takes scheduling. Work is the thing you do at work."

### I. Analogy attack
Compares two unlike things. The joke lives in precision.
> "Group texts are like a Costco — too big, too loud, everyone's buying something, and at least one person is having a personal crisis in aisle 4."

---

## Joke shapes to actively avoid

- "You ever notice…" openers
- "What's the deal with…"
- Jokes that include "the point is" or "which is funny because"
- Knock-knock jokes
- "Why did the chicken…"
- Punchlines at the expense of protected groups
- AI-flavored "now isn't THAT something" closers

## 2. Premise to bit (the development framework)

A bit is 3-5 jokes around one premise. Worked example.

**PREMISE**: My 4-year-old negotiates bedtime like a hostage situation. He doesn't want anything specific — he just wants me to be there longer.

**ANGLE**: Comic as victim (he's being worn down by a person under four feet tall).

**JOKE 1 (entry)**: My son has figured out that bedtime is a negotiation, and he doesn't have demands. He's not asking for water. He's not asking for one more book. He's just trying to extend the meeting.
[Setup-punch with embedded analogy.]

**JOKE 2 (deepens — different angle, not joke 1 with different nouns)**: He's the only person I've ever met who wins by losing. He doesn't have to win. I have to be there. If I'm in his room at 9:42 PM, he won.
[Reframe of the rules of the conflict.]

**JOKE 3 (tag — escalates)**: I've started countering. I'll say, "I love you, I'll see you in the morning." He'll say, "but what if there's an emergency." And then he just lays there. Waiting for one.
[Act-out tag. Earns a callback later.]

**JOKE 4 (optional callback)**: [Later in set, after a different bit] "Anyway, I have to go — there's a four-year-old at home generating emergencies."

**CALLBACK-READY PHRASE**: "generating emergencies."

The reason this is a bit and not three jokes: each beat is a different angle on the same premise. Joke 1 is the analogy, Joke 2 is the rules, Joke 3 is the move he makes back. Joke 4 reuses the language as currency. Bits live or die on this.

---

## Sanity-check: is this a bit or just one joke?

Ask, on any draft bit:

- Is Joke 2 a new angle, or Joke 1 with different nouns?
- Does Joke 3 escalate, or just continue?
- Is there a phrase or image strong enough to callback later?

If you answer no to any of these, you have one joke, not a bit. That's fine — let it be one joke and find something else.

## 3. Late-night packet structure by show

Packets are 12-15 jokes written to a specific show's monologue style.

### Daily Show / Last Week Tonight
- Longer setups (often 2-3 sentences embedding the news)
- Dry/exasperated punchlines, often with a sigh built in
- Political and corporate-stupidity heavy
- Cited sources matter — these shows reference actual articles

**Sample (template, not real news)**:
> "A new study this week found that the average American checks their phone 144 times a day. The CDC has declined to comment because they were watching TikTok."

### Late Show / Late Night
- Medium setup (one news beat, one comic move)
- Sharp punch, often with a political pivot to the absurd
- Pop-culture cross-over works

**Sample**:
> "A senator has introduced a bill banning teenagers from using TikTok during school hours. The bill was drafted in a Google Doc the senator's intern shared on TikTok during school hours."

### Tonight Show / Kimmel
- Short setups (one sentence)
- Broader, more audience-friendly punch
- Pop culture, sports, weird viral news, celebrity moments
- Less political density

**Sample**:
> "A new survey says the average wedding now costs $33,000. Or about half a starter home, if those still existed."

### SNL Weekend Update
- Very short setups, often one clause
- Joke fits in one breath, written to be read off the Update desk
- Pun-tolerant when the pun is sharp; never lazy
- "Cold" reactions encouraged in the writing — the joke does the work

**Sample**:
> "A man in Florida was arrested this week for stealing 18 ATMs from gas stations. In his defense, the ATMs were not nailed down. The gas stations also were not nailed down."

### Packet conventions across all four shows:
- Each joke stands alone — no cross-joke callbacks
- News-based: 48-hour to 7-day window
- 12-15 jokes, mixed across politics / pop culture / sports / weird local / business / internet
- Include one genuinely strange joke — packets get read fast; weird-good stands out
- Include the comic's name and contact info; do NOT include date stamps that make the packet feel stale

## 4. Social-first 30-second beat sheet

For TikTok / Reels / Shorts. The structure:

```
0:00-0:03 — HOOK
The premise stated upfront, OR the punch teased upfront, OR a visual that sets the question.
NO "Hey guys." NO "So today I want to talk about." NO intro music over the joke.

Worked example: [Direct to camera, holding a water bottle] "My toddler thinks this bottle is haunted."

0:03-0:15 — SETUP
The world of the joke, the conflict, the angle.

Worked example: "He saw me drink from it once and now every time I pick it up he goes, [act-out] 'no, that's for ghosts.' I asked him what ghosts. He said 'all of them.'"

0:15-0:25 — ESCALATION
1-2 beats that raise the stakes or sharpen the comic's predicament.

Worked example: "I'm not allowed to drink from it now. I bought a new water bottle. He told me that one was also for ghosts. There is a hierarchy of ghost water in my house. I don't know who's in charge."

0:25-0:30 — PUNCH or PUNCH-OUT
The line, the visual, or the cut that lands.

Worked example: [Cut to him drinking from said bottle, looking directly at camera] "He's drinking it. He's the one who's haunted."
```

Notes for video:
- The hook is everything. If the first 3 seconds don't tell the viewer what the joke is, they swipe.
- Captions on. Sound off is the default viewer experience.
- One joke per video. The temptation is to stack three; resist.
- The punch can be visual, audio (a cut, a sound effect), or verbal. Vary across your videos.

## 5. Diagnostic checklist for a joke that isn't landing

Run this on a joke the writer says is "almost there":

1. **Is the setup funnier than the punch?** Common. The fix is to find a sharper punch or cut the joke and use the setup as a tag for a different joke.

2. **Is the tag actually the joke?** Sometimes the "punch" the writer wrote is the setup for the real joke that they wrote one line later. Try the joke without the original punch.

3. **Is the punch landing on the wrong word?** English jokes land on the final stressed word. If the punch word is mid-sentence, the audience misses it. Rewrite to end the sentence on the punch.

4. **Does the joke explain itself?** Check for "the point is," "which is funny because," "the joke is," anywhere after the punch. Cut everything after the punch.

5. **Is the premise topical-decayed?** A joke about a story 8 days old in a fast news cycle is dead. Either find a fresh angle or shelve.

6. **Is the comic in the wrong angle?** Try the same premise with the comic as the perpetrator instead of the victim, or vice versa.

7. **Is this a performance-only joke?** Some jokes require act-out or rhythm that doesn't read on the page. If the writer says it lands in the room but reads flat, that's not a joke problem.

8. **Is the joke in someone else's territory?** Mulaney's UPS bit. Hedberg's escalator. Gadsby's setup-punch deconstruction. If the joke is in known territory, flag it and ask if the writer wants to keep going or pivot.

The diagnostic should produce 2-3 rewrites that preserve the writer's voice while fixing the diagnosed issue. Not a full rewrite — a targeted move.
