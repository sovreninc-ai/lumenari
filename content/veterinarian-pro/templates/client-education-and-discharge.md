# Templates — Discharge, Client Education, Euthanasia, Financial Options

Four templates a DVM or RVT actually uses. Each has a prompt and a worked example. All doses are placeholders for DVM verification against Plumb's. Apply species-specific cautions before client delivery.

## 1. Discharge instruction sheet

### Prompt

```
Draft a one-page discharge instruction sheet.

Patient (no PHI for the owner, but include patient details):
- Species: [dog / cat / rabbit / etc.]
- Breed if relevant
- Weight: [in kg AND lb]
- Age, sex/neuter status

What was done today: [procedure, in clinical terms — I'll have you translate]
Diagnosis / findings:
Medications going home — for each: drug, dose (DVM-determined, just write what I tell you), frequency, duration, with/without food

Activity restriction:
Feeding instructions:
Incision/e-collar instructions if applicable:
Monitoring at home:
What I want them to call about (specific signs):
What I want them to take to ER (specific signs):

Recheck date:

Reading level grade 6-8. Apply species-specific cautions. End with "DVM verify" block.
```

> **Patient: [name placeholder]** | **Species: dog** | **Breed: mixed** | **Weight: 18 kg / 39.7 lb** | **Date: [placeholder]**
>
> **What we did today**

>
> **What we found / diagnosis**
> Routine procedure. Reproductive tract removed cleanly. No surprises.
>
> **Medications going home**
>

> - Dose: [PLACEHOLDER — DVM verify against Plumb's for 18 kg dog]
> - Give: every 12 hours
> - With food (a small treat or meal is fine)
> - Duration: 4 days
> - Watch for: vomiting, not wanting to eat, dark or tarry stools. Call us if any of those happen and stop the medication until you talk to us.
> - Missed dose: give it when you remember unless it's almost time for the next one — then skip it. Never double up.
>
> **Trazodone** (a calming medication to help her rest)
> - Dose: [PLACEHOLDER — DVM verify against Plumb's for 18 kg dog]
> - Give: every 8-12 hours as needed for restlessness
> - Can give with or without food
> - Duration: as needed for 5-7 days
> - Watch for: excessive sedation. If she's hard to wake or unsteady, skip the next dose and call us.
>
> **Care at home over the next 10-14 days**
> - **Activity**: leashed bathroom breaks only. No running, jumping, stairs, or playing with other dogs for 10-14 days. Crate or small room when you can't watch her.
> - **Feeding**: small meal tonight if she wants it. Back to normal tomorrow. Drinking water is normal.
> - **E-collar**: keep on at all times until the incision is healed. She will hate it for one day and then forget about it.
> - **Incision**: check it once a day. A small amount of bruising or pink-tinged drainage in the first 48 hours is OK. No bathing or swimming until the incision is fully healed.
> - **Monitoring**: she should be more like herself each day. Eating, drinking, peeing, pooping normally by tomorrow.
>
> **Call us if you see:**
> - The incision is open, oozing yellow or green discharge, or smells bad
> - Significant swelling at the incision
> - She vomits more than once, or won't eat for more than 24 hours
> - She seems painful despite the medication (panting, restless, hiding)
> - Anything that doesn't feel right
>
> **Take her to ER if you see:**
> - Pale gums or extreme weakness
> - Difficulty breathing
> - Distended belly, vomiting white foam, retching without producing anything
> - Collapse

## 2. Client education on a specific condition

### Prompt

```
Build a one-page client education handout for [condition].

Patient (no PHI for owner, include patient):
- Species, weight (kg AND lb), age, sex/neuter
- Breed if relevant
- Other relevant context

Client context: emotional state estimate, financial constraint mentioned y/n, language preference, prior experience with this condition.

Specific client questions to address: [list — and remember, these get answered in GENERAL terms for the condition, not as patient-specific clinical decisions]

Structure: what it is (plain, species-relevant), what we know about why (honest), what we'll do for [pet name placeholder], what to expect (qualified), what you can do at home, honest cost discussion (no shame), questions for next visit.

Reading level grade 6-8. 400-700 words. Apply species-specific cautions. End with "DVM verify" block.
```

### Worked example (excerpt, feline CKD stage 2, owner on tight budget, owner asked about Tylenol)

> **Chronic Kidney Disease in Cats**
> For: [pet name placeholder] | Species: cat | 4.2 kg / 9.2 lb | 11 years old
>
> **What it is**
> Chronic kidney disease (CKD) means [pet name]'s kidneys are working at a reduced level and that this isn't going to fully reverse. Her stage 2 result means her kidney function is mildly reduced — caught early. Cats can do well with stage 2 CKD for years with the right care.
>
> **What we know about why**
> Most older cats develop some degree of CKD. We don't always know the specific cause. It's a wear-and-tear condition more than a "something is wrong" condition. We grade it 1 to 4. We caught hers at stage 2, which is the best position to start managing it.
>
> **What we'll do for [pet name]**
> - **Diet change:** prescription kidney diet, fed consistently. This is the single most impactful thing we do for CKD cats.
> - **Hydration:** encouraging water intake. We can show you home fluid administration if it becomes appropriate later — for now, water fountains and wet food count.
> - **Monitoring:** recheck bloodwork and urine in 3-6 months to track her trend.
> - **Medications:** none right now. We add them if her potassium, phosphorus, or blood pressure tells us to.
>
> **About the Tylenol question — important**
> Acetaminophen (Tylenol) is **never safe for cats**, even in small amounts. Cats lack the enzyme that processes acetaminophen safely; even one regular tablet can cause severe liver damage and damage to her red blood cells. The same is true for ibuprofen and most other human pain medications. Aspirin is also limited in cats and only ever given under direct veterinary direction — not from the medicine cabinet.
>
> If [pet name] seems uncomfortable, **call us before giving anything**. There are cat-safe options we can prescribe, but they're determined by us based on her current kidney function and weight.
>
> **What to expect**
> Most stage 2 CKD cats live well for one to several years and often longer. We expect her to feel like herself, eat well, and have normal energy. We watch for: increased drinking and urination, weight loss, decreased appetite, vomiting. Any of those — call us.
>
> **What you can do at home**
> 1. Feed the kidney diet consistently. No human food on the side.
> 2. Wet food whenever she'll eat it (it adds water).
> 3. Multiple water bowls in different rooms. A fountain often helps cats drink more.
> 4. Weigh her once a month if you can — same scale, same time of day. A consistent drop is the early signal.
> 5. Quiet, low-stress environment.
>
> **Honest cost discussion**
> The plan above is the foundation. Here's the range:
> - **Foundation tier (recommended):** prescription diet + recheck bloodwork/urine every 3-6 months. ~$[placeholder] per recheck visit, ~$[placeholder] per month for food.
> - **Pragmatic tier:** prescription diet + annual recheck instead of every 3-6 months. We catch changes later but cost is lower.
> - **Comfort tier:** standard wet food (not prescription) + recheck only if she shows symptoms. We accept we'll catch progression later.
> There's no wrong choice. We'll work with whichever tier fits your situation.
>
> **Questions to ask at her next visit**
> 1. Is her trend on bloodwork stable, getting better, or getting worse?
> 2. Is it time to add any medication?
> 3. Is her blood pressure stable?
> 4. What signs at home should make me call sooner than the next visit?
> 5. Is there a cat-safe pain option we should keep on hand?
>
> ---
> **DVM verify before client delivery:**
> - IRIS stage 2 management aligned with current AAFP / IRIS guidelines
> - Species caution on acetaminophen applied (correct)
> - NSAID guidance for cats applied (no OTC NSAIDs, prescription cat-specific only under DVM direction)
> - Cost placeholders filled with your clinic's numbers
> - Prognostic language qualified ("often live well for years"), not certain
> - Owner literacy and language match handout

## 3. Euthanasia conversation guide

### Prompt

```
Build a euthanasia conversation guide for [species] [age] with [primary diagnosis or quality-of-life concern].

Bond strength estimate: [strong / very strong / new client]
Family situation: [single owner / family with children / etc.]
Client emotional state: [accepting / not yet there / oscillating]
Prior experience with euthanasia: [yes / no / unknown]
Anticipated appointment timing: [today / this week / not scheduled yet]

Jurisdiction: [state or province — affects controlled-drug and body-care rules]

Structure: before the conversation, opening, quality of life framework, what the procedure involves, client choices (presence, body care, keepsakes), the hour itself, afterward, team debrief.

This is a GUIDE for the conversation, not a script the team reads. End with "DVM verify" block.
```

### Worked example (excerpt, 13-year-old golden retriever with osteosarcoma, family with two teenagers, strong bond, has not yet been through euthanasia)

> **Euthanasia conversation guide — [pet name placeholder]**
> Patient: 13-year-old male neutered golden retriever, 32 kg, osteosarcoma right forelimb, declining mobility, owner family with two teenagers, strong bond, no prior euthanasia experience.
>
> **Before the conversation**
> - Block a 20-minute appointment in a private room (not an exam room if possible)
> - Have the consent form, body care form, and payment handled before the appointment when possible
> - Have a soft bed or blanket on the floor; the dog should be on something other than a metal table for this size of dog
> - Make sure the team knows not to interrupt
> - Confirm IV catheter approach is set up in advance
> - If the family is bringing the teenagers, prepare a quiet space for them and confirm with parents that the kids want to be present
>
> **Opening — if the family arrives ready**
> "Thank you for bringing him in. I know this is one of the hardest days. Take whatever time you need with him here. When you're ready, I'll walk through exactly what we're going to do, and you can ask me anything."
>
> **Opening — if the family is wavering**
> "I want to check in with you before we move forward. Tell me what you're seeing at home. ... Based on what you're describing and what we know about his cancer, we're at a point where we can give him a peaceful day rather than wait for a bad one. But this is your decision and there's no wrong call. Would you like a few minutes alone with him?"
>
> **Quality of life framework — if it helps**
> Pain. Appetite. Mobility. Joy. Hygiene. More good days than bad. If you've been counting bad days lately and they're outnumbering the good — that's the signal.
>
> **What the procedure involves**
> "Here's what's going to happen. I'll give him a sedative first that will make him sleepy and very relaxed — like the deepest nap. Then, when he's fully asleep, I'll give a second injection that stops his heart gently. He won't feel either one. From the moment he relaxes to the moment his heart stops, it's usually a couple of minutes. Sometimes the body takes a breath or twitches a little after — that's a reflex, not awareness. He'll be gone before that."
>
> **Choices the family makes**
> - Presence: "You can be with him the whole time, just for the sedation, or wait outside — any of those is okay. Some people want to be there, some don't. I'll be with him either way."
> - The teenagers: "Some kids want to be there. Some don't. It's their call too. Whatever they choose is fine. If they want to step out partway through, that's okay."
> - Body care: home burial where permitted [check jurisdiction], communal cremation (no return), individual cremation with ashes returned (often takes ~2 weeks), private cremation with viewing for some clinics.
> - Keepsakes: paw print (clay or ink), fur clipping, photo. Offer these without pushing.
>
> **The hour itself — phrases**
> Use: "Take your time." "You can talk to him." "There's no rush." "He's so calm."
> Avoid: "He's in a better place." "I know how you feel." "It was the right thing" (let them say that). "He's just sleeping" (he's not).
>
> Handling a client who changes their mind: "We can stop. There's no problem with stopping. Tell me what you're thinking." Sometimes the answer is "I just need 10 more minutes." Sometimes it's "I want to bring him home and try one more weekend." Both are valid responses; the team accommodates without judgment.
>
> **Afterward**
> - Give the family time alone with the body if they want it
> - Walk them out a back exit if available — they shouldn't have to pass other clients in the lobby
> - Sympathy card mailed within a week
> - Paw print delivered with the cremains or by mail
> - Phone callback at 7-10 days — RVT or front desk can do this; brief, warm, "we're thinking of you"
> - Grief resources offered (pet loss support lines, ASPCA pet loss hotline, local resources)
>
> **For the team**
> Brief debrief if needed, especially for newer team members or particularly hard cases. Vet med has high compassion fatigue rates — this matters.
>
> ---
> **DVM verify before appointment:**
> - Euthanasia agent doses verified against Plumb's for 32 kg dog
> - Controlled-drug recordkeeping current
> - Body care options accurate to your area (jurisdiction-specific)
> - Crematorium logistics confirmed
> - Sympathy card and paw print process in place

## 4. Financial-options conversation script

### Prompt

```
Build a tiered financial-options conversation script.

Patient: species, weight (kg + lb), age, primary concern
Recommended workup or treatment (the gold standard):
Estimated cost of the gold standard:
Pragmatic alternative (stepwise / limited workup):
Estimated cost of pragmatic option:
Comfort/supportive-only alternative:
Estimated cost of comfort:
Is euthanasia a humane option in this case? [yes / no / depends on findings]

Client context: financial constraint level, emotional state, prior experience.

Structure: frame ("we recommend X, options if cost is a concern, no wrong choice"), Tier 1 gold standard with cost + what it tells/treats, Tier 2 pragmatic with cost + tradeoffs, Tier 3 comfort only with cost + what we accept, Tier 4 euthanasia if applicable, resources (CareCredit, Scratchpay, charity programs, low-cost clinics), what we'll do today regardless.

No cost shaming. End with "DVM verify" block.
```

(Worked example follows the same matter-of-fact, options-without-shame pattern. The cost numbers are filled in by the user; the structure ensures every conversation hits the same beats: respect, options, resources, and the floor of care provided today regardless of choice.)
