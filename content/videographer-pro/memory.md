# Memory — Videographer / Filmmaker Pack

## Domain context

A working videographer or filmmaker runs a small business that looks more like a creative agency than a film studio. They might shoot a wedding Saturday, a corporate testimonial Tuesday, and a brand spot Friday — all with the same FX6 and a kit they've assembled over five years. They are usually a solo operator or part of a 2-4 person production company. They are the producer, the shooter, the editor, the colorist, the audio mixer, and the one who sends the invoice. The work pays $500/day on the bottom end (real estate, social content) to $15,000-50,000 per project on the upper end (brand films, doc work, music videos with name talent).

The rhythm of the work is messy. Pre-production is treatments, shot lists, location scouts, gear prep, and call sheets. Production is 10-14 hour shoot days, often weekends. Post is the longest phase — first cut, client review, second cut, color, sound, deliverables, exports for social. Most of the client-facing writing happens between these phases or at 11 PM after a wrap. Pitch decks are written under deadline pressure. Treatments get finished the night before they're due. Recap emails after a shoot day are often the difference between a happy client and a chargeback. The AI is most useful in those exact moments — turning a verbal idea or a creative brief into a document that reads like a working filmmaker wrote it.

Success looks like: the pitch lands, the treatment gets the green light, the shoot goes smoothly, the client signs off in two rounds, the final delivery is on time, the invoice gets paid, and the client refers the next one. Failure looks like: scope creep that wasn't documented, a fourth round of revisions you didn't budget for, a client who expected social cuts you didn't quote, a music track they assumed was cleared, and a 60-day collection on a project that's already wrapped.

## Vocabulary the AI should know

- **DP**: director of photography — the person running camera and lighting decisions
- **AC / 1st AC**: assistant camera / first AC — pulls focus, manages lenses, media handler on small crews
- **B-cam / B-roll**: secondary camera angle / supplemental footage that isn't the main interview or scene
- **NLE**: non-linear editor — DaVinci Resolve, Premiere Pro, Final Cut Pro X, Avid Media Composer
- **ProRes / DNxHR**: high-quality intermediate codecs used for editing and masters. ProRes 422 HQ is the standard mid-tier
- **LUT**: look-up table — the color transform used to apply a look or convert between color spaces
- **DI / color**: digital intermediate / color grade — the colorist's pass. Done in Resolve typically
- **Lock / picture lock**: the cut is finalized, no more structural changes. Triggers color and sound to finish
- **Stems**: separated audio tracks (dialogue, music, effects) delivered alongside or instead of a final mix
- **DCP**: Digital Cinema Package — theatrical delivery format. Rarely needed for commercial work
- **9:16 / 1:1 / 16:9**: aspect ratios. Vertical (TikTok, IG Reels, Shorts) / square (IG feed) / horizontal (YouTube, broadcast)
- **Master**: the highest-quality final file delivered. Usually ProRes 422 HQ or higher
- **Frame.io / Vimeo Review**: cloud review platforms where clients leave timecoded comments
- **Call sheet**: production document for shoot day — call times, locations, crew, gear, contacts
- **Day rate / project rate**: billing by the day vs. flat for the whole project. Brand work tends to flat-rate, commercial day work hourly
- **Sync license / master license / mech license**: music licensing terms. Sync is rights to use the recording with picture; master is the recording itself; mech is the composition. Most stock music libraries (Musicbed, Artlist, Soundstripe, Marmoset) bundle these
- **Work-for-hire**: contract clause where the client owns the work product outright. Common in commercial, rare in editorial / doc
- **Color space**: Rec. 709 (HD), Rec. 2020 (UHD), DCI-P3 (theatrical). The AI shouldn't guess — ask the client what their delivery needs are
- **Frame rates**: 23.976 (cinematic), 24 (true cinema), 25 (PAL / European broadcast), 29.97 (broadcast), 30 (web), 50/60 (slow-mo or sports)
- **Camera names worth knowing**: Sony FX3, FX6, FX9, A7S III, Burano. Canon C70, C300 Mark III, R5C. RED Komodo, V-Raptor. Blackmagic Pocket 6K, URSA. ARRI Alexa Mini, 35. DJI Ronin (gimbal), Inspire (drone)

## Common workflows

- **Pitch a brand spot or commercial:** client RFP comes in or a referral lead reaches out. Discovery call (30-45 min) to understand the business, the audience, the budget, and the existing creative they like. Pitch deck (problem → approach → references → price → next step). If they go for it, contract + 50% deposit, then pre-production.
- **Pitch a wedding film:** couple inquires, often through Instagram or a referral. Discovery call (or video call) about the day, their vibe, what they don't want (cheesy effects, slow-mo of the kiss, etc.). Quote with three tiers usually (highlight only, highlight + ceremony, full coverage). Contract + 50% deposit on signing, balance due 2 weeks before shoot.
- **Write a treatment:** for a brand or commercial gig, after the pitch lands. Concept, visual refs, narrative arc, look (camera/lens/color), deliverables, timeline. PDF, 2-4 pages. Sent to client for approval before shoot. Often goes through one round of notes from the brand team.
- **Shoot day:** call sheet sent 48-24 hours before. 10-14 hour day. Wrap with a verbal recap to the client onsite if possible. Recap email within 24 hours.
- **Edit and review cycle:** first cut delivered to Frame.io or Vimeo Review. Client leaves timecoded comments. Editor addresses in second cut. Two rounds standard, third round at additional cost. Picture lock → color → sound → deliverables.
- **Deliverables and final invoice:** master file delivered (often via Frame.io, WeTransfer, or Google Drive). Social cuts delivered with each cut. Final invoice goes out at delivery; net 14 or net 30 terms.
- **Schedule slip or technical issue:** something happens — a card corruption, a sick crew member, a weather day. Honest email to client immediately, options outlined, decision requested.

## What to avoid / common mistakes

- **"Cinematic storytelling," "we'll capture your story," "passion for the craft."** Every videographer's website says this. Cut.
- **Treatments without deliverables.** "Final video, color graded, sound mixed" is not enough. Specify resolution, codec, frame rate, aspect, social cut count and aspects, raw files included or not.
- **Promising final cut dates without buffer.** A 4-week post timeline becomes 5-6 weeks more often than not. Always add buffer to the date you commit, especially around color and sound.
- **Vague edit-review notes accepted without pushback.** "Make it pop" / "more energy" / "the music isn't right." Coach the client to use timecodes and the COPY/PACE/COLOR/AUDIO/STRUCTURE format. Saves rounds.
- **Quoting one master when the client expects 14 social cuts.** Spec the deliverables in the treatment and the contract.
- **Claiming a music track is cleared when the client supplied it without a license.** Push back. "Did you license this through [platform]? I'll need the license document for my files."
- **Work-for-hire clauses signed without reading them.** A brand client's standard contract may take rights you didn't intend to give. Flag any rights language.
- **Using "phases" and "deliverables" in client emails when "first cut," "second cut," "color," "final" works better.** Producers use phase language; clients respond to milestone language.

## Tone / register

A working filmmaker writing on a phone between shoots sounds confident, specific, and a little tired. They mention the camera, the lens, the location, the look. They use sentence fragments. They acknowledge what's hard about a shoot without complaining about it. They write treatments that other filmmakers respect because the references are real and the language isn't recycled from a deck template. They write recap emails that say "got it" or "we'll flag this in the cut, here's how we'll handle it" — not "we performed the production phase successfully." They don't use exclamation points except in texts. They never write "we will leverage cinematic storytelling techniques." That's the voice.
