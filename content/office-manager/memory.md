# Memory — Office Manager Pack

## Domain context

An office manager is the person who runs the physical room and the operational glue that holds a 30-200 person company together. At smaller end (30-75), they're usually solo — one human owning facilities, vendors, IT triage, exec support, event logistics, swag, the kitchen, parking, mail, badges, the snack budget, and "the culture stuff." They report to the COO, head of People, CFO, or directly to the founder, depending on how the org grew. At the larger end (75-200), there might be an office coordinator or admin under them, but the role doesn't get much more strategic — it gets more vendors, more meetings, more requests. The office manager is in the office every day the office is open. The job is largely physical presence plus a thousand small decisions per week.

The rhythm is interrupt-driven. The proactive layer: a monthly building walkthrough, quarterly vendor reviews, an annual lease check-in, seasonal supply orders (winter mats, summer fans), event calendar (holiday party, summer thing, two or three offsites), the rolling birthday and work-anniversary list. The reactive layer is everything else: someone locked themselves out of a conference room, the espresso machine is making a noise, the landlord wants to do HVAC work next Saturday, the founder wants a lunch-and-learn organized by Thursday with a topic he hasn't decided yet, IT is escalating a printer issue back to facilities because nobody knows whose problem it is, three people are sick and the catering for tomorrow's all-hands needs to be cut down. Most comms are written in 5-10 minute windows between interruptions, on a laptop in the kitchen, while someone is asking where the extra HDMI cables are.

Success looks like: the office runs and people stop noticing the office running, vendors show up when scheduled, events happen under budget and feel like someone thought about them, the new-hire seat is ready on day one, the visitor knows where to go, the snow-day comm goes out by 6 AM, the espresso machine works on Monday morning, the holiday party doesn't blow the budget, and the founder doesn't get asked operational questions because the answer is already on the wiki. Failure looks like: stuff is broken and nobody knows who owns fixing it, an event goes over budget without anyone noticing until finance flags it three weeks later, the comms about a building closure goes out the morning of, vendors stop returning calls because invoices are 90 days late, the team starts working around the office instead of through it.

## Vocabulary the AI should know

- **COI**: Certificate of Insurance. Vendors and contractors send one before they're allowed on site. Standard ask.
- **NDA**: Non-Disclosure Agreement. Sometimes needed for vendors handling sensitive on-site work.
- **SOW**: Statement of Work. Vendor-side document defining what they're delivering, by when, for how much.
- **MSA**: Master Service Agreement. The umbrella contract you sign once; SOWs hang off it.
- **Net 30 / Net 60**: Vendor payment terms. Net 30 = invoice due 30 days after issue.
- **Par level**: the inventory threshold you keep on hand for a recurring supply item (e.g., 6 bags of coffee).
- **Reorder point**: the threshold below which you place a new order. Usually par level minus lead time of consumption.
- **FF&E**: Furniture, Fixtures, and Equipment. Office buildout / move language.
- **Tenant improvement (TI) allowance**: landlord contribution to a fit-out, negotiated in the lease.
- **CAM charges**: Common Area Maintenance fees. The portion of your rent that covers shared building costs.
- **Triple-net (NNN) lease**: a lease where tenant pays base rent plus taxes, insurance, and CAM.
- **HVAC**: Heating, Ventilation, Air Conditioning. The system you'll be on a first-name basis with the technician for.
- **MSP**: Managed Service Provider. The outsourced IT vendor — third-party help desk and on-site support.
- **Badge / fob / kastle / kisi / verkada**: building access control systems. Common brand names you'll deal with.
- **EAP**: Employee Assistance Program. Counseling and support benefit. Sometimes office managers handle the promo signage and intro comms.
- **Capex / opex**: capital expenditure vs. operating expense. Different approval paths usually.
- **Cintas / Aramark / Unifirst**: floor mats, towel service, uniform rentals. The "we change the mats Tuesday" vendors.
- **Iron Mountain / Shred-it**: document storage and secure shredding.
- **WeWork / Industrious / Regus / Knotel**: coworking and flex office operators. You may negotiate with them as landlords or as overflow space.
- **WFH stipend / hybrid model / RTO mandate**: language around work arrangements that office managers help operationalize.
- **Stipulated headcount**: the maximum number of people allowed in a space per fire code. Posted near elevators.
- **Loading dock / freight elevator booking**: the part of move-in / move-out logistics that always needs to be reserved 1-2 weeks ahead.

## Common workflows

- **New vendor onboarding:** identify need → request 2-3 quotes → COI requested → reference check → SOW signed → first delivery / appointment → invoice loop set up with finance.
- **Recurring supply reorder:** par level set → consumption tracked (usually informally) → reorder at threshold → delivery → restock → next cycle. Coffee, snacks, paper goods, cleaning supplies, toner, batteries.
- **Small event planning (lunch-and-learn, team offsite, holiday party):** brief from leadership → budget confirmed → 2-3 venue / catering options → invite sent → RSVPs collected → dietary captured → day-of run-of-show → vendor thank-you and invoice routing.
- **Facilities ticket triage:** issue surfaced (by Slack, in person, walk-by) → diagnose if you can → assign (you, landlord, IT MSP, specific vendor) → ticket logged → follow-up until closed → confirm with reporter.
- **Building closure / disruption comms:** trigger (snow, HVAC work, fire drill, building emergency) → confirm details with landlord → draft message → leadership sign-off if material → send via Slack + email + door sign → follow-up when service restored.
- **New hire seat setup:** offer signed → laptop ordered with IT → desk / chair / monitor / keyboard / mouse assigned → badge created → welcome kit prepped → day-one tour scheduled → 30-day check-in to see what's missing.
- **Visitor handling:** visitor expected → notify reception or door if applicable → NDA if needed → escort policy confirmed → cleanup / reset after.

## What to avoid / common mistakes

- **"Hi team!!" with multiple exclamation points.** Reads as performative. Real office comms are warmer when they're calmer.
- **"Effective immediately" for things that aren't policy.** A kitchen reminder is not policy. Save the firm voice for things that warrant it.
- **Vendor negotiation that hides budget.** You're not procurement — pretending to be slows everything down. State a range or say "flexible if scope fits."
- **Event invites that don't say whether attendance is expected.** "Optional but really hoping you can make it" is fine. Hiding it isn't.
- **Treating the founder's "let's do something for the team" as a fully specified ask.** Always pull out: budget, expected outcome, who's invited, optional or not, date constraints.
- **Filing facilities tickets without saying what you already tried.** Sends you to the back of the queue.
- **Building a swag order without sizing data and dietary/allergen info collected first.** You will order 50 mediums and have nothing for the L's and XL's.
- **Letting a vendor's verbal "we can do it next week" go unconfirmed in writing.** It will become "actually six weeks."

## Tone / register

A working office manager writes short, warm, and specific. They lead with the thing the reader needs to know. They use real names — the vendor, the team, the building engineer — not "a third party will be on-site." They acknowledge inconvenience without apologizing for it ("HVAC work Saturday means no building access 8 AM - 2 PM — sorry for the timing, this is the only window the landlord had"). They sign off plainly ("Thanks, Jamie"). They never say "as a friendly reminder" because every reminder is friendly by default. They don't write to be applauded — they write to be understood and then get back to the next thing. The voice should land slightly under-thanked but never bitter. The role is glue; glue works best when nobody's noticing it.
