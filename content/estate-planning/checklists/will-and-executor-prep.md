# Will Conversations and Executor Prep

Four working documents in one file. Copy what you need.

---

## 1. Pre-conversation prep for a family meeting

Use this BEFORE you sit down with a parent, partner, sibling, or adult child to talk about wills, end-of-life wishes, or executor roles.

### Step 1: What's the goal of this conversation?

Pick one. Don't try to do all three in one meeting.

- [ ] Find out what the other person has in place (their will, POAs, etc.) and where it lives
- [ ] Share what YOU have in place (informing them)
- [ ] Make a specific decision (who's the executor, what happens to a specific asset, who's named guardian for the kids)

### Step 2: Who's in the room?

- [ ] Just the two of you
- [ ] You and your partner
- [ ] Multiple siblings (different dynamic — be ready for it)
- [ ] Anyone NOT in the room who matters? (Estranged sibling, second marriage's kids, the executor candidate)

### Step 3: Anticipate the reaction

Most reactions fall into one of these:
- "I don't want to think about this." → Acknowledge, then offer to start with the easy parts (where the documents live, not what's in them)
- "Why are you asking?" → Anchor on yourself ("we just did this for ourselves, made me realize")
- "It's all taken care of." → Ask one specific follow-up ("who's the executor?" or "where's the will?")
- Avoidance / changing the subject → Don't push. Schedule a follow-up.
- Tears / strong emotion → Sit with it. The conversation can wait.

### Step 4: Pick an opener

Adapt one of these:
- "We just updated our will and named an executor. Made me realize I have no idea what you have in place. Would you be willing to walk me through it sometime?"
- "I've been reading about the executor role. If something happens to you, am I the one who's supposed to handle it? Because I'd want to know how, and I don't."
- "Could I help you organize where everything is? It's a project I can take on."
- (Parent to adult kids) "I want you to know where things are so you're not figuring it out blind. Can I walk you through it?"

### Step 5: What to bring (or not)

- [ ] If you're asking, bring NOTHING formal. This is a conversation, not a meeting.
- [ ] If you're sharing your own plan, bring the doc or a one-page summary.
- [ ] Don't bring forms for them to sign. That makes it feel transactional.

### Step 6: How to end

- One concrete next step: "Can I send you a link to a fee-only CFP / estate attorney?" / "Will you show me where the documents are next time I'm over?" / "Let's talk again in a month."
- A line that closes it warmly: "Thanks for talking about this. I know it's not the easiest thing."
- Don't relitigate the conversation. Let it sit.

## 2. "What I'd want" worksheet

For yourself. Fill it in over time — weeks, not minutes. Then bring it to your estate attorney meeting.

### Section A: Medical

If I'm in a catastrophic medical situation and can't speak for myself:

- I want the person making medical decisions for me to be: ____________________
- Backup: ____________________
- What this person should know about my values: ____________________

In a permanent vegetative state, my wishes are:
- [ ] Continue all interventions
- [ ] Continue some interventions but no extraordinary measures
- [ ] No life-prolonging interventions; comfort care only
- [ ] Other: ____________________

If I'm terminally ill with no realistic prospect of recovery:
- [ ] All available treatments
- [ ] Standard care only, no experimental interventions
- [ ] Palliative / hospice care only
- [ ] Medical assistance in dying if eligible and I choose it (MAID in Canada / state laws vary in US)

## 3. Executor first-30-days checklist

Customize for your jurisdiction and the deceased's situation. Bring this to your meeting with the estate attorney.

### Week 1: preserve and notify

- [ ] Locate the original will (with the deceased's papers, with their lawyer, in a safe deposit box, filed with the court in some jurisdictions)
- [ ] Confirm you're named as executor / personal representative / estate trustee
- [ ] Obtain certified copies of the death certificate from the funeral director (get at least 10 — banks, government agencies, and insurers each want their own)
- [ ] Secure the residence: lock up, manage incoming mail, protect valuables, check on pets
- [ ] Notify the deceased's lawyer (or retain an estate lawyer if there isn't one)
- [ ] Notify close family of the death (you may already have done this)
- [ ] Do NOT pay any debts yet
- [ ] Do NOT distribute any assets yet
- [ ] Do NOT promise anyone anything specific yet ("you're getting X")

### Week 2: gather information

- [ ] Begin an asset inventory:
  - [ ] Bank accounts (chequing, savings, GICs / CDs)
  - [ ] Investment accounts (registered: RRSP / RRIF / TFSA / FHSA in Canada; 401k / IRA / Roth in US — non-registered / taxable accounts)
  - [ ] Real estate (primary residence, secondary properties)
  - [ ] Vehicles
  - [ ] Insurance policies (life, home, auto, umbrella)
  - [ ] Business interests
  - [ ] Personal property of value (jewellery, art, collections)
  - [ ] Digital assets (cryptocurrency, online accounts)
- [ ] Itemize debts:
  - [ ] Mortgage
  - [ ] Credit cards
  - [ ] Lines of credit
  - [ ] Personal loans
  - [ ] Outstanding tax owing
  - [ ] Final-illness medical bills
- [ ] Identify beneficiary-designated accounts (these pass OUTSIDE the will — go directly to the named beneficiary):
  - [ ] Life insurance
  - [ ] RRSP / RRIF / TFSA (if a beneficiary is named)
  - [ ] 401(k) / IRA
  - [ ] Pensions
- [ ] Notify employer (if applicable) — final pay, benefits, pension
- [ ] Notify government:
  - [ ] Canada: Service Canada (CPP, OAS, GIS), provincial vital statistics, CRA
  - [ ] US: Social Security Administration, IRS, state agencies
- [ ] Notify creditors of the death; ask for current balances

### Week 3: apply and open

- [ ] Begin probate application if required for this estate (your lawyer drives this — process varies materially by province / state)
- [ ] Open an estate bank account (executor signs in their executor capacity)
- [ ] Cancel:
  - [ ] Subscriptions (streaming, software, magazines, gym, clubs)
  - [ ] Credit cards (where appropriate — keep at least one open until the estate is settled if it's tied to recurring charges)
  - [ ] Utilities if the home is being sold (or transfer to estate)
  - [ ] Phone / internet if no longer needed
- [ ] Forward mail to your address (or PO Box)
- [ ] Retain a tax accountant if the estate is complex (foreign assets, business interests, multiple properties, deemed disposition of significant non-registered assets)

### Week 4: plan and communicate

- [ ] Map out the next 6-12 months:
  - [ ] Probate timeline (varies by jurisdiction)
  - [ ] Asset gathering and consolidation
  - [ ] Sale of real estate if applicable
  - [ ] Final tax return (terminal return — due dates vary)
  - [ ] Trust returns if applicable
  - [ ] Distribution timing
  - [ ] Holdback for unknown liabilities (you want a buffer before final distribution)
- [ ] Communicate the plan to beneficiaries:
  - [ ] General timeline, not specific dollar amounts yet
  - [ ] Set expectations: 12-18 months is normal for most estates
  - [ ] Don't promise specific amounts until the inventory is complete and probate is granted
- [ ] Budget your own time and energy:
  - [ ] Executor work is typically 100-300 hours over 12-18 months
  - [ ] You're entitled to executor compensation in most jurisdictions (typically a % of the estate; varies)
  - [ ] You can resign if needed (usually requires court process — talk to the lawyer)

### Throughout

- [ ] Document everything you do and every dollar that moves
- [ ] Keep the estate bank account separate from your personal money — always
- [ ] Get professional advice on anything that feels complex
- [ ] Take breaks. Grief and admin work are exhausting in different ways.

Paste any document into a conversation with the system prompt loaded and say: "Adapt this for my situation. Country: ____, province/state: ____. Here's the context: ____." The AI will customize without re-explaining, and will redirect you to your estate attorney for anything that crosses into legal-document territory.
