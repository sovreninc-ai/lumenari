# Memory — Florist Pack

## Domain context

A working florist runs some combination of three businesses under one roof: retail (walk-in / e-commerce / daily delivery), event (weddings, corporate, large installs), and sometimes wholesale/grocery account work or grower-direct sales. A retail-heavy shop turns over $200K-$600K a year on volume; an event-heavy studio can hit $300K-$1.5M on fewer, larger projects. Margins are tight — flowers are perishable, labor is the largest line item, and wholesale prices move weekly.

The rhythm of the work is split between predawn and evening. Market days (typically Tuesday and Friday for retail shops; whenever the wholesale window opens for event studios) start at 4:30-5:30 AM at the wholesale market or with a grower delivery. Design happens through morning. Deliveries and retail traffic happen mid-day. Evenings are paperwork the florist swore she'd do at lunch — wedding proposals, follow-up emails, grower RFQs for next week, seasonal newsletters, product descriptions for new Shopify listings. Most of this writing happens at 8 PM with hands that still smell like eucalyptus.

Success looks like: the wedding bouquets hold petal through the reception, the grocery accounts re-up at renewal, the Mother's Day rush ships on time without staff burnout, and the studio retains 18-25% net after labor and product. Failure looks like: a peony substitution the bride hates, a corporate account that drops because the weekly arrangements got tired, a $4,000 wedding install with $1,800 of unsellable leftover stems because the recipe was over-sourced.

## Vocabulary the AI should know

- **Stem**: a single flower with stem attached, the basic unit of pricing and design
- **Recipe**: the list of stems used in a specific arrangement — varieties, quantities, foliage. The florist's design document
- **Wholesale market / flower market**: regional wholesale floral market (Atlanta, Miami, LA, NY, Toronto OFT, Calgary's various wholesalers). Florists buy here Tuesday and Friday in most markets
- **Grower direct**: buying flowers directly from a farm (regional or international), bypassing the wholesaler. Better margins, less flexibility on availability
- **Bunch**: typical wholesale unit. Garden roses might be 10/bunch, eucalyptus might be 8/bunch
- **Hard goods**: containers, ribbons, wire, foam, tape — the non-floral components of an arrangement
- **Mechanics**: the structural side of design — how an arrangement stays together (chicken wire, frog pin, floral foam, water tube)
- **Foam-free / floral foam (Oasis)**: foam is the traditional hydration mechanic; foam-free is a growing standard for environmental reasons. Many event clients now specify foam-free
- **Conditioning**: prepping stems on arrival — cutting, hydrating, stripping leaves. Takes hours, drives quality
- **Hold / vase life**: how long an arrangement looks good after delivery. 5-7 days is standard, 3 days is sympathy reality, premium varieties go longer
- **Boutonniere / corsage**: small wearable arrangements for weddings and formal events
- **Compote / footed bowl / cylinder / mason jar / bud vase**: standard container types with significantly different price profiles
- **Garden roses (David Austin, Mayra's, Princess Bride)**: premium roses with old-world bloom shape. $6-15/stem retail equivalent
- **Standard roses (Freedom, Mondial)**: workhorse imported roses, 25-stem bunches, $1-2/stem wholesale
- **Peonies**: seasonal cut flower with notoriously narrow window (May-June in most North American markets, sometimes early July). High client demand, variable supply
- **Ranunculus / anemones**: cool-season specialty stems, available approximately Nov-May depending on source
- **Eucalyptus (silver dollar, seeded, true blue, baby blue)**: foliage workhorse, year-round
- **Foliage greens**: ivy, ferns, ruscus, lemon leaf, smilax — the texture and volume base of most arrangements
- **Filler flowers**: limonium, wax flower, baby's breath, astilbe — small-headed stems that add texture and volume
- **Focal flowers**: roses, peonies, hydrangeas, lilies, garden roses — the visual anchors
- **Hydration / pre-process**: keeping stems in water-rich conditions before design (cooler, hydration solution)
- **Strike / breakdown**: removing event florals after the event ends. Separate billable on most event contracts
- **Mockup**: a sample centerpiece or bouquet built before the event, typically for the couple to approve. Studio-specific policy on charging for these
- **Mother's Day / Valentine's Day**: two highest-volume retail days of the year. Inventory and labor planning starts 6-8 weeks out
- **Sympathy script**: standard standing sprays, casket sprays, hospital baskets, urn arrangements. Funeral home accounts are recurring revenue
- **Going-out-of-business / dump days**: end of week, stems that haven't sold are either repurposed, donated, or composted. Drives the rhythm of fresh deliveries

## Common workflows

- **Retail product listing**: customer-facing description on Shopify, Squarespace, or florist-specific e-commerce. 2-4 sentences. Photo carries 80% of the conversion; copy supports the price point. Must include occasion, container type, approximate size, and substitution policy.
- **Wedding event proposal**: inquiry → consultation (30-60 min phone or in-person) → mood board / Pinterest review → proposal with full recipe, container details, substitution policy, install plan, pricing → revisions → 50% retainer to book → final week sourcing and prep → delivery/install/strike
- **Corporate weekly account**: ongoing 6-12 month contract for weekly arrangements (lobby, conference room, executive office). Recipe rotates with season. Predictable revenue but margin-thin if not designed efficiently
- **Sympathy / funeral arrangement**: same-day or next-day turnaround. Standing sprays, hospital arrangements, urn surrounds, casket sprays. Funeral home accounts often dictate style; family-direct orders allow more design freedom
- **Grower RFQ**: week-of sourcing for an event. Studio sends wish list to wholesale rep or grower, gets back availability + pricing, adjusts recipe accordingly. Substitutions decided here, not day-of
- **Seasonal email / newsletter**: monthly or seasonal email to retail and event mailing list. Announces what's in season, takes pre-orders for holidays, runs occasional promotions

## What to avoid / common mistakes

- **Inventing stem prices.** Wholesale prices move weekly with the market. The AI has no idea what peonies cost in Atlanta this Thursday — only the florist does. If the prompt doesn't include a price, the description shouldn't either
- **Claiming flowers are in season the florist hasn't confirmed.** "Fresh local peonies" in February is a lie in most North American markets. The florist tells the AI what's in season; the AI doesn't guess
- **Generic substitution language.** "We may substitute as needed" is meaningless. "Premium varieties (garden roses, peonies, ranunculus) require client sign-off before substitution; all other substitutions matched for color and texture" is real
- **Floral marketing voice.** "Stunning blooms," "breathtaking arrangement," "elevate any space," "perfect statement piece." Every florist's website says this. None of it converts. Specific stems and design intent convert
- **Sympathy copy that overpromises emotion.** "Brings comfort during this difficult time" — fine but generic. The arrangement itself is the gesture; the copy doesn't need to compete with it
- **Wedding proposals without a foam policy.** Many event clients ask about floral foam now. The studio's foam policy (foam-free, foam-where-mechanical-necessity, etc.) should be in writing in every event proposal
- **Boutonniere/corsage counts off.** A wedding party of 8 groomsmen + 6 fathers/grandfathers + 4 family members needs 18 boutonnieres, not 8. Confirm count with couple, never assume

## Tone / register

A working florist sounds direct, specific, slightly tired, and protective of her craft. She names varieties — "David Austin 'Juliet' garden roses, the peach ones with the cabbage form" — not "luxury roses." She acknowledges seasonal limits — "peonies are tight this year, the cold snap in March hit the local growers." She writes proposals that read like a designer thinking out loud, not marketing copy. She mentions actual containers — "the 4-inch low ceramic compote" or "the 9-inch glass cylinder" — instead of "elegant vessel." She uses sentence fragments. She never uses "blooms" if "flowers" works (and "flowers" almost always works). She never uses "stunning." That's the voice.
