# Memory — HVAC Tech Pack

## Domain context

An HVAC contractor or service tech runs a business shaped by weather. Spring is tune-up season and the start of AC replacement calls. Summer is the heat-wave gauntlet where the phone never stops and good shops triage emergency calls against scheduled installs. Fall is furnace tune-ups, no-heat first calls, and the gas tech season. Winter is no-heat emergencies, ice-related damage, and the planning period for next year's capital install proposals. Maintenance contract renewals stack up at the boundaries of the seasons. The good shops run on a recurring-revenue maintenance base plus seasonal install peaks; the marginal shops survive on one-off repairs.

The rhythm is brutal in season and slow off it. A heat wave week is 16-hour days, 12 calls deep, all of them upset. The slack weeks are when you write the install proposals you couldn't write in July. That's where the AI earns its keep — turning load calc notes and a tired tech's "they need a new system" assessment into a real proposal with rebate references, financing terms, and a defensible spec selection. Most of the proposal writing happens at 9 PM at the kitchen table or at 6 AM before the first call, and the proposals that win against the cheap quote are the ones that walk the customer through what makes the install different.

Success looks like: customers who choose your $11,800 system over the competitor's $9,500 because they understood what's in the price. Maintenance contracts that renew at 85%+ annually because customers feel the priority service during heat waves. Install crews that don't get callbacks because commissioning was done right. Failure looks like: race-to-the-bottom pricing, callbacks on improper refrigerant charges, customers who got an oversized system and now blame you for high humidity.

## Vocabulary the AI should know

- AHRI: Air-Conditioning, Heating, and Refrigeration Institute — issues certificates for matched system performance ratings
- Manual J: ACCA-standard residential load calculation, the basis for proper sizing
- Manual D: duct design calculation
- Manual S: equipment selection based on the Manual J load
- SEER2 / EER2: cooling efficiency ratings (2023 onward; replaced SEER/EER)
- AFUE: Annual Fuel Utilization Efficiency — furnace efficiency rating
- HSPF / HSPF2: Heating Seasonal Performance Factor — heat pump heating efficiency
- COP: Coefficient of Performance — heat pump efficiency at a specific condition
- BTU/h or MBH: thousand BTU per hour, the unit of heating/cooling capacity (12,000 BTU/h = 1 ton)
- Ton: 12,000 BTU/h of cooling capacity
- Variable speed / inverter / two-stage / single-stage: equipment modulation levels
- ECM motor: electronically commutated motor — modern blower motor with variable speed capability
- Heat pump: equipment that does both heating and cooling using refrigeration cycle
- Cold-climate heat pump (ccASHP): heat pumps rated for performance at low ambient temperatures (-15°F / -25°C and below)
- Dual fuel / hybrid: heat pump paired with gas furnace, switches based on outdoor temperature
- Mini-split / ductless: small wall-mounted air handlers, no ductwork
- Multi-zone: one outdoor unit driving multiple indoor heads
- AC condenser / outdoor unit: the unit outside containing the compressor and condenser coil
- Air handler / fan coil / furnace: the indoor unit
- Evaporator / evap coil / A-coil: the indoor refrigerant coil mounted on top of the furnace or inside the air handler
- TXV / EEV: thermostatic / electronic expansion valve — meters refrigerant flow into the evap
- Refrigerant: R-22 (legacy, phased out), R-410A (current standard), R-454B / R-32 (next-gen, low-GWP refrigerants taking over in 2025+)
- EPA 608: US refrigerant handling certification required for techs
- Static pressure: pressure drop across the duct system, key commissioning metric
- Temperature split / delta-T: temperature difference between return and supply, used to verify charge and airflow
- Superheat / subcool: refrigerant charging measurements
- Combustion analysis: measuring flue gas to verify proper combustion on gas equipment
- CSA B149.1 / B149.2: Canadian gas codes (natural gas / propane)
- TSSA: Technical Standards and Safety Authority (Ontario gas regulator)
- AHJ: Authority Having Jurisdiction
- Carrier, Trane, Lennox, Daikin/Goodman, Mitsubishi, Fujitsu, Bosch, Bryant, Rheem, York: major HVAC manufacturer brands; reputation and warranty terms vary
- Brand reputation shorthand: Trane and Carrier = mainstream premium; Lennox = premium with parts-availability concerns; Daikin/Goodman = good value-tier; Mitsubishi/Fujitsu = mini-split leaders; Bosch = popular for inverters and heat pumps; York = budget premium

## Common workflows

- System replacement quote: customer's system fails or is end-of-life, tech assesses, runs Manual J (or estimates), recommends a configuration with rationale, produces a proposal with AHRI match, spec sheets, rebates, and financing options. Trigger → assess → load calc → AHRI-matched recommendation → proposal → follow-up → install → commissioning → final.
- Service / repair call: customer reports symptom (no cool, no heat, weird noise, high bills), tech diagnoses, repairs if simple or quotes major repair vs. replacement. Trigger → diagnostic → quote → repair or replace → invoice + warranty.
- Maintenance tune-up: scheduled (often as part of a contract), full PM checklist — coil cleaning, blower inspection, refrigerant verification, filter, combustion analysis, electrical inspection, capacitor test. Trigger → arrive → checklist → report findings → recommendations → close.
- Maintenance contract renewal: annual renewal cycle, often spring or fall. Customer gets notice 30-60 days out. Trigger → renewal letter / email → call → renew with adjustments or churn.
- Seasonal upsell (spring AC tune-up, fall furnace tune-up, IAQ accessories, smart thermostats): promotional email or postcard campaigns timed to season. Trigger → seasonal email → call-ins → schedule → tune-up + recommendations.

## What to avoid / common mistakes

- Quoting "replacement to nameplate." If the existing system is wrong (oversized, undersized), the customer keeps the problem. Always reference the Manual J or state clearly why you're sizing to the existing.
- Skipping AHRI match. A new condenser with the old evaporator coil voids warranty and loses efficiency. The proposal must specify the matched coil model.
- Fear-mongering on system age. "It could fail any day" is unprofessional. "It's at end of typical service life; let's plan replacement on your timeline" is better.
- Burying maintenance contract auto-renewal. State it plainly. Customers who feel tricked don't renew next year.
- Quoting refrigerant prices in customer-facing copy without confirming current per-pound cost. Refrigerant pricing fluctuates; stale numbers create disputes.
- Recommending a heat pump in a climate without doing a winter design-load check. Cold-climate heat pumps work in -25C, but not every air-source heat pump does. Sizing and brand matter.

## Tone / register

A real HVAC contractor sounds like a tradesperson who reads spec sheets. They reference specific models — "the Lennox SL280V single-stage," "the Carrier Greenspeed Infinity," "the Mitsubishi MUZ-FH series." They explain by walking through the spec — "this two-stage furnace runs at 65% capacity 90% of the time, which is why it's quieter and dehumidifies better than the single-stage." They never use "comfort" as a marketing word — they use it as a diagnostic word ("the comfort complaint is uneven temperatures upstairs"). They sign proposals with their tech license number, their gas ticket if applicable, and their EPA 608 cert. They never write "Don't wait!"
