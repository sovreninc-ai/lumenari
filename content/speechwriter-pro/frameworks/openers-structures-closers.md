# Openers, Structures, Closers

The reference doc. Three sections. Use directly or as scaffolding.

---

## 1. Twelve openers, with worked examples

### A. The specific moment
Time, place, sensory detail. No abstraction.
> "It was 2:47 PM on a Wednesday in March. I was in the rental car in the Hertz lot in Newark, and I couldn't make myself put the keys in the ignition."

### B. The disagreement
Name what the audience expected; then break it.
> "I was told this speech should be about leadership. I'm not going to talk about leadership tonight. I'm going to talk about what we keep calling leadership when we mean something else."

### C. The confession
Open with what you didn't want or don't know.
> "I almost didn't write this speech. I'm going to tell you why I changed my mind, because I think the reason matters more than anything I planned to say."

### D. The uncomfortable number
A single stat, stated cleanly.
> "Forty-two percent of you will leave this company within three years. I want to talk for a few minutes about the other fifty-eight."

### E. The non-rhetorical question
Only if the speaker will actually do something with the answer.
> "How many of you have been laid off before? [pause; look at the hands] Okay. So most of us. That's who I'm talking to."

### F. The image
A single sensory image. No commentary yet.
> "There's a brown paper bag in my mother's freezer that has been there since 2018."

### G. The shared thing — but actually shared
Only if the audience really did share it. Avoid "we've all been there."
> "Every one of us in this room sat through the all-hands six weeks ago when Vince said the numbers were going to be fine. They were not fine."

### H. The blunt thesis
State the argument in one sentence, then back up.
> "This company is going to be unrecognizable in three years. Let me back up and explain why I think that, and why I'm telling you tonight."

### I. The artifact
A physical object, a quote, a screenshot, an old email.
> "I have an email from my dad on my phone. It's from 2009. It says four words. I'll show it to you in a minute."

### J. The structural opener
Tell them what they're about to hear. Only use when the audience is impatient.
> "Three things tonight. None of them are good news. All of them have a fix. I'll be done in nine minutes."

### K. The single-sentence story
A story compressed into one sentence — the rest is unpacking.
> "My grandfather built a house with his hands, lost it twice, and built it again with hers."

### L. The honest start
The "I don't know how to start this" start, when truly earned.
> "I've been trying to write this speech for two weeks, and I haven't been able to. So I'm going to talk instead of read."

---

## Forbidden openers (always cut)

- "Today I want to talk about…"
- "Let me tell you a story…" (with no story right after)
- "First, I want to thank you for having me…"
- "Everyone in this room knows…"
- "Webster's defines…"
- "It's an honor to be here…"
- "Before I start, a quick joke…" (unless the joke is genuinely funny and on-topic)
- "We live in a world where…" / "In today's fast-paced…"

## 2. Six structures

### Structure 1: Problem / Picture / Proof / Promise
Most flexible. Works for keynotes, internal updates, pitches.

- **Problem (1-2 min)**: name the situation honestly. No "challenge"; say problem.
- **Picture (2-3 min)**: paint what it looks like if it's solved. Concrete, sensory.
- **Proof (3-5 min)**: evidence the picture is achievable. Cases, data, anecdotes.
- **Promise (1-2 min)**: what the speaker (or the audience) will do.

**When to use**: any speech where action is the outcome.

### Structure 2: Chronological
Best for eulogies, anniversaries, retirements, founder stories.

- Open with a moment in time (not "born in 1947").
- Move forward in scenes, not summaries.
- 3-5 scenes is enough; don't recite a resume.
- End in the present or future, not in the past.

**When to use**: when the speech is about a life, an arc, or a decade.

### Structure 3: Thematic
For content without a natural arc — academic talks, awards, panel intros.

- Pick 3-5 themes, equal weight.
- Order them by emotional pull, not chronology or importance.
- Tie them together with a recurring image or phrase.
- Avoid the "and finally" land-the-plane vibe — each theme should feel like it could be the last.

### Structure 4: The ladder
Small → bigger → biggest. Graduation speeches, closing keynotes.

- Beat 1: smallest example (one person, one moment)
- Beat 2: bigger example (a community, a project)
- Beat 3: biggest example (the field, the country, the species)
- Close: hand the ladder back to the audience — what's the smallest example they're going to act on tomorrow?

### Structure 5: Frame / Reframe
Open with the expected version of the story. Re-tell it differently.

- Beat 1: the version everyone tells (5-7 sentences, deadpan)
- Beat 2: the pivot — "but here's what actually happened"
- Beat 3: the same story, but the meaning is different
- Close: the lesson is in the difference, not in either telling

**When to use**: anniversaries, founding-story keynotes, corrective-narrative speeches.

### Structure 6: List with a turn
Three items, then a fourth that breaks the pattern.

- Beat 1: item 1 (expected)
- Beat 2: item 2 (expected, but bigger)
- Beat 3: item 3 (expected, but the audience starts feeling the rhythm)
- Beat 4: the thing that's NOT on the list. The pattern break.

The fourth thing is the whole speech. The first three were setup.

## 3. Eight closers

### A. The specific ask
> "When you walk out of this room, I want you to do one thing. Tomorrow morning, before you open your inbox, send a one-sentence email to the person on your team who did the thing that mattered last week. Just one sentence. That's the whole ask."

### B. The callback
Return to the opening image with new meaning.
> "I started by telling you about the rental car in Newark. I want to tell you what happened next. I drove home."

### C. The benediction
Works for weddings, graduations, retirements.
> "May the work you do next outgrow the building it starts in. May the people you trust today still answer the phone in twenty years. And may you keep the version of yourself that's afraid of how big this could get."

### D. The honest landing
> "I don't know how this ends. I know how it starts. It starts in this room, with this conversation, on this Tuesday, between people who chose to show up. That's enough for me."

### E. The hand-off
For panel intros, award presentations.
> "I could tell you what I think of her work. I'd rather have you hear it from her. Please welcome [name]."

### F. The image-and-stop
Last sensory image. Then sit down.
> "The brown paper bag in my mother's freezer has my grandfather's handwriting on it. It says 'for emergencies.' She hasn't opened it. Neither will I. Thank you."

### G. The single-line tag
The title-of-the-speech is the last sentence.
> "We don't get to choose the room. We get to choose what we're willing to say in it."

### H. The repeat-with-new-meaning
A phrase used earlier in the speech, said again, with different weight.
> Early in the speech: "Most days I don't know what I'm doing."
> Closing: "Most days I don't know what I'm doing. I'm starting to think that's the job."

---

## Forbidden closers

- "In conclusion…"
- "To wrap up…"
- "I'll leave you with this…"
- "Thank you for your time and attention" (just say "thank you")
- "Any questions?" — that's the moderator's job

## 4. Audience-tailoring grid

The same content delivered to four rooms. Use this when the speaker has to give "the same speech" multiple times.

| Audience | What stays | What cuts | What's added | The line that changes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Engineering all-hands | Technical specifics, honest numbers, the systems detail | Sales metaphors, customer anecdotes that flatten complexity | Architecture context, "what this means for what you're building" | Tactical line about what they should change in next sprint |
| Sales kickoff | Customer stories, the magnitude, the energy | Engineering deep-dives, ambiguity about what's possible | Quota implications, competitive framing, what's enabled | "Here's the line you can use with the customer who asks about X" |
| Board meeting | Strategic implications, capital efficiency, risk view | Internal-baseball, emotional appeals, jargon the board doesn't know | Comparable company benchmarks, ask for backing | "Here's where I need your help" |
| Customer keynote | Outcome stories, the why-now, the credibility signals | Internal anxieties, financial details, anything that sounds inward-looking | Customer-side proof points, what they can do with this on Monday | "Here's what this changes for your team" |

The line that changes per audience is usually the close. Same speech, four different closes.

## 5. The cadence pass

Before any draft is final, run this rewrite:

1. Read aloud at speaking pace.
2. Any sentence over 25 words without a breath point — cut or split.
3. Any subordinate clause stacked on a subordinate clause — flatten.
4. Any consonant cluster that trips ("statistics significantly suggests") — replace.
5. Mark [PAUSE] at hard stops, [BEAT] at shorter stops, [LOOK UP] for prompter-aware speakers.
6. Vary sentence length intentionally — three short, one long, two medium. Don't average.

If the speaker is reading off prompter, every line gets a deliberate break. The eye reads what the prompter shows.
