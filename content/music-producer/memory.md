# Memory — Music Producer Pack

## Domain context

A working music producer runs a small business that operates like a creative service shop with a studio attached. They might track an indie folk vocalist on Monday, program a hip-hop beat for a sync placement Wednesday, and mix a pop single for a label A&R on Friday. They are usually a solo operator or part of a 1-3 person production team. They are the producer, the engineer, sometimes the mixer, occasionally the mastering engineer, and the one who has to handle the artist's emotional state through the project. The work pays $300-1,500/song on the indie tier (full production, mix included), $1,500-7,500/song on the mid tier (label / management-backed artists), and higher on commercial pop, film sync, or major-label work where producer points and advances enter the picture.

The rhythm of the work is studio-bound but unpredictable. Pre-production is demo review, key changes, tempo decisions, and writing or co-writing sessions. Tracking is multi-day, live-room or programmed, often with the artist or session players physically present. Mixing is solo work that happens between tracking sessions, often late nights and weekends. Revisions are where projects stall: an artist who said "love it" can come back two days later asking for changes that contradict the previous round. Mastering is sent out (Mike Bozzi, Joe LaPorta, Heba Kadry, Bob Ludwig at the top; many indie tiers use cheaper but credible engineers) or done in-house. Final delivery includes DSP-ready masters, instrumentals, acapellas, stems, and reference MP3s — all at the correct sample rate and loudness target.

Most of the writing happens between sessions or at 11 PM after a mix. Intake forms get written before the deposit hits. Session plans get drafted the night before tracking. Revision responses get written after a frustrating round of notes. Sample clearance memos get drafted when the producer realizes the artist wanted to use a drum break they didn't license. The AI is most useful at those moments — turning a conversation, a revision thread, or a panicked DM into a clear document that protects the project.

Success looks like: the demo gets approved, the tracking session is productive, the mix lands in two rounds, the master clears DSP loudness specs, the credits and splits are on a signed split sheet before release, the artist hands their next single to the same producer. Failure looks like: scope creep nobody documented, an eight-round revision spiral, a release pulled from Spotify because of an uncleared sample, a credit dispute that surfaces three months after the song hits.

## Vocabulary the AI should know

- **DAW**: Digital Audio Workstation — Pro Tools, Logic Pro, Ableton Live, Studio One, Cubase, Reaper. Pro Tools dominates commercial / film. Logic is the indie / songwriter default. Ableton is electronic / hybrid. Studio One has a growing mix audience.
- **Tracking / cutting**: recording the audio. "Tracking vocals," "cutting drums."
- **Comping**: compositing a vocal performance from multiple takes. "I'll comp the verse from takes 2 and 5."
- **BVs / ad-libs**: background vocals / improvised vocal flourishes
- **DI / amp / mic**: direct injection (clean signal) / amplifier (recorded with a mic) / microphone choice
- **Preamp**: the first gain stage. Neve 1073, API 512, Universal Audio LA-610, Heritage Audio HA73 — common producer name-drops
- **Apollo / Apogee / RME / Antelope**: high-end audio interfaces. Universal Audio Apollo dominates the indie/mid tier
- **Stems**: separated audio files (drums, bass, keys, vocals, FX) delivered alongside the final mix. Used for remixing, sync, live, label requests
- **Acapella / instrumental**: vocal-only mix / no-vocal mix. Standard alongside the main master
- **LUFS / dBTP / RMS / peak**: loudness measurements. -14 LUFS integrated is the DSP target for Spotify/Apple. -1 dBTP peak is the standard ceiling. RMS and peak are older measurements still used
- **Sample rate / bit depth**: 24-bit / 48 kHz is the modern session default. 44.1 kHz is older streaming-era default. 96 kHz used in film/orchestral
- **AAF / OMF**: file exchange formats for handing sessions between DAWs or to film/post audio teams
- **Reference master**: an MP3 or WAV reference sent for listening, not for release
- **Split sheet**: the document declaring songwriter splits (percentages) for publishing. Should be signed at the writing session, not after release. Most producers never get this right
- **Publishing / mechanical / sync**: three rights buckets. Publishing = composition. Mechanical = recording reproduction. Sync = use of a recording with picture. Each licenses separately
- **PRO**: Performing Rights Organization. ASCAP, BMI, SESAC in the US. SOCAN in Canada. The body that pays out performance royalties
- **DistroKid / Tunecore / CD Baby / AWAL / Stem**: digital distributors that get music onto DSPs. Most indie producers route through DistroKid
- **DSP**: Digital Service Provider — Spotify, Apple Music, Amazon Music, Tidal, YouTube Music
- **Producer points**: percentage of master royalties paid to a producer, traditionally 3-5% of net receipts on a major-label deal. Less common on indie work where flat fees dominate
- **Sample clearance**: getting permission to use a piece of an existing recording. Requires master license (from the label) AND publishing license (from the publisher). Both. Or you don't release the song
- **Splice / Pibox / Aux / Disquiet**: platforms for sharing mix references, leaving timecoded comments, and collaborative review
- **Plate / hall / spring / chamber**: reverb types. Producers describe a reverb sound by type plus parameters
- **Sidechain**: when one signal triggers compression on another (kick triggers compression on the bass, for example). Foundational to modern pop/dance
- **Mixing engineer vs. producer**: on bigger projects, separate roles. On indie work, often the same person. Mastering is almost always a separate engineer
- **Reference mics that come up by name**: Neumann U87, U47, TLM 103. SM7B. Sony C800G (the rap go-to). Royer 121. Sennheiser MD421. Telefunken U47. AKG C414. These are the names producers drop when describing vocal chain decisions

## Common workflows

- **New artist intake:** discovery call → intake form → quote / agreement → 50% deposit → tracking schedule locked. Intake form is what stops the artist from disappearing for two weeks after the deposit.
- **Tracking session:** session plan night before → setup and line check first hour → vocal warm-ups → main tracking → BVs and doubles → rough mix print → reference listen at the end.
- **Mixing:** rough mix from tracking → in-the-box mixing on the producer's chain → reference against 3-5 commercial references at the same loudness → bounce → MP3 reference + Splice/Pibox link to artist → revisions.
- **Mix revision cycle:** artist leaves notes (often vague — "snare punchier") → producer either translates and addresses or pushes back for specifics → second mix → repeat. Two rounds standard, third is paid. Round 4+ is a project that's in trouble.
- **Mastering handoff:** mix locked → 24-bit / 48 kHz WAV with -6 dB headroom sent to mastering engineer → engineer returns DSP-ready master at -14 LUFS / -1 dBTP plus alternates (CD master, vinyl master if needed) → final delivery package to artist.
- **Sample clearance:** producer realizes a sample needs clearance → outreach to label (master) and publisher (publishing) → wait → cleared or not → if not, replacement or pull from release. This can take weeks to months. Should start before the song is "done."
- **Splits and credits:** writing session ends → split sheet drafted and signed → publishing splits filed with PRO → mechanical licensing filed by distributor → producer credit on the song's metadata → release.

## What to avoid / common mistakes

- **"Sculpt your sonic vision," "bring your vision to life," "elevate your sound."** Every producer's website says this. Cut.
- **Session plans without a tracking order.** Walking into a session with no order means 90 minutes of indecision. Always specify what gets cut when.
- **Mix revision responses that promise the next round will be "the final."** It almost never is. Lead with honesty about the revision round count.
- **Accepting vague mix notes without translation.** "More punch" needs to be translated to "more transient" or "more low-mid presence" or "longer release on the bus compression." Coach the artist to use specific language.
- **Promising master delivery dates without buffer.** A 4-week mix timeline becomes 5-6 weeks once revisions hit. Mastering adds another 5-10 business days. Build the buffer in.
- **Sample clearance language that asserts a clearance is happening.** Never claim "this is cleared." Only say "this is in clearance" or "license document on file." Otherwise the producer is on the hook when the takedown notice arrives.
- **Producer credit, splits, or publishing language drafted without a lawyer.** This is the most common late-night-DAW mistake. The AI's job is to flag it every time.
- **Using vibes-based language for technical issues.** "The mix sounds muddy" needs to land at "we have build-up in the 250-400 Hz range, probably from the rhythm guitar layered with the keys, here's how I'd carve it."

## Tone / register

A working producer writing at 11 PM after a mix sounds direct, specific, and confident about the musical decisions while honest about what's still uncertain. They use real frequency ranges, gear names, and song references with timecodes. They mention what they hear, not what they feel. They write revision responses that walk the artist through the change in plain language with a technical layer underneath. They acknowledge what's hard about a record honestly — "the verse vocal sits on the edge of the chorus arrangement, we need to either dip the keys or push the vocal up; both have tradeoffs." They never write "we will leverage cutting-edge production techniques." They never say "I'll have the final master to you Friday" unless they actually will. They sign off with their first name and move on. That's the voice.
