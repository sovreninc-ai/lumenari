# Cold Email Frameworks: PAS, BAB, AIDA

Three frameworks worth knowing. Each one is a different shape for a different message. Pick by intent, not by habit.

For each framework, you'll find:
1. When to use it
2. The structure
3. A worked example for B2B SaaS
4. A worked example for a services business
5. A worked example for a physical product

---

## PAS — Problem, Agitate, Solve

**Use when:** The prospect has a current, named pain that your solution addresses. Best for replacement/swap pitches — when they're using a competitor, an in-house build, or a workaround that's clearly hurting them.

**Structure:**
1. Name the problem in their language (one line)
2. Agitate — make the cost of the problem concrete (one line)
3. Solve — what you do, briefly (one line)
4. Proof (one line, optional)
5. Ask (one line)

**Why it works:** It mirrors how the prospect already feels. They've been complaining about this internally for months. You're the first person to name it out loud.

**Why it fails:** When the problem isn't real, PAS reads as fake-empathy. Skip it if you're guessing at the pain.

---

### PAS example — B2B SaaS

**Context:** Selling a CI/CD optimization tool to a VP of Engineering at a Series B SaaS company. Signal: they tweeted last week about their CI bills hitting $40K/month.

**Subject:** $40K CI bill

```
Saw your tweet last week — $40K/month on CI is the kind of number that comes up in board meetings.

Flaky tests are usually 30-40% of that, because every retry is a fresh container at full duration.

We sit in front of your test runner and don't rerun the deterministic-flaky tests. Cuts the bill by an average of 35% for teams your size.

Linear and Vercel are customers. Happy to share what we did for their pipelines.

15 min next Tuesday or Wednesday?

[Sign-off]

---
Two things you might want to change before sending:
- The "$40K is the kind of number that comes up in board meetings" line — punchy but check that it doesn't read as preachy to this specific VP
- "Deterministic-flaky" might be jargon depending on their stack — swap for "the ones that pass on retry" if so
```

### PAS example — Services business

**Context:** Selling a fractional CFO service to a Series A founder. Signal: their last all-hands deck (publicly posted to YouTube) included a finance slide that was clearly built in Google Sheets the morning of.

**Subject:** that finance slide

```
Watched your Series A retro talk — when you got to the finance slide, you said "we put this together this morning."

That's normal at Series A. It also breaks at Series B, when investors ask for board materials that didn't get built that morning.

I do fractional CFO work for 6 Series A SaaS companies. Two days a week. Board decks, runway model, hiring plan, cash forecast — built once, maintained always.

Stripe and Notion alumni run my book of clients if you want a reference.

20 min Thursday or Friday?

[Sign-off]

---
Two things you might want to change before sending:
- "That's normal at Series A" might come off slightly condescending depending on tone — read it aloud
- Make sure both reference clients have actually agreed to be named before sending
```

### PAS example — Physical product

**Context:** Selling premium ergonomic chairs to office managers at growing startups. Signal: company just signed a 12-month WeWork-to-office move.

**Subject:** new office, old chairs

```
Saw on your LinkedIn that you signed a lease on the new space at [address]. Congratulations.

Most companies your size buy chairs from the same office furniture catalog and then deal with back-pain complaints six months later. The chairs are the cheapest line item — and they're the one your team sits in 8 hours a day.

We make a chair that runs about 2x the catalog price and lasts 4x as long. Forty companies in [your city] have switched in the last two years.

Can I drop two demo chairs at the office for your team to try for a week? No cost, I pick them up either way.

[Sign-off]

---
Two things you might want to change before sending:
- The "back-pain complaints six months later" line is true but slightly presumptive — consider softening if the office manager you're emailing is the person who'd be blamed
- "Forty companies" needs to be a real number — confirm before sending
```

## BAB — Before, After, Bridge

**Use when:** Your value is transformation, not pain relief. The prospect may not have a clearly named problem — they have a current state, and you're showing them a better state. Best for productivity tools, new categories, automation, anything where the "before" feels normal until they see the "after."

**Structure:**
1. Before — describe their current state in specific terms (one line)
2. After — describe the state your customers operate in (one line)
3. Bridge — what makes the difference (one line)
4. Proof (one line, optional)
5. Ask (one line)

**Why it works:** It doesn't argue with the prospect about whether their current state is bad. It just shows a different state and lets them decide.

**Why it fails:** When the "after" is generic ("save 10 hours a week"), nobody believes it. The After has to be specific enough to be falsifiable.

---

### BAB example — B2B SaaS

**Context:** Selling a customer-data-platform / reverse-ETL tool to a Head of Growth at a Series A consumer SaaS company.

**Subject:** how Loom syncs customer data

```
Most growth teams I talk to: every campaign requires a SQL request to the data team, a 2-day wait, a CSV, then a manual import to whichever tool sends the campaign.

The teams using us: when someone signs up and their first session goes a certain way, the next email, the next ad audience, and the next CS hand-off all fire automatically — pulled live from the warehouse, no CSVs.

The bridge is a reverse-ETL connector between Snowflake/BigQuery and 100+ destinations.

Loom replaced 5 internal sync scripts with us in a month — happy to share their setup.

20 min this week?

[Sign-off]

---
Two things you might want to change before sending:
- The "every campaign requires a SQL request" claim — check that this is actually their workflow before sending; some growth teams have already solved this
- "100+ destinations" is a generic claim — replace with the 2-3 destinations they're most likely to care about if you can find them on the company's job postings
```

### BAB example — Services business

**Context:** Selling a content marketing service to a B2B SaaS marketing director.

**Subject:** content that ranks vs content that converts

```
What I see at most Series B SaaS companies: content team ships 8 blog posts a month. Half rank, half don't. None of them tie cleanly to pipeline.

What our clients ship: 2-3 long-form posts a month. Each one targets a buyer-stage keyword. Each one has a CTA tuned to the stage. Each one has its pipeline attribution tracked.

The difference: a content strategist who sits in your sales calls before they write, not after.

We run content for [Customer A] and [Customer B] — their inbound pipeline grew 2-3x in 9 months from this approach.

30 min this week to walk through what we'd recommend?

[Sign-off]

---
Two things you might want to change before sending:
- "8 blog posts a month" might be high for some teams — adjust if you know their cadence
- The 2-3x claim needs to be defensible with the actual customer's data — only send to people who'd take a reference call
```

### BAB example — Physical product

**Context:** Selling commercial espresso equipment to a café owner planning a second location.

**Subject:** second location, new machine?

```
Most café owners opening location two: same La Marzocco as location one, same trained baristas, same drink quality. Cost: $22-28K per machine. Six-month lead time.

The cafés we work with: same La Marzocco, but with a service-and-parts plan that means a 4-hour swap if the machine goes down, plus a calibration check every 90 days. Net cost over 3 years usually lower than the warranty-included model.

The difference is a local technician on contract rather than a manufacturer service line in another country.

Two cafés in [your city] just opened second locations on this model — happy to introduce.

15 min next week to walk through what we cover?

[Sign-off]

---
Two things you might want to change before sending:
- The "lower net cost over 3 years" claim needs to hold up — make sure your math is right before someone asks
- "4-hour swap" is a specific promise — only state if it's actually the SLA
```

## AIDA — Attention, Interest, Desire, Action

**Use when:** You have a strong specific hook — a recent raise, a launch, a hire, a quote in a podcast. AIDA rides that hook into the CTA without spending too many words on context. Best when timing is the differentiator.

**Structure:**
1. Attention — the specific hook (one line)
2. Interest — why it matters for them right now (one line)
3. Desire — the outcome your product delivers (one line, occasionally two)
4. Action — the ask (one line)

**Why it works:** The hook earns the open. The rest is built to convert openings into replies.

**Why it fails:** When the "hook" is shallow ("congrats on the raise!"), AIDA collapses into noise. The hook has to earn its line.

---

### AIDA example — B2B SaaS

**Context:** Selling a hiring assessment platform to a CTO. Signal: their company just announced a $30M Series B with plans to grow engineering from 25 to 80 in 12 months.

**Subject:** 25 to 80 in 12 months

```
$30M B-round and a plan to grow eng 3x in a year — congratulations and also: that's the kind of growth where bad hiring decisions compound fast.

The teams that hit this growth curve cleanly all do one thing: standardize the technical bar with a structured assessment, then let hiring managers focus on the soft signals.

We are that assessment. 90-minute structured screen, language-agnostic, designed by ex-Google staff engineers, 70 customers including [A] and [B].

15 min next week to show you what the [A] team standardized on?

[Sign-off]

---
Two things you might want to change before sending:
- "Bad hiring decisions compound fast" is true but sharp — soften if you don't have a warm intro
- "Ex-Google staff engineers" is a credibility claim — make sure it's true and not overstated
```

### AIDA example — Services business

**Context:** Selling executive coaching to a CEO. Signal: they were on a podcast last week and said "I haven't taken a vacation in 2 years."

**Subject:** 2 years no vacation

```
Heard you on [podcast] last week — when you said "I haven't taken a vacation in 2 years," that line stayed with me.

That's the founder pattern that ends in burnout, not in a clean Series C close. And the team can usually feel it before the founder does.

I coach 8 CEOs at the moment. The work isn't about wellness — it's about decision quality, delegation discipline, and a calendar that lets you stay on the hard problems for longer than 90 days at a time.

20 min next week to see if it's a fit?

[Sign-off]

---
Two things you might want to change before sending:
- Reference to the podcast must be exact — don't paraphrase the quote
- "Burnout, not a clean Series C close" is sharp — read it aloud and confirm tone fits
```

### AIDA example — Physical product

**Context:** Selling a premium standing desk to a remote-work-tools company that just shipped a new product. Signal: their CEO posted on LinkedIn yesterday that the team is now fully remote and growing.

**Subject:** new home setups

```
Saw the announcement yesterday — fully remote and hiring 30 more people in the next quarter. That's a lot of home offices about to get worse before they get better.

We make a sit-stand desk built for remote teams: ships flat to the employee's house, assembles in 20 minutes, $400 cheaper than the equivalent Herman Miller setup.

We work with [Company A] and [Company B] on home-office stipends — they offer this as the default option to new hires.

10 min sometime this week to see if it makes sense for your hiring wave?

[Sign-off]

---
Two things you might want to change before sending:
- The "$400 cheaper than Herman Miller" comparison should be exact — confirm before sending
- "Home offices about to get worse" is a strong line — fine for a B2B remote-work company, would be tone-deaf for others
```

## Picking the right framework

A 30-second decision tree:

- Does the prospect have an obvious, current, named pain? → **PAS**
- Is their current state "fine" but the after-state is dramatically better? → **BAB**
- Did something just happen at their company that makes today different from last week? → **AIDA**

If you can't decide between PAS and BAB, default to BAB — it's less presumptuous and works better for prospects you don't have a deep read on.

If you're tempted to do AIDA on a stale signal (an event more than 30 days old), don't. Switch frameworks.

---

## What every framework shares

Regardless of structure, every cold email needs:

- A subject line under 40 characters
- A first sentence that proves you read something specific
- A value statement in plain language, not marketing copy
- A single, specific CTA with proposed times
- A total length under 75 words

If a draft fails any of these checks, the framework didn't fail — the input did. Go back to the input and tighten.
