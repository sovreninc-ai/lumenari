# Objection Handling: 7 Common Replies, the Right Shape for Each

Not scripts. Shapes. Scripts get caught immediately because every SDR in your prospect's inbox is reading the same Salesloft Top 10 Email Templates blog post. Shapes — actual response patterns tuned to the underlying truth of each objection — survive scrutiny.

For each objection, you'll find:
1. What the prospect is really saying
2. What NOT to do
3. The response shape
4. A worked example

---

## Objection 1: "We're already using [competitor]."

**What they're really saying:** Most of the time, "we're not interested enough to evaluate alternatives right now." A small minority of the time, "we are evaluating, and your email is the third one I've gotten this week."

**What NOT to do:**
- Argue. "Yeah, but we're better than [competitor] because..."
- Trash the competitor. "[Competitor] has some real issues with..."
- Ask for a meeting to "just chat." That's the universal stall reply.

**Response shape:**
1. Acknowledge the choice in one line, no caveat.
2. Ask a single, low-effort question that signals you understand their stack.
3. Offer a small, specific next step that's not a meeting.

**Example:**

```
Makes sense — [competitor] is a real product. Two questions if you have 30 seconds:

1. How are you handling [specific edge case where you outperform competitor — e.g., "data residency for EU customers"]?
2. If [competitor] vanished tomorrow, what would you wish it did differently?

No call needed. Honest answers are useful either way.

[Sign-off]

---
Two things you might want to change before sending:
- The two questions should be specific to your actual differentiation — don't paste a generic version
- If you don't have a clear competitive edge to ask about, skip this shape entirely and move to the lost-deal nurture instead
```

The second question is the gold one. Prospects almost always have a frustration with their current tool. If they share it, you have a foot in the door. If they don't, you've still gained intel and they remember you as the rep who didn't argue.

## Objection 2: "Send me more info."

**What they're really saying:** Nine times out of ten, "I'm trying to politely get rid of you." One time out of ten, "I actually need a doc to forward internally."

**What NOT to do:**
- Send a deck without qualifying. That email gets archived in 30 seconds.
- Say "great, I'll send our one-pager!" and disappear.
- Reply with a 600-word email containing 90% of the deck.

**Response shape:**
1. Send something useful — but make it specific to their context, not a generic deck.
2. Ask one question that surfaces whether the "more info" was real or polite.
3. Make the next step contingent on the answer.

**Example:**

```
Sending two things:

1. A 1-page summary tuned to [their company segment — "Series A SaaS companies in your stage"]. Three pages of generic deck wasn't going to be useful.

2. A 4-minute Loom from our founder walking through the demo. Link below.

[link]

Quick question: are you looking at this for a current initiative, or scoping for later? Different answer changes what's useful to send next.

[Sign-off]

---
Two things you might want to change before sending:
- You need to actually have the 1-pager and the Loom — don't promise content you can't deliver in the next 2 hours
- If you don't have a segmented 1-pager, send a 3-line email summary instead
```

If they reply to the question, you've re-engaged. If they don't reply, you've at least avoided the trap of mailing a 30-page deck into the void.

## Objection 3: "No budget."

**What they're really saying:** Usually one of three things:
- "We have budget but you haven't shown me ROI worth the spend."
- "We genuinely don't have budget — but timing might change."
- "I don't control the budget and I don't want to escalate this."

**What NOT to do:**
- Defend the price. "Actually our ROI is..."
- Discount on the spot. ("If I could get you 20% off, would that change things?") — kills perceived value.
- Ask "when does next year's budget cycle start?" That gets ignored.

**Response shape:**
1. Accept the budget answer at face value.
2. Reframe to a low-cost or no-cost next step that builds a case.
3. Plant a question about timing or ROI that they can answer when they have a beat.

**Example:**

```
Fair. Most teams aren't sitting on extra budget mid-year.

Two things that don't require budget:

1. We run a 2-week free pilot for teams that want to bring numbers to the next budget conversation. No card, no contract — just data on what we'd save you.

2. If pilot is too much commitment right now, our cost calculator takes 5 minutes and gives you the ROI number you'd need to justify the spend. [link]

What kind of cycle does budget run on for you — quarterly, annually, or initiative-based?

[Sign-off]

---
Two things you might want to change before sending:
- "We run a 2-week free pilot" must actually be true — many teams say this and then can't deliver
- The cost calculator needs to be a real link — if you don't have one, replace with "happy to put numbers together on a 15-min call"
```

The budget-cycle question is what re-engages them later. You're not asking for a yes today — you're asking for the timing of the conversation that matters.

## Objection 4: "Not the right time."

**What they're really saying:** "I'm not saying no. I'm saying not now. Don't push me, but maybe come back."

**What NOT to do:**
- Push. "What if I could show you something quick?"
- Ask "when would be a better time?" (Vague answer, no anchor.)
- Disappear and forget to follow up in 90 days.

**Response shape:**
1. Accept entirely. One sentence.
2. Anchor a specific re-engagement trigger — not a date, but an event.
3. Confirm you'll respect the timing.

**Example:**

```
Got it — pausing here.

Two re-engagement triggers, pick whichever fits:

1. When [event you can watch for from outside — "you ship the next product"] — I'll check in.
2. When [internal milestone they mentioned — "you finish the migration"] — I'll check in.

If neither works, give me a month and I'll check in then.

[Sign-off]

---
Two things you might want to change before sending:
- "Pick whichever fits" works only if you can credibly watch for both — pick triggers you'll actually notice
- If you don't have any context on what they're working on, skip this and use the breakup email from follow-up-cadences.md
```

Event-triggered re-engagement converts dramatically better than date-triggered, because the prospect's situation has actually changed when you reach out.

## Objection 5: "I'm not the right person."

**What they're really saying:** Three possibilities:
- They genuinely aren't. (Most common.)
- They are, but they want to redirect you so they don't have to engage.
- They are, but the decision sits with a committee and they're not the loudest voice.

**What NOT to do:**
- Argue. "Actually you might be — most VPs in your role..."
- Ask "who is the right person?" without a path. (You'll get "I'm not sure.")
- Disengage entirely without using the connection.

**Response shape:**
1. Accept fully.
2. Ask for the introduction with a specific frame that makes it easy.
3. Offer to keep them in the loop so they're not blindsided later.

**Example:**

```
Got it — appreciate the heads up.

Two asks if you're willing:

1. Could you forward this thread to [likely correct title at company — "your Director of Engineering"] with a one-line note? If they reply to me directly, you're out of it.

2. If that's not appropriate, would you mind giving me their name? I'll reach out directly and not loop you in.

Either way, I'll send you the summary if a conversation happens — figured you'd want to know what's going on in your org.

[Sign-off]

---
Two things you might want to change before sending:
- "Send you the summary" is a real commitment — only promise if you'll do it
- For very large orgs (10,000+ employees), they often genuinely don't know the right person — make the ask less specific
```

The "I'll send you the summary" line works because it converts the redirect into a small favor instead of a dead end.

## Objection 6: "We tried something like this before — it didn't work."

**What they're really saying:** "I'm cautious. I've been burned. Convince me you're different or leave me alone."

**What NOT to do:**
- Argue. "But we're different because..."
- Diminish the prior experience. "That product had issues..."
- Ignore it and pitch as if the objection didn't happen.

**Response shape:**
1. Acknowledge with respect — they did the work to evaluate; they earned the right to be skeptical.
2. Ask one question that surfaces what specifically didn't work.
3. Offer a small, asymmetric next step that addresses that specific failure mode.

**Example:**

```
That's fair, and I appreciate you saying it directly — most people don't.

Quick question: was the issue with [predictable failure mode 1 — "implementation taking longer than promised"] or [predictable failure mode 2 — "the integration breaking in production"]?

If it was the first, our pilot process has a checkpoint at day 5 specifically because of how often we saw that pattern.

If it was the second, happy to send the post-mortem we did when one of our customers hit that issue and what we changed.

Either way, no meeting required to find out.

[Sign-off]

---
Two things you might want to change before sending:
- The two predictable failure modes need to actually be the ones your space sees — generic ones won't land
- "Happy to send the post-mortem" needs to be real — if you don't have one, swap for "happy to share specifically how we handle that"
```

The naming of specific failure modes shows you understand the category. The prospect's skepticism gets converted into "okay, this person knows what they're talking about."

## Objection 7: The silent ghost

**What they're saying:** Nothing. They opened the first email, maybe the second. Then went silent.

**What NOT to do:**
- Send "did you see my email?" three times.
- Send a guilt-trip ("I'll assume you're not interested...").
- Pretend the silence didn't happen.

**Response shape:**
1. Acknowledge the silence directly, with warmth.
2. Give them an easy out — multiple choice.
3. Make the no-cost option the lightest possible reply.

**Example:**

```
You've gone quiet, which is fine — but I want to close the loop cleanly.

Pick one:

1. "Not interested, please stop." — I'll go away, no follow-up.
2. "Not now, ask me in [month]." — I'll come back then.
3. "Send me one more thing and I'll get back to you." — happy to.

A one-word reply works.

[Sign-off]

---
Two things you might want to change before sending:
- Make sure you actually honor "please stop" — including removing from sequences
- The multiple-choice format is the magic — don't reduce to a single question
```

Multiple-choice ghosts convert higher than open-question ghosts because the cognitive cost of replying is one word, not a full sentence.

## The meta-pattern

Every objection response follows the same underlying shape:

1. **Accept the objection at face value.** Don't argue. The argument is the trap.
2. **Surface the underlying assumption.** What's the real reason underneath the stated reason?
3. **Offer a small, asymmetric next step.** Something with low cost for them and high information value for you.

If you only remember one thing from this playbook: arguing with the objection is the move that ends the relationship. Accepting it and reframing keeps you in the conversation.

---

## When to walk away

Three signals that the deal is dead and you should stop following up:

1. The prospect explicitly asks you to stop. Stop immediately. Remove from sequence.
2. The prospect replies once, you reply, and they go silent again. The first silence might be timing. The second is a decision.
3. The objection contradicts itself across emails. ("No budget" + "we just signed a $200K contract with a competitor" = polite no, accept it.)

The deals you walk away from gracefully are the ones that come back six months later. The ones you push through silence are the ones that block your phone number.
