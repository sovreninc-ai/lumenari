# Pricing and Niching Playbook

Two of the hardest conversations in solo work: the one where you raise your rates, and the one where you stop trying to be everything to everyone. Plus the LinkedIn visibility posts that bring inbound while you sleep.

---

## Part 1 — Pricing pushback scripts

Every solopreneur eventually hears one of these four sentences. Here's what to say when they happen.

### "That's more than we budgeted."

This is rarely a real budget objection. It's a "convince me you're worth this" objection. Don't drop the price. Restate the value, then offer a smaller scope at a smaller number — not a discount on the same scope.

**Paste-ready response:**

> Totally fair — let me share how I think about the scope, and then we can figure out what fits.
>
> The $[X] tier covers [3-4 specific deliverables that solve specific problems]. The reason it's at that number is [the outcome — e.g., "we get to a launched site in 4 weeks, not 8" or "you have a positioning doc you can hand to your next hire"].
>
> If $[X] is outside the runway, I can absolutely scope a smaller version — say, $[X-30%], where we'd do [a tighter slice]. The tradeoff is you'd still need to handle [the thing that gets cut].
>
> Which version makes more sense for where you are right now?

What this does:

- Names the outcome, not the hours
- Offers a smaller SCOPE, not a discounted same-scope (which trains the client that your prices are negotiable)
- Hands the decision back to them

### "Can you do it for $X?" (their number, lower than yours)

**Paste-ready response:**

> I can scope a project at $[their number] — here's what would fit in: [shorter list of deliverables].
>
> If you want the full scope I quoted, the number is [your number]. Both are real options — let me know which one you want and I'll send a SOW.

Notice: you didn't reject their number. You accepted it for a smaller scope. They self-select.

### "We're talking to a few other freelancers."

Not an objection. A signal that they're in evaluation mode. Don't get defensive.

**Paste-ready response:**

> Smart — comparison is the right move on something like this. A few things that might help you compare:
>
> - I'd ask each freelancer to walk you through their last 2 projects in [the relevant area]. Specifics matter more than portfolios on a homepage.
> - I'd ask how they handle [the thing that always goes wrong in this kind of project — e.g., late copy, scope changes, stakeholder ping-pong].
> - My rate is in the [mid / high] range for this work. If price is the deciding factor, I'm probably not the right fit. If outcome + how-it-feels-to-work-together is the deciding factor, that's where I want to compete.
>
> Happy to send my [last 2 case studies / 3 references] if useful.

This is confident, helpful, and ends with you offering to do MORE — not less. The client either picks you because of the help, or picks someone else and you didn't lose the relationship.

### "Can we do a smaller test project first?"

Sometimes a great move (mutual fit check). Sometimes a trap (you do $1K of work, they ghost, you've subsidized their decision).

**Paste-ready response:**

> Yes, with one note — I structure those as a paid 2-week diagnostic, not a "free trial" or a discounted sample. Format:
>
> - Week 1: discovery call, audit of current [whatever], short written recommendations doc
> - Week 2: implement one specific thing we agree on
> - Cost: $[X] (flat)
> - Outcome: at the end, you decide whether to continue with the full engagement or not. No obligation. The diagnostic stands on its own.
>
> This works better than a "small test" because both of us get clarity in 2 weeks without committing 12 weeks. Want me to send the diagnostic SOW?

## Part 2 — Raising rates with existing clients

The conversation everyone postpones. Three approaches, depending on the relationship.

### Approach 1: New rate at renewal (the easy one)

Use when: you have a defined renewal point (annual retainer, end of a project phase, January 1).

**Paste-ready email:**

> Subject: 2026 rate update
>
> Hi [Name],
>
> Quick admin note — as of [date], my rates are moving from $[old] to $[new] for [the work type]. I'm telling you in advance because the new rate applies to our work starting [date].
>
> Why: [one honest sentence — e.g., "demand has grown faster than my capacity, and this keeps me from over-extending" or "operating costs are up and the old number isn't sustainable"]. Nothing about our work is changing — same scope, same delivery, same me.
>
> Let me know if you want to chat. Otherwise I'll send the next invoice at the new rate.
>
> Thanks for the work we've done together — looking forward to [a real, specific thing coming up].
>
> [You]

Notes:

- Give at least 30 days notice
- ONE sentence on why. Don't over-justify.
- Don't apologize. You're a business, not their employee.
- End with something genuine about the relationship.

### Approach 2: New rate triggered by new scope

Use when: the client asks for more, or scope is shifting.

**Paste-ready:**

> Happy to take that on. Two things worth flagging:
>
> 1. It expands the scope beyond what we agreed in [original SOW / retainer]. I can either fold it in via a Change Request, or bundle it into a revised retainer.
> 2. The rate for the revised retainer would be $[new], up from $[old]. Reflects the broader scope and how the work has grown.
>
> If that works, I'll send a revised SOW. If not, we keep the current scope and I'll quote the new ask separately.

The expansion is the trigger. You're not raising the rate arbitrarily — the scope grew, so the price did.

### Approach 3: New rate because you've outgrown the client

Use when: the rate would need to double for the client to still make sense.

This is the hard one. Don't double the rate as a "polite no." Either keep the relationship at the current rate or graduate the client.

**Paste-ready (graduation):**

> Hi [Name],
>
> I want to be straight with you about something. Over the last [time], my work has shifted toward [new niche / bigger projects / different client type], and my rates have moved with it. The math no longer works for me to keep us at $[old] — but I don't want to come back with a $[2x] number and pretend it's a small adjustment.
>
> A few options:
>
> 1. We wind down our retainer with a 60-day transition. I help you find a replacement and hand off cleanly.
> 2. We restructure to a much smaller scope — say, a monthly office-hours call at $[X] — where I'm a sounding board, not a hands-on contributor.
> 3. We continue as-is, and the new rate is $[new]. Honest about what that is.
>
> I'd love your read on which makes sense. I've valued working with you and want this to land well either way.
>
> [You]

This conversation is uncomfortable. It's also how solopreneurs grow. The clients who pay $50/hr aren't a stepping stone if you're still doing $50/hr work in year 5.

## Part 3 — Niching down

The single most-asked-about decision in solo work, and the one most operators stall on for years.

### Why niching matters (the honest version)

You don't need to niche to start. You need to niche to grow past $150K-$200K annual revenue as a solo.

Here's what changes when you niche:

- Referrals get cleaner. People say "you should talk to [you], they only do [thing]" instead of "you should talk to my friend the freelancer."
- Marketing gets cheaper. You're optimizing for a smaller audience that's easier to find.
- Pricing goes up. Specialists charge 2-3x what generalists charge for the same hours of work.
- You become more confident. You see the same problems repeatedly and develop real opinions about them.

The cost: you say no to work you used to say yes to. That's the actual price of niching. Most people can't pay it.

### The niche-down brainstorm prompt

```
You are the Solopreneur Co-Pilot.

Help me think through niching down. I'll give you my context, you ask me 8-10 questions, then propose 3 niche directions with the trade-offs.

My context:
- I do [BROAD SERVICE TYPE]
- I've been at this for [TIME]
- My last 10 clients were roughly: [LIST INDUSTRIES / COMPANY SIZES]
- My typical project size: $[RANGE]
- Revenue last 12 months: $[NUMBER]
- I want to grow to $[NUMBER] within [TIMEFRAME]

Ask me your questions one at a time. Wait for my answer before the next one. After question 10, give me your 3 niche proposals.
```

### What good niche proposals look like

After the AI runs the brainstorm, you should get back something like this:

> **Niche A: Brand design for B2B fintech, Series A to B**
>
> Why: 6 of your last 10 clients were fintech, you priced the last fintech project 40% higher than the design average, and you mentioned the fintech founders "got" your taste faster than other industries.
>
> Risks: smaller market, very dependent on the VC funding cycle. If fintech funding contracts, your pipeline contracts with it.
>
> First moves: rewrite your homepage and case studies around fintech only, set up Google Alerts for "Series A fintech [your country]," start commenting on the LinkedIn posts of 20 founders in that space.

> **Niche B: Brand voice + content systems for technical founders**
>
> Why: every client you mentioned struggled with "translating engineer-talk into customer-talk," and you've quietly been doing it as a hidden service inside design projects. There's a productizable thing here.
>
> Risks: niche straddles design and copy, harder to position. May confuse referrers.
>
> First moves: name it ("Engineer-to-English brand kits"), build one paid pilot at a steep discount, write 3 case studies about it.

> **Niche C: Just go broader and stay generalist**
>
> Why: you've grown 30% YoY without a niche, which is rare. The market is rewarding you for being broadly competent.
>
> Risks: ceiling. Generalists rarely break $250K solo. Referrals stay shallow.
>
> First moves: don't niche. Instead, raise rates by 25% and watch what happens. If revenue holds, your niche is "people who can pay premium for a generalist." If it drops, the market is telling you to specialize.

That third option is real. Sometimes "don't niche" is the right answer, especially in your first 3 years. The kit won't push you toward niching if your data says otherwise.

## Part 4 — LinkedIn visibility posts

The three formats that bring inbound. Each one paste-ready.

### Format 1: Build-in-public

Used after shipping something for a client (with permission).

**Paste-ready prompt:**

```
You are the Solopreneur Co-Pilot.

Write a LinkedIn post in the "build-in-public" format.

I just shipped: [WHAT — be specific]
For: [CLIENT TYPE — anonymized if needed]
The interesting thing about it: [ONE SPECIFIC DETAIL OR DECISION]
A screenshot or visual is: [YES / NO]

Format:
- Hook line (one line)
- 4-6 lines of detail, broken into single-line paragraphs
- One closing line — either a question, a "DM me if you want to see it," or a quiet "more soon"

No "I'm so humbled." No "excited to share." No emojis.
```

**Worked example:**

> Just shipped a brand voice doc for a B2B SaaS founder.
>
> The interesting part: she's an ex-engineer, and every page on her old site sounded like an API spec.
>
> Three sessions to translate. Same ideas, same product, same founder. Different tone of voice on the page.
>
> Her words: "I sound like myself again."
>
> Brand voice is mostly translation work, not invention.
>
> DM me if you want to see the before/after.

That's 7 lines. Will out-perform 90% of LinkedIn posts in the same feed.

### Format 2: Teach-one-thing

Pick a mistake you used to make, name it, explain the fix.

**Paste-ready prompt:**

```
You are the Solopreneur Co-Pilot.

Write a LinkedIn post in the "teach-one-thing" format.

The mistake: [DESCRIBE]
Who makes this mistake: [AUDIENCE]
The fix: [ONE SPECIFIC FIX]

4-6 lines. Hook first, then the mistake, then the fix, then one closing line. Plain language, no jargon.
```

**Worked example:**

> Most freelancers send one-price proposals. It's why they get ghosted.
>
> When there's one number, the client compares it to other one-number proposals from other freelancers.
>
> When there are three numbers, the client compares YOUR options against YOUR options. The decision becomes "which version of working with you," not "should I work with you."
>
> Same effort to write. Higher close rate. Higher average project size.
>
> I run almost every proposal as three tiers now.

### Format 3: Going on holiday

Out-of-office auto-responder PLUS a public-facing LinkedIn post. Counterintuitive: bookings often spike when you announce you're closed.

**Paste-ready prompt (auto-responder):**

```
You are the Solopreneur Co-Pilot.

Write an out-of-office auto-responder. I'm out from [DATE] to [DATE]. I'll respond [WHEN].

For urgent work, the alternative is: [SOMETHING — e.g., "send to my voicemail" or "book a 30-min call for the week of X"].

Keep it under 60 words. No "thanks for your patience!" — say what's happening and what to do.
```

**Worked example:**

> Out of office from May 26 to June 6 — taking my kids to the mountains.
>
> I'm not checking email. I'll respond the week of June 9.
>
> If you're a new client looking to book, the next open project slot starts June 15 — happy to send a proposal when I'm back.
>
> — Chris

**Paste-ready prompt (LinkedIn post):**

```
You are the Solopreneur Co-Pilot.

Write a LinkedIn post announcing I'm closed [DATES] and that bookings reopen [DATE] with [NUMBER OF SLOTS] available.

One screen. No false urgency. No "DM me to lock in." Just stating it.
```

**Worked example:**

> Closing the studio May 26 to June 6.
>
> Next project slot opens June 15 — I take on three concurrent projects, and the first two are already spoken for. One slot left for June kickoff.
>
> If you're considering a brand refresh, voice doc, or site redesign and want to lock in the June slot, DM me before I leave on Friday.
>
> Otherwise, see you in two weeks.

The math: you announce scarcity, give a deadline, and create urgency without manufacturing it. The third slot books 70% of the time within 48 hours.

## Cheat sheet — what to use when

| Conversation | Use |
|---|---|
| Client says "too expensive" | Pricing pushback script #1 |
| Client names a lower number | Script #2 — accept the number, shrink the scope |
| Client is comparing freelancers | Script #3 — help them compare, offer references |
| Client wants a "small test" | Script #4 — paid 2-week diagnostic |
| Annual renewal coming up | Rate-raise approach #1 |
| Scope is expanding | Rate-raise approach #2 |
| You've outgrown the client | Rate-raise approach #3 (graduation) |
| You're stuck as a generalist | Niche-down brainstorm prompt |
| Just shipped something cool | LinkedIn build-in-public |
| Want to teach without selling | LinkedIn teach-one-thing |
| Going on holiday | Auto-responder + holiday LinkedIn post |
