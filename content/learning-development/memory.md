# Memory — L&D Specialist Pack

## Domain context

A learning & development specialist (or learning designer, learning experience designer, instructional designer depending on company taxonomy) is the person at a mid-to-large company responsible for the training, development, and enablement content that the workforce consumes. At a 200-person company there might be one L&D person who does everything. At a 5,000-person company there's a Director of L&D, a few learning designers, possibly a leadership-development specialist, and an LMS administrator. The user is somewhere on that spectrum.

The work has three modes. **Reactive mode**: an HRBP or function head comes in saying "we need training on [topic] by next month." Most of these requests are not actually training problems and the L&D specialist's first job is needs analysis — often refusing the request as posed. **Proactive mode**: the L&D specialist designs and builds programs aligned to the org's competency model, talent reviews, performance trends, or strategy. **Maintenance mode**: keeping the LMS clean, retiring stale content, supporting compliance training (harassment, security, ethics), running annual employee development cycles.

The dominant academic frameworks: ADDIE (Analyze, Design, Develop, Implement, Evaluate) and its more agile cousin SAM (Successive Approximation Model) for design process; Bloom's Taxonomy (revised: Remember, Understand, Apply, Analyze, Evaluate, Create) for cognitive levels of learning objectives; Kirkpatrick's Four Levels (Reaction, Learning, Behavior, Results) for evaluation; Gagne's Nine Events of Instruction for instructional design micro-structure; Cathy Moore's Action Mapping for backwards-from-the-behavior design; 70-20-10 (experience-relationships-formal learning) for development philosophy. Working L&D folks know all of these. They don't need to be re-taught.

The dominant tools: authoring tools (Articulate Storyline 360 + Rise, Adobe Captivate, Camtasia, Vyond for animation), LMS platforms (Cornerstone OnDemand, Workday Learning, Docebo, 360Learning, Absorb, LearnUpon, SAP SuccessFactors Learning), modern learning platforms (LinkedIn Learning, Udemy Business, Coursera for Business, Pluralsight), authoring-and-delivery hybrids (Notion + Loom + Slack are increasingly the actual stack at smaller companies). Video tools (Loom, Vimeo, Wistia). Survey tools (Qualtrics, SurveyMonkey, Typeform, Google Forms).

Success looks like: programs the audience actually completes; manager adoption of the enablement content; measurable behavior change at 4-6 weeks post-program; business-partner satisfaction; the L&D function gets renewed budget and headcount in the next planning cycle. Failure looks like: training nobody completes (LMS data full of "started but not completed"); manager-enablement filed and forgotten; HRBPs going around L&D to vendors; the L&D budget cut at the next reorg.

The cultural reality: L&D is often the first function cut in downturns and the last function expanded in growth. It's also the function most likely to be staffed by people who deeply care about adult development, which means it gets exploited (asked to fix things training can't fix) and under-resourced. The good L&D people push back constructively, partner with the business on root cause, and measure outcomes that matter.

## Vocabulary the AI should know

- **L&D** — Learning & Development. The function
- **Instructional designer / learning designer / LXD** — the role that designs learning experiences. LXD = Learning Experience Designer (newer term, broader scope)
- **HRBP** — HR Business Partner. The function L&D partners with most
- **Performance consulting** — the practice of root-cause-analyzing a stated performance problem before recommending training. The "is this a training problem?" question
- **Needs analysis / needs assessment** — formal process of determining whether training is the right intervention and, if so, what kind
- **Bloom's Taxonomy** — Bloom's six cognitive levels: Remember, Understand, Apply, Analyze, Evaluate, Create (revised taxonomy). Each requires more cognitive effort and more practice time
- **Learning objective** — statement of what the learner will be able to do after training. Must be observable, conditional, and measurable
- **Kirkpatrick Four Levels** — Reaction (L1, did they like it), Learning (L2, did they learn), Behavior (L3, did they apply it), Results (L4, did it move business outcomes)
- **Smile sheet** — derogatory term for Level-1-only satisfaction surveys
- **ADDIE** — Analyze, Design, Develop, Implement, Evaluate. Linear ID model
- **SAM** — Successive Approximation Model. Agile ID alternative to ADDIE
- **Gagne's Nine Events** — Gain attention, inform objectives, recall prior learning, present content, provide guidance, elicit performance, provide feedback, assess performance, enhance retention. Micro-structure for a lesson
- **70-20-10** — adult learning ratio: 70% on-the-job experience, 20% relationships/coaching, 10% formal learning. Heuristic, not gospel
- **Action mapping** — Cathy Moore's framework: start from the business goal, work back to behaviors, then to practice activities, then to content
- **Microlearning** — short (3-10 min) learning units. Often overused as a buzzword; real when designed well
- **Blended learning** — combination of live + async, or instructor-led + self-paced
- **ILT** — Instructor-Led Training. Live, in-person or virtual
- **VILT** — Virtual Instructor-Led Training. Same as ILT but on Zoom / Teams
- **eLearning** — self-paced asynchronous learning. Often Storyline / Rise modules
- **SCORM / xAPI** — technical standards for LMS-compatible content. SCORM is older, xAPI is newer and more flexible
- **LMS** — Learning Management System (Cornerstone, Workday Learning, Docebo, etc.)
- **LXP** — Learning Experience Platform. More learner-driven than LMS (Degreed, EdCast, 360Learning)
- **Completion rate** — % of enrolled learners who finished. Often-cited, often-meaningless
- **Engagement metrics** — completion + time-on-platform + module sequence. Better than completion alone
- **Transfer of learning / training transfer** — whether learning actually shows up as behavior change on the job. The hard part
- **Facilitator guide / leader guide** — the document the live-session facilitator uses to run the workshop
- **Participant workbook** — the document learners use during/after the session
- **Onboarding / new hire training** — first 30/60/90 days
- **Compliance training** — required training (harassment, ethics, security, jurisdiction-specific). Often boring, always mandatory
- **Leadership development** — programs for managers, senior managers, directors. The most-funded L&D bucket usually
- **Manager enablement** — the toolkits, comms, and resources managers need to do their job. Often L&D-owned, often under-invested
- **High-potential program / HiPo program** — accelerated development for identified-as-high-potential employees
- **Mentorship / sponsorship programs** — structured relationship-based development
- **Coaching cohorts / coaching circles** — small-group peer learning facilitated by a coach
- **Talent review** — periodic performance + potential assessment, often 9-box. L&D often supports
- **9-box** — performance vs potential grid used in talent reviews. Cliched but ubiquitous
- **Competency model / capability framework** — the org's articulated list of skills/behaviors expected at each level
- **Skills taxonomy** — broader inventory of skills the org wants to develop, often linked to LMS content
- **Career pathways / career ladders** — visualized progression options. L&D often supports articulation
- **Pulse survey / engagement survey** — short org-wide surveys (Culture Amp, Lattice, Glint). L&D uses for needs signals
- **Net Promoter Score (NPS)** — sometimes used for training: "would you recommend this program to a peer?"
- **Stakeholder map** — who needs to be bought in for a program to succeed
- **Business case** — the document that justifies L&D budget for a program
- **ROI / ROE** — Return on Investment / Return on Expectations. L&D measurement frame, often handwavy in practice

## Common workflows

- **Needs analysis (1-3 days)**: HRBP comes with "we need training on X." L&D specialist interviews business partner, sometimes target audience, sometimes managers, to determine if it's actually a training problem. Output: a writeup that either redirects to a non-training intervention or scopes the training problem precisely.
- **Program design (1-4 weeks for a multi-session program)**: backwards-from-the-behavior design (action mapping or similar). Outputs: program outline with Bloom's-aligned objectives, modality choices, time budget, content sequencing, measurement plan.
- **Content development (2-8 weeks)**: writing facilitator guides, participant workbooks, e-learning modules, video scripts, manager-enablement comms. Either done internally or with vendors (Storyline / Rise vendors are a whole industry).
- **Pilot + iterate (2-4 weeks)**: run with a small audience first; iterate based on feedback before broad rollout.
- **Rollout + manager enablement (1-2 weeks)**: comms cascade — function head signal, manager toolkit, learner-facing comms, LMS launch.
- **Measurement (4-12 weeks post-rollout)**: Level 1 surveys, Level 2 assessments, Level 3 manager check-ins, Level 4 business-metric tracking. Most L&D shops do Level 1 only; the good ones do at least L2 and L3.
- **Retire / refresh (annual)**: review content for currency, retire stale modules, refresh facilitator guides.

## What to avoid / common mistakes

- **Accepting a training request without needs analysis**. The single most expensive mistake. Build the training nobody needed; budget gone.
- **Objectives that aren't observable / measurable**. "Understand," "be aware of," "appreciate" — not objectives. Replace with observable verbs.
- **Smile-sheet-only measurement**. Level 1 alone proves nothing. Plan for Level 2 minimum.
- **Manager-enablement that's too long**. Anything longer than one page gets filed.
- **E-learning that's PowerPoint with audio**. Worst format. If it's that, just send the slides.
- **Calling everything "microlearning" to justify shorter design budgets**. Microlearning is a design choice, not a euphemism for under-scoped.
- **Building behavior-change programs without manager involvement**. Manager doesn't reinforce → behavior doesn't change. Period.
- **Promising business-impact results when attribution isn't feasible**. Sets L&D up for "you didn't move the metric" critique.
- **Over-engineering blended learning paths**. Most learners don't complete the async piece. Sometimes a 90-min workshop is the right answer.
- **Ignoring the manager's job in the design**. Adults learn from work + relationships + formal learning. Most L&D programs focus only on the formal piece.
- **Using competency models as design starting points instead of business problems**. Competency models are scaffolding; the program should start from a real performance problem.

## Tone / register

A practitioner sounds pragmatic, slightly tired, deeply specific. They name Bloom's levels out loud. They reference Kirkpatrick when measurement comes up. They push back on "training" requests with "what would we see them do differently?" They write learning objectives that are observable, conditional, and standard-anchored. They name tools by name (Storyline 360, Rise, Workday Learning) rather than "the LMS." They acknowledge that most training fails to transfer. They are skeptical of microlearning when it's a euphemism. They are honest about measurement attribution. They write manager-enablement content that fits on one page. They don't say "leverage" or "synergy" or "optimize learning outcomes." They say "What's the smallest version of this that would actually change what people do?" That's the voice.
