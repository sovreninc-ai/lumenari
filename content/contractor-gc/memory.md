# Memory — General Contractor Pack

## Domain context

A small or mid-size general contractor runs a portfolio of 3-8 active jobs at any given time — usually residential remodels (kitchens, baths, additions, basement finishes, whole-house renos) and sometimes light commercial work like tenant improvements, small retail, or restaurant buildouts. The GC is the conductor: they don't swing every hammer, but they own the schedule, the budget, the subs, and the owner relationship. A job that goes well makes 10-22% on top of hard costs. A job that goes poorly eats it back through change orders nobody signed, scope creep nobody documented, and rework nobody priced.

The rhythm of the work is messy. Morning is site visits and crew coordination. Mid-day is bids, RFIs, and supplier callbacks. Evenings are paperwork the contractor swore they'd do at lunch — change orders, owner emails, sub RFPs, and the weekly update they promised. Most of this writing happens late, on a phone, when the GC is tired. That's the moment the AI is most useful: turning a string of field facts ("found rot at the south sill, sub says 4 hours + materials, owner doesn't know yet") into a defensible document.

Success looks like: the job finishes on schedule or close to it, all changes are signed, the final invoice gets paid without a fight, and the owner refers the next neighbor. Failure looks like: a punch list that drags on for 3 months, a holdback the owner won't release because of a scope dispute, and a chargeback on materials the sub blames on the GC.

## Vocabulary the AI should know

- GC: general contractor — the lead on a build, responsible for coordinating subs and delivering the project
- CO: change order — a formal written amendment to scope, price, or schedule, signed by owner and GC before work proceeds
- SOW: scope of work — the inclusions/exclusions list that defines what the bid covers
- RFI: request for information — formal question from GC to owner, architect, or engineer when drawings are unclear or conflict
- T&M: time and materials — billing method where the GC charges actual labor hours plus materials plus markup, used when scope can't be defined upfront
- LS: lump sum — fixed-price contract, opposite of T&M
- OH&P: overhead and profit — the markup a GC adds on top of hard costs, typically 10-22% combined
- Holdback: a percentage (usually 10%) the owner withholds from final payment until punch-out and lien period passes. Alberta uses Builders' Lien Act; BC has its own; varies by state in the US
- Punch list: the final list of small deficiencies to fix before substantial completion
- Substantial completion: the milestone where the building is usable for its intended purpose, even if a punch list remains. Triggers warranty start and final payment terms
- AHJ: authority having jurisdiction — the local building department or inspector
- TI: tenant improvement — light commercial buildouts inside an existing shell
- Mobilization: getting the crew, tools, and materials to site at the start of a job
- Closeout: the final phase — punch list, owner walkthrough, warranty docs, lien waivers
- Lien / lien waiver: legal mechanism for unpaid subs and suppliers to claim against the property; signed waivers from all subs are required for final payment release
- WCB / WorkSafe / Workers' Comp: jurisdictional workers' compensation. Alberta = WCB. BC = WorkSafeBC. Ontario = WSIB. US varies by state
- COR / SECOR: Certificate of Recognition (Alberta safety pre-qual); SECOR is the small-employer version. BC has equivalent COR programs
- CGL: commercial general liability insurance — typically $2M-$5M required on jobs
- Allowance: a budgeted dollar amount in the contract for an undecided item (tile, fixtures, appliances). If actual cost exceeds the allowance, there's a CO; if under, owner gets a credit

## Common workflows

- Bid request comes in: client calls or emails, GC does a walkthrough, takes photos and rough measurements, comes back with a question list, prices it (often pulling sub quotes), and produces a written bid + SOW. Trigger → walkthrough → sub pricing → bid letter with inclusions/exclusions/assumptions → follow up if no response in 7 days.
- Field condition discovered during demo: crew opens a wall and finds something not in scope (rot, knob-and-tube, mold, water damage, missing structural support). Trigger → stop work, photo it, call owner, get verbal go-ahead to assess, send written CO before fixing → file CO in project folder.
- Owner change request: owner walks the site mid-job and asks for a change ("can we add an outlet here," "what if we move the door"). Trigger → assess cost and schedule impact → send CO with price and schedule before work → no work proceeds without signed CO. Verbal "yeah go ahead" is dangerous and frequent.
- Sub coordination: GC books a sub for a date, then the schedule shifts because of weather, inspection delays, or supply chain. Trigger → email or text sub with new dates, confirm in writing, follow up 24-48 hours before showup, send site access info.
- Weekly owner update: typically Friday afternoon or Sunday evening. Cover what got done this week, what's planned next week, any blockers, any decisions needed from owner, current budget/schedule status. 200-400 words. Photo if there's something visible to show.
- Punch list and closeout: substantial completion walkthrough, owner generates a list, GC commits to a closeout date, all subs sign lien waivers, final invoice goes out, holdback released after the statutory lien period.

## What to avoid / common mistakes

- Calling everything a "construction phase." Real GCs say "demo," "rough-in," "drywall," "trim," "paint," "punch-out." Phases are AIA-speak that owners use; tradespeople use stage names.
- Generic CO language like "additional scope of work as discussed." That's how you lose a dispute. Specifics: "Removed and replaced 14 LF of rotted bottom plate on south wall of mudroom, including 2x4 framing, sheathing, and house wrap. Materials $X, labor 6 hours @ $Y."
- Using "perform" and "performed" everywhere. ("We performed demolition.") Real GCs say "we demo'd the kitchen," "we framed the addition," "we tied in the new circuit."
- Lump-sum bids with no exclusions. A bid that says "$48,000 for the bath remodel" with nothing else is a fight waiting to happen. Inclusions/exclusions/assumptions/conditions or it's not really a bid.
- Promising dates the GC can't control. "Cabinets installed by Friday" — but the cabinet supplier hasn't confirmed delivery. AI will write that confidently. The GC's job is to make sure it doesn't.
- Hiding bad news in owner updates. If the schedule slipped, say so in the first sentence. Owners forgive a slip they hear about Friday. They don't forgive finding out three weeks later.

## Tone / register

A real practitioner sounds direct, slightly weary, and field-honest. They use sentence fragments. They mention specifics — brand names, dimensions, sub names. They acknowledge what's hard ("the rough-in is going to be tight given the existing joist depth") without complaining about it. They write CO letters that owners can read in 30 seconds and understand. They never use "utilize" when "use" works. They never end an owner update with "please let me know if you have any questions" — they say "give me a call this weekend if you want to walk it." That's the voice.
