# Memory — Newsletter / Substack Writer

## Domain context

Newsletter writing in 2026 is a specific kind of work: you write a piece, ship it via email on a recurring cadence, and the reader gives you the most valuable thing on the internet — their inbox attention — in exchange. Most writers start solo on Substack or Beehiiv, sometimes ConvertKit, occasionally Mailchimp. A weekly cadence is the common ground; biweekly and monthly are fine for some genres; daily is brutal and worth it for almost nobody under 10k.

The week-by-week rhythm: roughly one full day on the issue (research, drafting, editing) and a few scattered hours on subscriber replies, growth moves (cross-promo, guest posts, recommendations), and reviewing analytics. Most writers also pay attention to "platform work" — building a paid tier, occasionally redesigning the site, deciding whether to add podcast or video. The temptation is always to do more of the platform work and less of the writing; the writers who grow are the ones who keep the writing on a flywheel.

Success looks like: a healthy open rate (40%+) on a list that's growing 5-15% month over month from compound sources (referrals, cross-promo, the occasional guest piece). Failure looks like: a list that grew via a one-time bump (a Hacker News post, a viral tweet) and then stalled because the writer never built repeatable growth loops. The second failure mode is the dangerous one — vanity-subscriber count, declining engagement, deliverability getting worse every quarter as the list gets staler.

## Vocabulary the AI should know

- Open rate: % of recipients who opened the email. Healthy is 40%+, excellent is 50%+, problem zone is <30%.
- Click rate: % of recipients who clicked any link. Healthy is 8%+ for a small list, 5%+ for a larger one.
- CTR (clickthrough rate): same as click rate, sometimes calculated as clicks/opens.
- Unsubscribe rate: % of recipients who unsubscribe in a given issue. Healthy is <0.5%. A spike usually means subject-line miss or audience-mismatch.
- Deliverability: whether your email lands in the inbox or in spam/promotions. Driven by sender reputation, list health, and content.
- Sender reputation: your email-sending domain's score with major providers (Gmail, Outlook). Tanks with high bounce rates or low engagement.
- Cold subscribers: subscribers who haven't opened in 90+ days. They drag down deliverability if you keep emailing them.
- Sunset / re-engagement: the practice of asking cold subscribers to confirm they still want emails, then removing the ones who don't.
- Substack, Beehiiv, ConvertKit, Mailchimp: the main platforms. Substack is most writer-focused; Beehiiv is faster on features and better for growth tools; ConvertKit (now Kit) is best for automations.
- SwapStack: a tool for newsletter cross-promotion swaps.
- Recommendations: Substack and Beehiiv feature that lets newsletters recommend each other. Compounding growth source.
- Paid tier / premium / subscription: the model where some content is paywalled and a subset of readers pay monthly or annually.
- Free conversion rate: % of free readers who upgrade to paid. Industry average ~1-3%; great is 5%+.
- ARR (Annual Recurring Revenue): for paid newsletters, the headline financial metric.
- TOFU / MOFU / BOFU: top/middle/bottom of funnel. Used loosely in newsletter growth too.
- Lead magnet: a free piece of content (PDF, course, tool) used to convert visitors to subscribers.
- Welcome sequence: the automated emails new subscribers receive after signup. Often 3-7 emails over 2 weeks.
- Drip: an automated email sequence.
- Pinned post / cornerstone: the issue you point new readers at first.
- Engagement curve: the pattern of how new subscribers behave (open rates drop ~5-10% per month for the first 6 months, then stabilize for the engaged ones).

## Common workflows

- **Write a weekly issue:** topic → outline → hook → middle → reframe → sign-off → subject line variants → send. From topic to ship is typically 4-8 hours for a 1,000-word issue.
- **Test subject lines:** generate 5-10 variants, pick 2-3 for A/B testing (if the platform supports), or pick one based on past data. Open rate is the signal.
- **Plan growth for the next month:** decide on 1-2 swap partners, 1 guest essay pitch, and any platform-level changes (recommendations, lead magnet update).
- **Run a re-engagement sequence:** identify subscribers who haven't opened in 90+ days, send a 3-email sequence over 14 days, sunset the non-responders.
- **Review the issue analytics:** open rate, click rate, unsubscribes, replies. Note what worked and what didn't. Adjust the next subject-line approach.
- **Repurpose an issue:** turn it into 3-5 tweets, 1 LinkedIn post, possibly a podcast snippet. Reaches readers who don't subscribe via email.

## What to avoid / common mistakes

- **Clickbait subject lines without payoff.** "You won't believe what happened" → reader opens, feels conned, unsubscribes. Open rate up one week, unsubscribe rate up the next.
- **Writing for everyone.** A newsletter for "everyone interested in productivity" gets skipped. A newsletter for "solo SaaS founders deciding what to build next" gets opened. Narrow wins.
- **Padding to hit a word count.** If the idea is 400 words, ship 400. Readers respect short.
- **Treating Substack like Twitter.** Substack readers came to read; they're not scrolling. Long-form is allowed and rewarded.
- **Buying subscribers via ads under 10k.** Math rarely works. Open rates on bought subscribers tank, which tanks deliverability across the list.
- **Never sunsetting cold subscribers.** Vanity-count looks good; deliverability gets worse every month. Sunsetting protects the engaged readers.
- **Pretending to be more successful than you are.** Readers can tell. "I went from 0 to 10k in 30 days" when you actually got there in 14 months reads as fake.
- **Recycling the same growth advice.** Most "how to grow your newsletter" content is restated newsletter-growth content. Either teach something specific or don't write that issue.
- **Cold open with "Hope you're well."** Generic, low-energy, signals you have nothing to lead with. Open with the idea.

## Tone / register

A real newsletter writer sounds like a person, not a brand. They mention specific weeks ("Last Tuesday..."), specific readers ("A reader emailed me..."), specific numbers ("48% open rate this week, down from 52%"). They admit when something didn't work. They have a point of view and they're willing to be wrong out loud. The voice should feel like the writer is talking to one reader at a time, even on a list of 5,000 — because the reader is sitting alone with their inbox. Avoid "we" and "community" framing for solo newsletters; the reader is not a community member, they're a person reading your email on a Tuesday morning. Slightly informal almost always wins. The writer's voice should be recognizable across issues — the reader should be able to tell it's you with the byline cut off.
