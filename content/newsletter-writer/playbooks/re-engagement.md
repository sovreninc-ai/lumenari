# Re-Engagement Playbook

> The 3-email sequence to send cold subscribers before you sunset them. Plus the honest case for sunsetting at all.

---

## Why this matters

A cold subscriber — someone who hasn't opened an email from you in 90+ days — is statistically gone. They might still be alive, they might still vaguely remember subscribing, but they're not reading. And every email you send to them does three bad things:

1. **Drags down your open rate.** Mailbox providers (Gmail, Outlook) use sender-engagement signals to decide whether your future emails go to inbox or spam. Cold subscribers are silent votes against you.
2. **Hurts deliverability for engaged readers.** When your sender reputation drops, your engaged readers start seeing your emails in Promotions or Spam. The people who actually want your work pay the cost of the ones who don't.
3. **Inflates vanity counts.** A 6,000-subscriber list at 28% open rate is worse — on every metric that matters — than a 4,000-subscriber list at 50%. You optimize for one metric or the other.

Sunsetting feels bad. It is correct. The re-engagement sequence is the polite way to do it: you give the cold subscriber a real chance to come back, and if they don't, you remove them.

---

## The sequence

Three emails, 14 days total. Roughly:

- **Email 1 (Day 0):** the soft return. "Are you still reading?"
- **Email 2 (Day 7):** the specific value. "Here's what you've missed."
- **Email 3 (Day 14):** the honest sunset notice. "Last call before I remove you."

Anyone who opens or clicks any of the three moves back to active. Anyone who doesn't open any of them gets unsubscribed on Day 17.

---

## Email 1 — The soft return (Day 0)

### The prompt

```
You are writing the first email in a 3-email re-engagement sequence
for cold subscribers. Rules:

1. Subject line: direct, slightly self-aware. Not "We miss you!"
2. Body: 100-130 words. Acknowledges they haven't been reading. Does
   NOT guilt them. Offers a real choice.
3. One specific link: either to the latest issue or to a representative
   issue (the writer's "if you only read one" pick).
4. Sign off with: "If you'd rather not get these, that's fine too —
   reply with 'no' and I'll take you off the list." Make it
   genuinely easy to say no.
5. Voice: personal, warm, not desperate. The writer is offering, not
   begging.

Output the subject line + body. No commentary.
```

### Worked example

```
Subject: Still reading?

Hi —

I noticed you haven't opened the newsletter in a few months. Inboxes
are noisy and that's fine; I just want to make sure I'm not sending
to someone who'd rather not hear from me.

If you're still interested, here's the most recent issue I think is
worth your time: <link>

If you'd rather not get these, that's fine too — reply with "no"
and I'll take you off the list. No hard feelings.

— Alex

P.S. Either way, thanks for the original subscribe. It meant something
when you signed up.
```

What this does:

- Subject line is direct. Not cute. Not desperate.
- Opens with the actual signal ("haven't opened in a few months") — honest, not accusatory.
- Single CTA: read the latest, or reply no.
- Easy out at the bottom. The reader doesn't have to find the unsubscribe link.
- P.S. is real, not manipulative. The writer means it.

Expected response: 5-15% of cold subscribers will open this. About half of those will click through. A small fraction will reply with "no." The rest stay on the list, waiting for the next email.

## Email 2 — The specific value (Day 7)

For the ones who didn't open Email 1, the AI assumes the subject line missed. Try a different angle.

### The prompt

```
You are writing the second email in a 3-email re-engagement sequence.
The recipient did NOT open the first email 7 days ago. Rules:

1. Different subject line angle than Email 1. If Email 1 was direct
   ("Still reading?"), this is curiosity or value-led ("3 things you
   missed since <month>").
2. Body: 130-180 words. Names 2-3 specific things they've missed —
   the most popular issues, the most useful piece, or the most
   controversial take from the last 90 days.
3. One CTA: read one of the named issues.
4. Same "if you'd rather not, reply no" closer.
5. Voice: still warm, still not desperate. Slightly more specific.

Output the subject line + body. No commentary.
```

### Worked example

```
Subject: Three things you missed since February

Hi —

You haven't opened the newsletter in a while, but I think a few of
the recent ones might land if you give them a look. Here are three
that have done especially well:

1. "The first 100 readers don't come from Twitter" — the most-
   forwarded issue I've ever written. Reply rate was 7x my average.

2. "What I cut from last month's issue" — a piece about killing
   your darlings. A handful of readers told me it changed how
   they edit.

3. "The math of newsletter growth at 1,400 subscribers" — a sober
   look at what tactics actually pay at this size.

If any of these sound interesting, here are the links: <link 1>,
<link 2>, <link 3>.

If not — totally fair. Reply "no" and I'll take you off the list.

— Alex
```

What this does:

- Subject line is curiosity-driven, different angle from Email 1.
- Names three specific issues with one-line reasons each. Shows the writer is doing real work, not just emailing.
- Provides three CTAs — the reader picks. More choice often beats one click in re-engagement.
- Same easy "reply no" out.

Expected response: another 3-8% of cold subscribers open this. Combined with Email 1, you've now reached roughly 10-20% of the cold cohort. The rest are statistically lost.

## Email 3 — The honest sunset notice (Day 14)

This one matters. It's the only re-engagement email where the writer is direct about what's about to happen.

### The prompt

```
You are writing the third and final email in a 3-email re-engagement
sequence. The recipient did NOT open either of the prior two. Rules:

1. Subject line: explicit. "I'm about to take you off the list."
   The reader's brain registers this as different from a normal
   subject line and is more likely to open.
2. Body: 80-120 words. Short. Honest. Names the date the unsubscribe
   will happen.
3. One CTA: a single link or button to "Stay subscribed" — clicking
   the link or replying with anything keeps them on the list.
4. NO guilt. NO "we'll miss you." NO ALL CAPS or "LAST CHANCE!!!"
5. Voice: calm, clear, mildly self-aware. The writer is doing list
   hygiene, not breaking up.

Output the subject line + body. No commentary.
```

### Worked example

```
Subject: I'm about to take you off the list

Hi —

This is the third email I've sent and you haven't opened any of them,
so I'm doing the polite thing and removing you from the list on
[Date, ~3 days from now].

If you want to stay subscribed, click here to confirm: <Stay
subscribed>

If you don't click — totally fair. I'd rather have a smaller list
of people who actually read than a bigger list that doesn't.

Thanks for the original signup either way.

— Alex
```

What this does:

- Subject line is direct in a way email subject lines rarely are. Open rates on this email are 2-3x the rates on Email 2.
- Body is short. Names the action (removal) and the date.
- One CTA: confirm to stay.
- Closes with the philosophical reason — the writer wants engagement, not vanity.
- No begging, no guilt, no manipulation.

Expected response: 5-10% of the cold cohort opens this. Of those, a meaningful fraction click "Stay subscribed." The ones who don't get unsubscribed on the date promised.

---

## What to do on Day 17

The cold subscribers who didn't open any of the three emails get unsubscribed. Most platforms (Substack, Beehiiv, ConvertKit) have a way to filter by "did not open in last X emails" and bulk-unsubscribe them.

This is the part that feels bad. Do it anyway.

After the sunset:

- Your list count drops.
- Your open rate jumps significantly. (Common outcome: 32% → 47% in one cycle.)
- Your deliverability improves over the next 4-8 weeks.
- The engaged readers — the ones you actually wrote for — start seeing your emails in inbox more reliably.

The cold subscribers were the cost of the engaged readers' deliverability. Sunsetting fixes that.

---

## How often to run this

For most newsletter writers: once per quarter is enough. Some platforms run it automatically if you turn it on.

Don't run it more often than that:

- Re-engagement sequences are emotionally heavy for the writer. Running them monthly is exhausting and the engagement-curve math doesn't change that fast.
- A subscriber who lapsed in week 10 and got re-engaged in week 13 isn't really cold — they're just sometimes-irregular. Give the engagement curve time to play out.

Once a quarter is the rhythm.

---

## What this playbook will NOT do

- Replace a healthy welcome sequence. New subscribers who never engage in the first 30 days are a different problem — fix the welcome flow, not the re-engagement sequence.
- Save a list with bad acquisition. If you bought subscribers or won them with a misleading lead magnet, no re-engagement sequence will fix the mismatch. You'd be re-engaging people who weren't a fit to begin with.
- Replace knowing your audience. Re-engagement copy works when the value proposition is real. If the writer is unclear about what the newsletter is, the cold subscriber will rightly stay cold.

The re-engagement sequence is a tool for list hygiene, not a substitute for writing readers want to keep opening.
