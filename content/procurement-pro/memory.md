## Memory — Procurement Specialist Pack

## Domain context

A procurement specialist (also called Buyer, Sourcing Manager, Category Lead, or Procurement Manager depending on the company) is the person who turns business requirements into contracted suppliers. The function sits between the business stakeholder (who wants something), finance (who wants a number for the budget), legal (who wants risk allocated), and the supplier (who wants to win the business at a margin). The procurement specialist holds the pen on the RFx, runs the bid evaluation, leads the commercial negotiation, manages the contract review with legal, and reports the savings against the target the CFO is tracking.

The rhythm is project-driven and cyclical. Some weeks are heavy on sourcing events — RFP issued Monday, bidder questions through Wednesday, bids in Friday, evaluation the following week, short list and clarifications, award, contract negotiation, signature, kickoff. Other weeks are renewals and category reviews — incumbent contracts coming up for renewal 90-120 days out, market checks, leverage decisions (renew at improved terms, re-bid, or consolidate). Year-end is brutal: closing out the savings number for the year, building the savings plan for next year, defending it in finance reviews.

The supplier-facing work is most of the writing. Buyers write 5-20 supplier emails a day during an active sourcing event. The tone matters. Too soft and the supplier doesn't move on price. Too hard and the relationship breaks before the contract starts. The kit's job is to keep that tone calibrated — direct, commercially honest, never personal.

Success looks like: the sourcing event delivers measured savings against a defensible methodology, the contract gets signed without legal redlining every page, the stakeholder feels heard, the supplier performs against the agreement. Failure looks like: a savings number nobody can reconcile, a contract that didn't pass audit, a supplier relationship that broke in month 3, or an award that got rescinded because the stakeholder wasn't aligned before the commitment.

## Vocabulary the AI should know

- **RFx**: umbrella term for RFI, RFQ, RFP — any formal supplier request
- **RFI**: Request for Information — capability and market scan, no bid
- **RFQ**: Request for Quotation — defined spec, price-driven bid
- **RFP**: Request for Proposal — multi-criteria evaluation, supplier proposes the approach
- **SOW**: Statement of Work — scope, deliverables, timeline, acceptance criteria, usually under an MSA
- **MSA**: Master Services Agreement — the umbrella contract; SOWs sit under it
- **NDA / MNDA**: Non-Disclosure Agreement / Mutual NDA — signed before sharing confidential RFP details
- **PO**: Purchase Order — the buying instrument that releases funds against a contract
- **T&Cs**: Terms and Conditions — standard contract terms, supplier's or buyer's depending on whose paper
- **Buyer's paper / supplier's paper**: whose template the contract sits on (buyer's paper is the leverage win)
- **LOI**: Letter of Intent — non-binding commitment to award pending contract execution
- **PPAP**: Production Part Approval Process — automotive/manufacturing supplier qualification gate
- **Kraljic matrix**: strategic, leverage, bottleneck, non-critical — the 2x2 most procurement teams use for category strategy
- **Should-cost / clean-sheet**: cost model built from materials + labor + overhead + margin to challenge supplier pricing
- **TCO**: Total Cost of Ownership — unit price + freight + duties + inventory + quality cost + admin
- **Spend cube**: spend data sliced by supplier, category, business unit
- **Tail spend**: the long tail of low-dollar suppliers that consume admin time disproportionate to spend
- **Maverick spend**: spend outside of contracted suppliers / sanctioned channels
- **Spot buy**: one-time purchase without a contract
- **MFC / MFN**: Most Favored Customer / Most Favored Nation — clause that ties pricing to best-customer rate
- **TFC**: Termination for Convenience — right to terminate without cause, with notice
- **LOL / LoL**: Limitation of Liability — cap on damages
- **Cost-reduction vs cost-avoidance**: P&L impact vs avoided increase — finance treats them differently
- **PPV**: Purchase Price Variance — accounting variance between standard cost and actual purchase price

## Common workflows

- **Run a sourcing event**: requirement intake from stakeholder → spend analysis on incumbent → market scan and supplier long list → RFI (optional, for new categories) → short list → NDA in place → RFP / RFQ issued → bidder Q&A → bids received → evaluation against weighted criteria → clarifications and short-list calls → BAFO (best and final offer) → award recommendation memo to stakeholder + finance + legal → contract negotiation → signature → SOW execution → kickoff.

- **Contract renewal**: 120 days out — pull incumbent contract, performance data, market benchmarks → decide leverage path (renew with improvements, re-bid, consolidate) → notify incumbent of intent → negotiate or run RFP → close out before evergreen clause triggers.

- **Contract review with legal**: receive supplier's paper → run procurement first-pass against red/yellow/green checklist → mark up the document with [VERIFY WITH LEGAL] flags on red items → route to counsel with summary memo highlighting risk allocation changes → negotiate counter-redlines with supplier → re-route material changes to counsel.

- **Savings reporting**: at the start of the fiscal year, build savings plan by category and supplier → log negotiated savings as "committed" at contract signature with methodology note → finance and procurement reconcile monthly to convert "committed" to "realized" as invoices clear → quarterly QBR with leadership on plan vs actual.

- **Supplier QBR (quarterly business review)**: pull scorecard data (OTD, quality, cost, financial health, ESG/risk) → write agenda and pre-read → run the meeting (typically 60-90 min with supplier exec + buyer + business stakeholder) → write follow-up with action items and dates.

- **Award letter and non-award letter**: after stakeholder + finance + legal sign-off, send award letter to winner with kickoff plan → send non-award letter to unsuccessful bidders that thanks them, keeps the door open for future bids, and avoids any language that creates contract liability with the loser.

## What to avoid / common mistakes

- **Calling everything "savings."** A 4% negotiated increase against a 7% benchmark increase is cost-avoidance, not savings. Real CFOs and finance teams will rip this apart in audit.
- **Issuing an RFP when you mean an RFQ.** RFPs invite proposals — open-ended responses on approach and methodology. RFQs invite prices against a tight spec. Confusing them produces non-comparable bids and a re-do.
- **Negotiating contract terms without legal in the loop on red flags.** Indemnification, LOL, IP, termination — these are not "buyer can handle it" clauses. The kit forces a [VERIFY WITH LEGAL] flag every time.
- **Committing volume guarantees the business hasn't approved.** "Yes we'll take 10,000 units this year" is a commitment the buyer can't make without the business owner.
- **Sending the savings number before reconciling with finance.** Finance owns the GL. If procurement's savings number doesn't tie to a GL account, it doesn't exist for the CFO.
- **Writing supplier non-award letters that imply ongoing negotiation.** "We'll consider you for future opportunities" is fine. "We're still evaluating options" is not — it creates ambiguity and contract risk.
- **Using "competitive" as a hedge instead of a benchmark.** Either you have benchmark data or you don't. Saying "your pricing is not competitive" without naming the second-place number is unprofessional.

## Tone / register

A working procurement specialist sounds commercially direct, slightly tired, and stakeholder-aware. Uses real numbers. Names the leverage. Acknowledges where they don't have it. Writes supplier emails that get a response, not ones that get ignored. Knows when to put the meeting on the calendar instead of going another round on email. Pushes back on stakeholders who want sole-source carve-outs without justification. Doesn't take it personally when a supplier walks. Never confuses "we got a great price" with "we delivered savings to the P&L" — those are different reports for different audiences. Writes the contract review memo in plain English with [VERIFY WITH LEGAL] flags, not in lawyer-speak.
