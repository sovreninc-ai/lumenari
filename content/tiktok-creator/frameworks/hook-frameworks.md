# Hook Frameworks + Trend-Fit Matrix + Caption Library

Ten hook frameworks with worked examples for each. A trend-fit decision matrix. A caption pattern library. A response-to-comment script template. All field-tested patterns — not theory.

---

## The 10 hook frameworks

Every TikTok hook lives in one of these ten frames. Producing 10 variants for a single topic means hitting all ten so you can A/B test the strategic spread, not three flavors of the same hook.

### Framework 1: The Direct Claim

**Pattern:** State the most interesting fact first, no setup.

**Example for the Supreme Court lawyer niche:**
"Your boss legally can't fire you for a TikTok you posted on a Saturday."

**What it promises:** A specific legal fact viewers might not know.
**Why it works:** No filler. Lands the value in 1.4 seconds.

### Framework 2: The Specific Number

**Pattern:** Lead with a precise number that signals depth or surprise.

**Example:**
"There's exactly one Supreme Court case standing between your boss and your TikTok account."

**What it promises:** A walkthrough of that one case.
**Why it works:** Numbers create curiosity gaps. "One" is the most clickable number.

### Framework 3: The Contrarian Open

**Pattern:** Go directly against the obvious or popular take.

**Example:**
"Free speech doesn't protect what you say at work. But you might be more protected than you think."

**What it promises:** A correction of conventional wisdom.
**Why it works:** The tension hooks the scroll.

### Framework 4: The Visceral Specific

**Pattern:** Open with a concrete, sensory moment — a place, a date, a name.

**Example:**
"A cheerleader posted a Snapchat in 2017 that made it to the Supreme Court."

**What it promises:** A story-driven explanation.
**Why it works:** Specific details signal a real story, not abstract analysis.

### Framework 5: The "You" Mirror

**Pattern:** Put the viewer directly in the situation.

**Example:**
"If you've ever posted something on a weekend and worried about Monday — this is your case."

**What it promises:** Personally relevant legal info.
**Why it works:** Direct address pulls scroll into engagement.

### Framework 6: The Honest Confession

**Pattern:** Admit something the viewer assumes you wouldn't.

**Example:**
"I almost cited this case wrong in court — and 90% of lawyers I know couldn't summarize it."

**What it promises:** Inside-baseball honesty.
**Why it works:** Vulnerability from an authority figure creates trust fast.

### Framework 7: The Surprising Comparison

**Pattern:** Compare two things the viewer wouldn't expect to be related.

**Example:**
"A drunk cheerleader's Snapchat protects your job more than most union contracts."

**What it promises:** A counterintuitive connection.
**Why it works:** The juxtaposition forces a "wait, what?" beat.

### Framework 8: The Question They Can't Answer

**Pattern:** Open with a question that the viewer KNOWS they don't know.

**Example:**
"Quick — what's the line between speech your boss can punish and speech they can't?"

**What it promises:** The answer.
**Why it works:** Cognitive itch, but only if the question is genuinely interesting.

### Framework 9: The Calendar Anchor

**Pattern:** Tie the topic to a specific date or anniversary the viewer can place.

**Example:**
"Four years ago this week, the Supreme Court quietly changed everything about employee speech."

**What it promises:** Time-anchored historical context.
**Why it works:** Date specificity reads as authoritative.

### Framework 10: The Inside-Out Reveal

**Pattern:** Start in the middle of the story, mid-action, no context.

**Example:**
"She lost the cheerleading squad. She won the Supreme Court."

**What it promises:** A story that resolves the tension.
**Why it works:** Drops the viewer into a scene; they stay to figure out what's happening.

## The trend-fit decision matrix

When the creator brings a trend, run this six-step check before writing the video.

```
Trend in question: [name the sound, format, or phrase]

1. What IS this trend?
   [Sound / Format / Phrase] — be specific. A sound is a single audio track.
   A format is a structural pattern (e.g., two-cut with beat drop on cut).
   A phrase is a verbal convention (e.g., "tell me without telling me").

2. Structural pattern:
   [one sentence describing the beat-by-beat shape]

3. Niches it's working in so far:
   [be honest — if you don't have current data, say so. Don't fabricate.]

4. Does it fit THIS creator's niche?
   [The honest test: would the creator's regular viewer think "ah, this
   fits her" or "this feels off"?]

5. If yes — the angle nobody's taken yet:
   [Specific. If you can't name an unclaimed angle, the trend may already
   be saturated.]

6. Verdict: RIDE / SKIP / WAIT 48 HOURS
   [Pick one and explain in one sentence why.]
```

### Worked example

```
Trend in question: the "Of Course" sound by Pippa Garner, currently used
to underscore a person realizing something obvious about their own
behavior.

1. What is this trend?
   Sound. Specific audio clip approximately 8 seconds long with a slow
   build into a deadpan vocal "of course."

2. Structural pattern:
   Single cut. Open on the realization scenario; the sound builds; at the
   "of course" beat, the on-screen text or face delivers the punchline.

3. Niches it's working in so far:
   Comedy, mental health POVs, self-aware fitness content, ADHD content.
   Has not crossed cleanly into educational or legal niches as of the
   training cutoff — verify in your FYP today.

4. Does it fit THIS creator's niche?
   Partial fit. The dry voice maps well to the "of course" deadpan. But
   the trend's emotional register is "ruefully self-aware," which fits a
   personal anecdote better than a Supreme Court case walkthrough.

5. If yes — the angle nobody's taken yet:
   Possible angle: lawyer realizing why a case is decided the way it is —
   the "of course" lands when the legal logic clicks. But this requires the
   creator to be the protagonist, not the explainer.

6. Verdict: WAIT 48 HOURS.
   The trend's emotional shape doesn't quite fit a straight legal explainer.
   If the creator wants to use it, the right move is to wait and see if a
   comparable-niche creator finds an angle worth borrowing.
```

## The caption pattern library

Three caption jobs, with examples for each. Pick ONE per video.

### Job 1: Hook reinforcement

The caption strengthens the spoken/on-screen hook.

**Examples:**
- "Watch this if you've ever wondered why your boss can't actually do the thing they keep threatening."
- "The case is Mahanoy v. B.L. — I'll spell it because Google won't autocomplete it."
- "This isn't a hot take. This is the actual majority opinion."

**When to use:** The hook is dense and the caption gives one extra beat of context.

### Job 2: Comment bait (when earned)

A specific, narrow question that one type of viewer feels pulled to answer.

**Examples:**
- "What's the dumbest thing your boss ever tried to discipline you for?"
- "If you've actually been in HR, tell me what people get wrong about this."
- "Lawyers — what's your version of this case you keep having to explain at parties?"

**What to avoid:** "What do you think?" — too broad, signals lazy.

**When to use:** Topics where comments drive reach more than rewatches.

### Job 3: Punchline land

The video sets up; the caption pays off.

**Examples:**
- (video shows lawyer pulling a thick book off a shelf) → caption: "the cheerleader Snapchat is on page 1,247."
- (video shows lawyer giving a stern legal explanation) → caption: "couldn't tell my boss that to his face."
- (video shows lawyer doing a quick legal explainer) → caption: "law school: $200k. This video: free."

**When to use:** Comedy, surprise twist, or contrast-driven content.

## Hashtag stack patterns

3-6 tags. Mix:

- **1-2 niche-specific:** #scotuscases, #lawtok, #employmentlaw
- **1-2 broad:** #lawyer, #legaltips, #constitutionallaw
- **1 trend hashtag (if relevant):** the actual trending tag from your FYP today, not a stale one

**Example for the Supreme Court lawyer niche:**
`#mahanoy #scotuscases #lawtok #employmentlaw #freespeech`

**What NOT to do:**
- 20+ tag stacks — buried since 2022
- `#fyp #foryou #foryoupage` — these were a myth even when they "worked"
- Trend hashtags that don't actually appear in your FYP today

## Response-to-comment script template

The format that AI does worst by default. Use this 4-beat shape every time.

```
Comment to respond to: [paste the comment verbatim]

Creator's angle on the response: [the actual point they want to make]

---

Beat 1 (0:00 - 0:01.5): Show the comment as text on screen
[Read aloud or paraphrase. Don't re-explain who the original poster is —
the audience figured it out.]

Beat 2 (0:01.5 - 0:02): The pivot
[One reaction. A face. A "hm." Sets up the response.]

Beat 3 (0:02 - 0:25 or 0:30): The actual response
[15-30 seconds. Specific. If the comment was a question, ANSWER it.
If it was a critique, address it directly. If it was praise, complicate it.
Don't ramble; this beat is where retention dies if it goes flabby.]

Beat 4 (last 2-3 seconds): The button
[A line that lands. An invitation to a follow-up. A beat that earns rewatch.]
```

### Worked example

```
Comment: "you're wrong, employees have NO first amendment rights against
private employers, this whole video is misleading"

Creator's angle: actually, public employees ARE covered, and the Mahanoy
case extends some protection in ways most people don't realize — but the
commenter is partially right about private employers.

---

Beat 1 (0:00 - 0:01.5):
On-screen text: "you're wrong, employees have NO first amendment rights
against private employers"
Lawyer reads it deadpan.

Beat 2 (0:01.5 - 0:02):
Slight head tilt. Quick "yeah, sort of" face.

Beat 3 (0:02 - 0:27):
"You're right about the private employer part — first amendment doesn't
apply to your shift manager at Starbucks. But that's not what I said.
Public employees — anyone working for a government employer — DO get
first amendment protection, with limits. Mahanoy is a public school case.
The reason it matters for everyone else is that the standard the court set —
that off-campus, off-the-clock speech gets more protection — has been
cited in lower-court cases involving public employees more than 80 times
since 2021. So if you work for a city, a school district, a state agency:
this case is yours. If you work for Starbucks, the protection comes from
the National Labor Relations Act, not the first amendment. Different
mechanism, similar outcome for some cases."

Beat 4 (0:27 - 0:30):
"Anyway. Glad you watched the whole video before commenting."

---
Check before posting:
- The "more than 80 times since 2021" stat needs sourcing — confirm or
  soften to "dozens of times"
- The NLRA mention is correct but oversimplified — consider a follow-up
  video on the private employer side
```
