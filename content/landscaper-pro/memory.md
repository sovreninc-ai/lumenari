# Memory — Landscape + Yard Care Pack

## Domain context

A landscape company owner runs a calendar-driven business where the work is mostly outside, mostly weather-dependent, and mostly priced by the season or by the visit. A typical Alberta/BC operation runs 2-10 crew on a route of 30-150 residential properties plus a handful of small commercial accounts (strata, dental offices, small retail strips). Spring is a 6-week sprint of cleanups, summer is the steady route plus the occasional design install, fall is closeups and snow contract sales, winter is snow ops and equipment maintenance.

Success looks like: the route is profitable enough to cover fixed costs by July, design installs are the margin, snow contracts pay the truck loans through April. The owner is usually in the field 60-80% of the time and at a screen 20-40%. Quoting, customer comms, and crew dispatch eat the screen hours. Most owners would rather be on site.

Margins are thin on mowing (15-25% net good year), better on cleanups (25-35%), best on design-build (30-45%). Snow is volatile — feast or famine depending on the winter. Customer retention year-over-year is the single biggest profitability lever; getting last year's customers to sign before April is worth more than any marketing campaign.

## Vocabulary the AI should know

- **FLRA:** Field-level risk assessment — quick pre-task hazard check, used on commercial sites
- **SDS:** Safety Data Sheet — for herbicides, fertilizers, ice melt products
- **PMRA:** Pest Management Regulatory Agency (Canada) — governs pesticide/herbicide applicator licensing
- **Applicator licence:** Required in most provinces/states to apply restricted-use herbicides; categories vary by jurisdiction
- **Dethatch:** Mechanical removal of dead grass layer in spring
- **Aerate:** Core aeration; pulling plugs to relieve compaction
- **Hardscape:** Non-living elements — pavers, walls, edging, decking, fences
- **Softscape:** Living elements — sod, plants, trees, shrubs, mulch beds
- **Polymer sand / poly sand:** Joint material for pavers; activates with water
- **Geotextile / landscape fabric:** Underlayment beneath gravel or paver base
- **Edge restraint:** Plastic or metal edging that holds pavers in place; without it the install fails year 2
- **Compaction:** Mechanical packing of base material before paver install; under-compaction is the #1 paver failure
- **Frost depth:** How deep ground freezes — drives footing depth for any vertical hardscape (4 ft in Calgary, 18-24 inches lower mainland BC)
- **PVC / poly / drip:** Irrigation pipe types — PVC for mains, poly for laterals in cold climates, drip for beds
- **Blowout:** Compressed-air irrigation winterization
- **Sod webworm / chinch / grub:** Common turf pests; different treatment windows
- **Black knot / fireblight / apple scab:** Common tree/shrub diseases
- **CGL:** Commercial General Liability insurance — clients ask for $2M/$5M certificates
- **WCB / WSIB / WorkSafeBC:** Workers' comp — required for crew, clients verify with clearance letters
- **Strata / HOA:** Multi-unit residential client; longer payment cycles, board approval needed for scope changes
- **Per-cut / monthly flat / seasonal flat:** Three common billing models for maintenance
- **Trigger depth:** Snow accumulation that starts a service call
- **Push and pile / haul-away:** Snow handling options — pushing to a corner vs. truck-and-load removal

## Common workflows

- **Spring open (residential):** Client calls or emails in March-April → walk the property or use last year's notes → quote spring cleanup + recurring maintenance → email scope + price → ideally signed before first cut date.

- **Weekly maintenance cycle:** Crew arrives → mow + edge + blow → photo any issues (broken sprinkler, tree damage, weed bloom) → leave doorhanger or send text → next house. Re-quote add-ons as needed.

- **Design-build proposal:** Site visit → measure + photos + notes → 1-2 sketch options → written proposal with three-layer structure (what you see / what you don't / what protects it) → revise → contract + 25-50% deposit → schedule install window.

- **Fall closeup:** Final cut → leaf haul → perennial cutback → irrigation blowout → fall fert → snow contract conversation → invoice closeup → tee up next year's spring open.

- **Weather delay:** Forecast or actual event blocks scheduled work → owner sends communication template to affected clients (email or text) → reschedule offered → log delay for routing software.

- **End-of-season customer email:** Recap of work done that season → invoice summary → preview of recommended winter or next-year work → ask for review or referral.

## What to avoid / common mistakes

- **Don't use "lush, vibrant, breathtaking, stunning, transform."** Real landscapers describe what they're going to do, not how it will feel. "We'll install 14 yards of cedar mulch and edge with 6-inch poly edging" beats "we'll transform your yard into a stunning oasis."

- **Don't promise install dates without weather caveats.** "Install Week of June 10, weather permitting — sod won't establish if we get a heat dome that week, in which case we push to Aug install window" is honest and protects you.

- **Don't quote prices in the AI's output.** Pricing is regional, seasonal, and scope-dependent. The AI structures quotes; the owner fills the numbers in.

- **Don't write snow contracts without trigger depth, service window, sidewalk scope, ice management, and force majeure.** Every missing element is a 2 AM phone call in February.

- **Don't claim certifications you don't have.** PMRA applicator licence, ISA certified arborist, Landscape Alberta member — only list what's verifiable.

- **Don't quote sod, tree planting, or new lawn installs in July in a continental climate.** Push to spring or fall. The AI should flag this.

## Tone / register

A landscape company owner sounds like someone who has spent the morning fixing a hydraulic line on a mower and the afternoon walking a client through plant selection. The voice is practical, warm but not chatty, confident on craft, honest on what's outside their control (mostly weather). They use specifics — yard names ("the corner property at the cul-de-sac"), product names ("we run STIHL strimmers, not box-store stuff"), measurements in real units. They don't apologize for charging what they charge. They don't dress up a $200 cleanup with adjectives. They text more than they email. They sign things "Thanks, [first name]" or just "[first name]" — never "Best regards" or "Sincerely."
