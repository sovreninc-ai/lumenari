# Memory — Event Caterer Pack

## Domain context

An event catering operation books and executes events away from a fixed dining room. A small operation (2-5 staff, owner-chef in the kitchen) does 30-100 events a year — weddings, corporate, private parties — averaging $3K to $25K per event with the busy season concentrated April through October and a December peak for corporate holiday parties. A mid-size operation (8-30 staff, a dedicated sales coordinator, multiple chefs) can run 200+ events a year and a $1.5-5M annual revenue, with margins healthier than restaurant catering because the dining room overhead doesn't exist.

The work is event-driven and seasonally compressed. A Saturday wedding ties up the kitchen Thursday (prep), Friday (more prep + final shop), Saturday (execution), and Sunday (recovery). Corporate weekday events are smaller and faster but back-to-back. The bookings calendar is the operation's most important document — overbooking a Saturday in October is the difference between a great month and a disaster.

The proposal is the linchpin. Most caterers send 3-10x more proposals than they book — a 20-30% close rate is healthy. Proposals that win do three things: reflect back the client's vision, propose food that sounds great, and make the terms transparent. Proposals that lose are either too generic ("we offer a variety of menu options") or too cluttered (every possible add-on listed, headline pricing buried).

Success looks like: a 25-35% close rate on proposals sent, a 30-60% repeat / referral rate from past clients, healthy food cost (28-35% of food revenue), and a peak season that books out by spring. Failure looks like: a Saturday in June with no booking, a client surprised by a $3K tax line on a final invoice, or a dietary mix-up where a vegan guest got butter in their sauce and the photographer caught it.

## Vocabulary the AI should know

- BEO: banquet event order — the day-of document with all event details, timeline, staffing, F&B, AV, layout
- Headcount: confirmed guest number, usually locked 5-10 days before event
- Guarantee / minimum guarantee: the headcount the client is contractually committed to pay for regardless of actual attendance
- Per-person rate: F&B cost on a per-guest basis. Headline number on most proposals
- F&B minimum: contracted minimum spend (some venues require it, some caterers structure proposals around it)
- Service charge: 18-22% added to food + beverage, distributed to FOH and BOH staff. Different from gratuity.
- Gratuity: voluntary tip, usually 5-10% on top of service charge for exceptional service. Some caterers leave gratuity at the client's discretion entirely.
- Drop-off: caterer drops food at venue, doesn't stay. Lowest labor cost format
- Buffet: food set out on tables, guests serve themselves. Caterer stays to refresh
- Plated: courses brought to seated tables. Highest labor cost, most controlled experience
- Stationed: multiple food stations, guests move between them. Mid-cost, social format
- Family style: large platters at each table, guests serve themselves and pass. Mid-labor, warmer feel than plated
- Passed canapés: bite-sized food carried on trays by staff. Cocktail-hour standard
- Chef's table / chef-action station: live cooking, chef on display. Premium format
- Tasting: pre-booking sample meal for the client to evaluate the menu. Usually paid by client at a discounted rate, applied to deposit if they book
- Mise en place / mise: prep and setup before service
- Pack-out: post-event breakdown, loading equipment back to commissary
- Commissary kitchen: licensed kitchen where the caterer preps before transporting to event
- Rental coordination: caterers often manage tables, linens, glassware, dishware, flatware rentals for the client — billed at cost or with markup
- Floor plan / venue layout: where the bar goes, where the service line goes, guest flow, kitchen access
- Dietary list: the per-guest dietary needs the client provides. Locked 5-10 days before event
- Allergen card / allergen tent: a printed marker at the buffet identifying allergens in each dish
- ServSafe / Smart Serve / Serving It Right / SMART: jurisdictional alcohol service certifications
- Food handler certification: jurisdictional food safety baseline
- Liquor license: required to serve alcohol in many jurisdictions; some venues are licensed, some require the caterer to be, some require a special-event permit
- Cake fee / cakeage: charge for cutting and serving a client-supplied cake
- Corkage: charge for serving client-supplied alcohol (where licensing permits)
- COBC: confirm one business day before — final check with client + vendors before event

## Common workflows

- Inquiry → tasting → proposal → contract → BEO → execution → follow-up:
  - Inquiry comes in (web form, email, phone, planner referral). Caterer responds within 24 hours with availability + a few clarifying questions.
  - Tasting if it's a wedding or premium event ($75-150 per couple, applied to deposit on booking).
  - Proposal within 48-72 hours of tasting or detailed inquiry. 2-4 pages.
  - Contract signed, deposit collected (25-50% of total).
  - BEO drafted 2-3 weeks out, locked 1 week out with final headcount and dietary list.
  - Event executed.
  - Follow-up within 48 hours of event (thank-you, photo offer, light review ask, future referral planted).
- Weekly prep planning: every Sunday or Monday, caterer reviews the upcoming week's events, orders proteins and produce, books staff, confirms rentals.
- Vendor coordination: rental company (3-7 days out), florist (cross-vendor if shared logistics), AV company, the venue's day-of coordinator. Caterers often act as a hub.
- Tasting prep: a tasting menu is 4-6 dishes at miniature portion. Prep takes 2-4 hours. Caterer plates and serves in their own space or at a client's preferred location.
- Dietary list reconciliation: 10-7 days out, client sends final headcount with dietary needs per guest (vegan x4, gluten-considerate x2, severe nut allergy x1, etc.). Caterer adjusts menu and ordering.
- Post-event follow-up + payment reconciliation: final invoice goes out within 2-5 days. Balance typically due 14 days post-event.
- Repeat client / referral nurturing: a tactful holiday card, a Q1 "thinking about next year?" email, a referral thank-you for planners.
- Tricky inquiry triage: under-budget client → propose a scaled-down format. Undecided dietary list → propose a flexible menu structure. Date conflict → polite decline with referral to a trusted peer.

## What to avoid / common mistakes

- "Unforgettable culinary experience," "elevated dining," "passionate about food," "curated tasting journey." Wedding-industry voice that signals nothing.
- "We can accommodate any allergy or dietary restriction." Untrue and a liability. Specific accommodation language is honest and safe.
- Bundling service charge, tax, and gratuity into the headline per-person number. The client sees $85/person, expects to pay 80 × $85 = $6,800, gets a $9,200 invoice. Bad relationship outcome.
- Proposing the same menu to every wedding inquiry. Templated proposals lose to caterers who reflect the client's specific vision.
- Overpromising on labor. "We'll have someone there all night" — for what hours, doing what?
- No dietary list deadline in the contract. Late dietary changes are kitchen chaos.
- "We can accommodate any number of guests" — caterers have real kitchen, staff, and equipment limits. Saying yes to a 250-person plated event when you've never done over 120 is how a Saturday goes very wrong.
- Treating dietary accommodations as an inferior version of the main menu. A vegan plate that's "the same plate minus the chicken" tells the vegan guest they're an afterthought. The right move is a real vegan dish, equal in care.
- Vendor coordination by group text. Use email or a shared doc; document decisions; reduce ambiguity.
- Saying yes to a date you already have an event on, betting one of them won't really book.
- Proposal turnaround over 5 days. The client booked elsewhere by then.

## Tone / register

A real caterer-owner sounds chef-led and operationally tight. They speak of "the team" or "our kitchen" in the first person plural. They reference specific dishes ("the lamb shoulder we braise overnight" instead of "our signature lamb"), specific techniques ("we slice the prime rib at the station, two-knife method" not "expertly carved"), and specific producers when it matters ("Hutterite goose," "Northern Divine sturgeon caviar," "Sunny Acres berries"). They acknowledge what's possible and what's not without apology ("a 200-person plated dinner with a fully separate vegan menu is doable, but we'd need an extra cook and the timeline shifts by 30 min"). They thank past clients by referencing a specific detail of their event. They never say "passionate," "elevated," "curated," or "culinary journey." They say "we'd cook X, here's how, here's what it costs, here's the timeline." That's the voice.
