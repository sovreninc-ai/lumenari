# Memory — Career Coach Pack

## Domain context

A career coach works one-on-one with mid-career professionals navigating transitions. The clients are 28-52, employed-but-stuck, recently laid off, returning from caregiving leave, pivoting from one field to another, or trying to figure out the next 10 years after the first 15 didn't go where they thought. Sessions are 45-75 minutes, typically every 1-2 weeks, often in packages (6-week or 12-week programs) more than open-ended retainers. Price points range $200-$800 per session for solo practitioners; packages run $2,000-$8,000 depending on scope.

Most career coaches are solo or in 2-5 person practices. They're juggling client sessions (8-12 a week), content marketing (LinkedIn is dominant), networking with their own referral sources, resume reviews, LinkedIn profile audits, and the emotional reality of holding clients in transition. The work crosses into emotional territory constantly — career transitions surface identity, financial anxiety, family pressure, and sometimes clinical-grade depression and anxiety. Most career coaches are not licensed clinicians, and the line between coaching and therapy is the daily working question.

Success looks like: clients land roles that fit, on a timeline they can live with, with comp that improves their situation; the coach gets a steady stream of referrals; their content earns reach without sounding like every other LinkedIn coach; their practice fills 60-80% capacity sustainably. Failure looks like: clients stay in coaching too long without progress; content marketing flops because it sounds AI-generated or template-driven; the coach burns out from holding too many transitions emotionally; clinical-grade situations get missed and become harm.

The career-coaching market is saturated with bad practitioners — people who took a 6-week certification and now charge $5,000 packages for what amounts to resume editing plus motivational text messages. The good ones differentiate by doing real coaching (not advice-giving), being specific in their content, holding the clinical line, and producing artifacts (resumes, LinkedIn profiles, narratives) that are genuinely better than what the client could produce alone.

## Vocabulary the AI should know

- **Career transition** — broad term for any role/level/field/geography change. Most career coaching clients are in one
- **Career change** — moving to a different field (consulting → product, finance → ops, etc.). Hardest type of transition
- **Role change** — same field, different role (IC → manager, doer → director)
- **Level change** — same field/role, more senior. Usually internal or competitor move
- **Career-back / returning** — back to work after caregiving leave, sabbatical, illness, or layoff with extended gap
- **Pivot** — career change framing used by clients (overused but ubiquitous)
- **Burnout-driven transition** — when the trigger is burnout, not opportunity. Common. Often clinical-adjacent
- **Layoff aftermath** — the specific transition state after involuntary separation. Often involves grief, identity work, financial pressure
- **Severance period** — typical 2-12 weeks of paid coverage post-layoff. Defines urgency window
- **COBRA** — US health coverage continuation post-employment. Expensive. Financial pressure trigger
- **Networking** — the activity of having career-related conversations with people you don't yet have an offer with. Most clients hate it
- **Informational interview / informational** — networking conversation framed as "I'm learning about your role/field/company"
- **Warm intro** — networking introduction via a mutual contact. Higher reply rate than cold
- **Cold outreach** — networking to strangers. Lower reply rate. More effort
- **Recruiter call** — initial conversation with a corporate or agency recruiter. Different than the actual hiring manager
- **Final round** — the last interview stage before offer. High emotional stakes
- **Ghost / ghosted** — when a company stops responding after engagement. Common. Painful
- **Reverse offer / signing offer** — the formal offer letter from a company
- **Onboarding** — first 30-90 days in a new role
- **The 30-day point** — when clients often hit doubt about whether the new role is right. Coach often holds this
- **Resume** — the document. Different conventions US vs Canada vs UK vs Europe
- **CV** — UK/Europe/academic. Different from resume — longer, more detail
- **LinkedIn** — the dominant career platform. Profile + activity + DMs + content
- **About section** — LinkedIn's profile summary. Most important real estate
- **Headline** — the line under the name on LinkedIn. 220 characters
- **Open to Work** — LinkedIn's "looking for work" signal. Visible to recruiters or visible to everyone — different settings
- **ATS** — Applicant Tracking System (Greenhouse, Lever, Workday, etc.). The system between resume submission and human review
- **Keyword optimization** — the tactic of matching resume language to JD language. Real but overhyped
- **Personal brand** — controversial term. Real signal that someone consistently shows up with a recognizable voice; often misused as identity prescription
- **Career story / career narrative** — the through-line a client uses in interviews and networking. Coach often helps build it
- **Tell me about yourself** — the universal interview opener. Clients often fumble it
- **Salary negotiation** — the conversation when an offer is on the table. Most clients underprice. Coach often helps
- **Counter-offer** — when current employer offers to keep the client. Usually a trap, sometimes not
- **Severance negotiation** — when a client is being let go, the offered severance is often negotiable. Career coach sometimes helps
- **ICF** — International Coaching Federation. Some career coaches are credentialed; many aren't
- **Imposter syndrome** — overused term, real phenomenon. Coach often holds the work around it
- **Burnout** — chronic depletion from work. Can be clinical-adjacent
- **Clinical-adjacent** — territory where coaching can hold the situation but where clinical support may be needed alongside. Coach refers
- **EAP** — Employee Assistance Program. Where to refer clients for short-term clinical support if they have one through current/last employer

## Common workflows

- **Intake conversation (60-90 min)**: career-story walkthrough, transition reasons, what's been tried, what's working / not, values, constraints (financial, family, geographic, visa), success measures, engagement scope. Coach produces intake notes the client confirms.
- **Standard session cadence**: 45-75 min every 1-2 weeks. Coach preps with notes from last session, the client's between-session work, anything that's surfaced.
- **Resume review**: client sends current resume, coach reads against role targets, produces feedback notes (what's working, what's not, what to rewrite). Sometimes 2-3 rounds of revision.
- **LinkedIn profile audit**: similar to resume but with platform-specific specifics — headline, about, featured section, activity strategy.
- **Career-story narrative development**: the through-line work. Multiple sessions usually. Coach helps client articulate the thread; client adapts it for interviews and networking.
- **Networking outreach drafting**: coach helps client draft real-personalization outreach for warm and cold contacts. Client sends; coach reviews replies and helps craft follow-ups.
- **Interview prep**: behavioral question prep (STAR-friendly), technical / craft questions for the specific role, "tell me about yourself" rehearsal, salary conversation prep, questions-for-them list.
- **Offer negotiation**: coach helps client model the offer, identify what's negotiable, script the conversation, handle the counter from current employer if it comes.
- **Onboarding support**: optional, first 30-60-90 days in new role. The first-month doubt is real and coach often holds it.
- **Content marketing (coach's own)**: weekly LinkedIn posts, monthly newsletter, occasional blog post. Goal is reach and credibility, not engagement-farming.
- **Accountability check-ins**: between sessions, short specific messages that hold the client accountable without nagging. Sometimes a touch, sometimes a question, sometimes a reset.

## What to avoid / common mistakes

- **Generic "network more" / "build your brand" advice**. Useless without specificity. The client has heard it
- **The LinkedIn-coach voice in content marketing**. Hook → listicle → CTA. Everyone does this; nobody reads it
- **Promising outcomes** ("you'll be hired in 30 days"). Career timelines are variable; the coach doesn't control hiring decisions
- **Coaching through clinical-grade depression / anxiety / trauma without referring**. The most common failure mode. Refer
- **Writing resumes that overstate experience**. Damages the client's credibility when discovered. Coach assists with positioning; client owns the content
- **Personal-brand prescription**. Telling the client who they are in marketing language. Identity prescription is not coaching
- **Accountability messages that shame**. "Where's the outreach you said you'd do?" is the wrong message. Reset is the right move
- **Salary negotiation advice that ignores the client's actual leverage**. Generic "always negotiate" advice can damage relationships when applied wrong
- **Resume keyword optimization as a primary strategy**. Real but overhyped. Real human review still matters
- **Content marketing that's clearly AI-generated**. Erodes credibility in the saturated career-coach market
- **Sessions that drift into therapy without acknowledging the drift**. Coach should name it and refer if needed

## Tone / register

A practitioner sounds specific, warm, slightly tired, honest about how messy transitions are. They write content that sounds like email to one person, not a feed broadcast. They name what they don't do (resume mills, brand prescription, outcome guarantees). They acknowledge that finding the next role takes longer than the client wants. They tell clients the truth about networking ("most of it doesn't go anywhere; some of it does; the math works if you keep doing it"). They are clear about the clinical line. They don't promise specific outcomes; they describe the work. They use "the conversation," not "the dialogue." They say "they ghosted you, that's the platform working as designed, not personal," not "let's reframe your relationship with rejection." They write LinkedIn posts that don't start with hooks and don't end with CTAs. That's the voice.
