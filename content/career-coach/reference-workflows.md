## 1. Client intake notes — post-meeting writeup

```
INTAKE NOTES — K — 2026-05-08

THE TRANSITION
Kind: career change (stated) / role change (revealed in conversation)
Trigger: laid off 5 weeks ago in a leadership reorg at a 300-person manufacturer where she'd been 7 years
Timeline: 8 weeks severance remaining. K wants a role within 12-16 weeks. Realistic window for a director-level operations role: 10-20 weeks; for a true career change: 6-12 months

THE CAREER STORY (in her words, distilled)
Started in retail ops at 22. Moved into manufacturing ops at 28 ("by accident, my partner got a job out here"). 13 years in manufacturing ops, the last 7 at one company. Director for the last 4. Got along with the COO who left in February; the new COO restructured and her role got merged with another director's. "I wasn't bad at it. I think I was just expensive."

VALUES (what surfaced)
- Stability — financial and identity. The breadwinner role matters to her
- Competence — she likes being good at the thing
- Independence — she pushed back twice on consulting/contract suggestions ("I want a job")
- Family — two kids (9, 7), partner works part-time, mortgage

CONSTRAINTS
- Financial: 8 weeks severance, then unemployment + partner's part-time income. Mortgage. Runway pressure is real
- Family: location-locked to current city for 18+ months (older kid in a sports program)
- Geographic: open to remote-US; not open to relocation right now
- Industry: SAYS open to anything; ACTUALLY applying only to manufacturing ops roles (see below)

WHAT SHE'S TRIED
- 22 applications in 5 weeks, mostly to LinkedIn-posted roles
- 4 networking conversations with former colleagues (felt awkward; hasn't done a 5th)
- One coffee with a recruiter (no follow-up from the recruiter)
- Updated her resume "a little" — hasn't touched LinkedIn

WHAT'S NOT WORKING
- The 22 applications: 1 first-round phone screen, 21 silences. She's reading the silences as personal rejection
- The networking is shallow ("I asked them how they were doing") — she hasn't asked anyone for help directly
- The resume isn't bad but the LinkedIn is dated 2019

WHAT'S OFF THE TABLE
- Relocation
- Comp below $135k base (current was $165k; severance was sized to that)
- Roles that require travel >25%

SUCCESS MEASURE (6-month view)
- Landed in a role at $140k+ base, with kids' lives undisrupted, with K not "in the bed-all-day place"
- The "bed-all-day place" line is hers — see clinical-line check below

ENGAGEMENT SCOPE
- 12-week program, 45 min weekly
- Between-session work: resume rewrite (2-3 rounds), LinkedIn rebuild, networking outreach drafting, interview prep, offer negotiation if applicable
- Off-scope: clinical work — if depression-adjacent signals deepen, refer

CLINICAL-LINE CHECK — FLAGGED
K mentioned "sleeping 11 hours and can't get out of bed before 10 some days" and described it as "just adjusting." She also said success looks like "K not in the bed-all-day place." Without diagnosing, the pattern (recent significant loss + financial pressure + extended sleep + identity-language about a bed-all-day place) is depression-adjacent.

Action: At session 2, I'll surface gently — "When you said the bed-all-day place, what did you mean? Has that been the last 5 weeks, or longer?" If it's the last 5 weeks and seems situational, we proceed with career work and watch. If it's longer or worsening, I refer.

K has medical coverage for 4 more weeks via COBRA-equivalent. Worth knowing.

THE THING K DIDN'T SAY (hold loosely)
She talked about manufacturing ops the whole conversation. She did not name a single thing she actually wants to do. The "pivot" language is aspirational; her behavior says "another ops role, ideally less corporate." I'll hold this and see if it surfaces in session 3-4 once we're past the initial shock.

---

What I assumed; what to verify before using:
- The severance timeline (confirm at session 2; she said "about 8 weeks" but didn't have an exact end date)
- The mortgage and partner-income detail (she mentioned but didn't go deep)
- That she'd be open to surfacing the "bed-all-day" question — I'll soft-ask, not direct-confront

Coaching stance check: held — intake is observational, no advice given, the inferences ("the pivot language is aspirational") are held loosely
Clinical-line check: flagged at top, protocol set for session 2
Voice check: this is for me, not for client — second-person to self
```

## 2. LinkedIn content series — five posts from one client pattern

Context: career coach has seen a pattern across 6+ clients in the last year — they say they want a career change, but they apply to lateral moves. The coach wants to write a five-post series about it. Anonymized.

### Post 1 — The pattern

```
The gap I keep seeing isn't between dreaming and doing.

It's between what people say they want and what they apply to.

A client three weeks ago: "I want to get out of manufacturing." Last week she sent me her tracker. 18 of 19 applications were manufacturing ops roles.

This isn't her failing. It's her body knowing what she can stand to face right now. The lateral move is the one she can apply to without re-asking the harder question. The harder question — what would I do if I could do anything — costs more than she has the energy for, six weeks into a layoff with a mortgage.

I'm not going to tell her to be more ambitious. I'm going to ask her what would make the harder question safer to sit with for ten minutes a day.

That's the work.
```

### Post 2 — Why the lateral move keeps winning

```
Why the lateral move keeps winning in the early weeks of a transition:

It doesn't require you to know who you are right now.

The career change requires a story. The lateral move requires a resume.

When someone's been laid off five weeks ago and the kids' tuition is in the bank account, the story is too expensive. The resume is what you already have.

This isn't a flaw. It's energy economics. Until the runway feels less pressurized, the question of what the next decade should look like is going to feel like a luxury.

The job of coaching here isn't to dissolve that. It's to make the next 90 days survivable enough that the harder question gets oxygen.
```

### Post 3 — What you can do when the lateral move is wrong but the energy is gone

```
What to do when you know the lateral move is wrong but the energy for the bigger change isn't there:

Don't try to solve it with a vision exercise.

Don't journal about your values.

Don't read another book about following your purpose.

What works, in my experience with clients in this spot:

Take 90 minutes a week — ONE 90-minute block — and protect it as the time you're allowed to sit with the harder question. Not solve it. Sit with it.

The rest of the week, apply to whatever you can stand to face. Take the lateral if the lateral is what comes.

The point isn't to make the perfect next move from a place of no energy. The point is to keep one corner of the week alive for the question that matters.

90 minutes a week is enough to keep the question warm. You don't have to know yet.
```

### Post 4 — The thing nobody tells you about networking when you're tired

```
The thing nobody tells you about networking when you're five weeks into a layoff:

It's not the "ask for help" part that's hard.

It's the part where you have to be a person in the conversation. You have to be interested in them. You have to have something to say. You have to not just sit there waiting for the part where they offer to forward your resume.

When you're depleted, networking doesn't fail because you didn't ask the right question. It fails because there's no "you" in the conversation.

The version of networking that actually works when you're tired: short, low-pressure, voice-note-style. "Hey — saw you got that role. Glad for you. No agenda, just glad."

That's enough. It's the easiest message to send when you're depleted. It does more for your network in the long term than a polished informational interview pitch you can't bring yourself to draft.

Send three of those tomorrow. Not for a job. Just because.
```

### Post 5 — When to refer

```
Sometimes the work in front of me isn't career coaching.

I had a session this week with someone who's six weeks into a layoff and described his sleep pattern in a way that made me stop the career conversation.

I'm not a clinician. I don't diagnose. But when a client describes vegetative symptoms — sleeping 11 hours and still tired, can't get out of bed, persistent flatness — career strategy is the wrong tool.

The conversation that session became: "Do you have someone clinical you talk to? Is there an EAP from your last employer that's still active? Want me to help you find a referral?"

That's the work that day.

The job search will still be there next month. The clinical work, if it's needed, won't wait.

If you're a coach reading this — practice that conversation. Have referral language ready. Know the line.

If you're someone in the middle of a transition reading this — the line between "I'm just adjusting" and "I might need real support" is a conversation worth having with someone who knows what to look for.
```

## 3. Accountability check-ins — three pacing examples

Context: same client K, three different weeks, three different situations.

### Week 2 — touch (no question, no expected reply)

```
Saw the manufacturing director role at [Company] posted yesterday. Knew you'd see it. Not sending it because you've already got it.
```

### Week 4 — question (one short, specific)

```
Curious how the call with Maya went on Tuesday.
```

### Week 6 — reset (K hasn't been heard from in 9 days past last agreed action)

```
You've gone quiet — which usually means one of three things in my experience. The thing you said you'd do felt harder once you got into it, OR something landed in life that pulled the oxygen, OR you've made progress and didn't want to tell me until it was further along.

Whichever it is, I want to hear. 15 min on the phone, anytime this week. Pick a slot: [link]

— [Coach first name]
```

## 4. Resume review notes

Context: K's current resume, targeted at Director of Operations roles in manufacturing or adjacent (logistics, supply chain). 2 pages, dense.

```
RESUME REVIEW — K — 2026-05-15

THE TARGET
Director of Operations roles, $140-180k base, manufacturing or adjacent (logistics, supply chain ops, distribution). Remote-US preferred; willing to consider hybrid in current metro

WHAT'S WORKING
1. The most recent role at [Company] has three quantified accomplishments — the $4.2M cost reduction, the 23% efficiency lift, the team-size growth from 18 to 31. Real signal. Lead with these
2. The credential line (Six Sigma Black Belt, APICS CSCP) is positioned right at the top. Recruiters scanning for ops will see it in 2 seconds
3. The progression from coordinator → manager → senior manager → director is visually clean. The arc is the signal

WHAT'S NOT WORKING
1. The summary section is generic. "Results-driven operations leader with proven track record" is what every operations resume says. The summary should sound like K — specifically, the part of K that ran a $32M ops budget through a COVID supply-chain crisis without missing a customer commitment
2. The third bullet on the [Company] role buries the lede. The team-size growth and the new shift launch are in the same bullet — separate them
3. The 2017-2019 senior manager role has 6 bullets; 4 of them are weak. Cut to 3 strong ones
4. The earlier roles (2010-2015) have too much detail. Two roles in one block, three bullets total, no more
5. The "Skills" section is a wall of buzzwords. Cut to 8-10 specific tools/methodologies (SAP, Oracle WMS, Six Sigma, Lean, etc.) — drop the soft-skill nouns ("leadership," "communication")

REWRITE PRIORITIES (in order)
1. Summary section — rewrite to sound like K, not like a template
2. [Company] role bullets — split, lead with the strongest
3. Skills section — prune
4. Senior manager role (2017-2019) — cut to 3 strong bullets
5. Pre-2015 roles — compress to one block

SPECIFIC LINE-BY-LINE NOTES

Current summary:
"Results-driven Director of Operations with proven track record of delivering operational excellence in fast-paced manufacturing environments. Strong leadership skills with demonstrated ability to drive process improvements and team development."

Rewrite:
"Director of Operations with 13 years in manufacturing ops, including 4 years owning a $32M operating budget across 4 facilities. Ran the COVID-era supply chain through 11 disruptions without a customer-commitment miss; led the post-2022 facility consolidation that delivered $4.2M annual savings. Six Sigma Black Belt; APICS CSCP."

Current [Company] bullet 3:
"Grew operations team from 18 to 31 and launched second shift at facility 4, supporting $8M revenue expansion."

Rewrite as two bullets:
- "Scaled operations team from 18 to 31 across 18 months, including hiring 2 shift supervisors and a CI lead; quarterly attrition held at 4% during the growth period"
- "Launched second-shift operations at facility 4 in 4 months from approval to first run, enabling $8M revenue expansion in 2023"

WHAT TO LEAVE ALONE
- The header (name, location, LinkedIn, phone, email) — clean
- The credential line — perfect
- The [Earlier Company] manager role bullets — strong as written
- The format / fonts / spacing — readable

WHAT WE'RE NOT FIXING IN THIS PASS
- The cover letter (separate engagement)
- The thank-you / follow-up note templates (after first interview)
- Tailoring for specific roles (do that per-application; this is the master)

---

What I assumed; what to verify before using:
- The $4.2M, 23%, 18→31 numbers — confirm with K's records before this resume goes live
- The "11 disruptions without a miss" framing — confirm K is comfortable with the specificity; some clients want this sharper, some softer
- The senior-manager role cuts — K may have attachment to bullets we're cutting; walk through together in session

Coaching stance check: held — feedback is specific and grounded, not generic, and the rewrites are positioning of K's actual experience, not invention
Clinical-line check: clear (resume work; no clinical signal here)
Voice check: review notes for coach use; rewrites are in client's voice (first-person facts about her work)
```

## 5. Networking outreach drafts (for the client to send)

Context: K identified three former colleagues she'd want to reach back out to. Coach drafts three outreach messages in K's voice for her to adapt and send.

### Outreach 1 — Warm reconnect, former colleague, no specific ask yet

```
Subject: Long time

Hi Dan,

Saw you moved to [Company] last spring — good move. Hope it's been going well.

I left [old company] in March (got merged out in the leadership reorg). Going to be in transition for a few months and figured I'd reach out to people I've been meaning to catch up with anyway.

No agenda — want to grab 20 min on a call sometime in the next few weeks?

— K
```

### Outreach 2 — Direct ask, mutual contact, specific role

```
Subject: Quick question — operations director role at [Target Company]

Hi Maria,

I know Pete Lin worked with you at [Target Company] before you moved over. I saw they posted a Senior Director of Operations role last week and the JD reads like it has my name on it.

Two specific things I'd love to ask you:
1. Anything you can share about what the team's like / what kind of person tends to do well there
2. If you'd be open to a brief intro to whoever the hiring lead is, or whether your judgment is that it's better to go through formal channels

Either way: no pressure. Happy to send you the JD link if you want context first.

— K
```

### Outreach 3 — Cold (no mutual contact), specific connection

```
Subject: Your recent post on ops as a customer-facing function

Hi Roy,

Read your post last week on ops-as-customer-facing — the line about "every shipping miss is a customer-trust event" landed. We were having the same conversation at [Company] before I left in March.

I'm in transition right now and thinking about which kinds of ops orgs I actually want to work for. The frame you're operating from is one of the ones I'm trying to be near.

Would 20 minutes on a call be too much to ask? No agenda beyond wanting to learn how you're thinking about ops design.

If no, totally fine — just glad I read the post.

— K
linkedin.com/in/kjones
```

## 6. Career-story narrative — K's through-line

```
CAREER STORY — K — 2026-05-22

THE THROUGH-LINE (one sentence)
I'm the operations director who runs the back-of-house so the front-of-house can keep its promises to customers.

THE PIVOT MOMENTS

1. 2007 — Started in retail ops at 22 because I needed a job after college; discovered I liked the part of ops that's invisible when it works
2. 2013 — Moved into manufacturing ops when my partner took a job in [city]. The transition from retail to manufacturing was harder than I thought; the rigor was different. Stayed because the work was more substantive
3. 2017 — Promoted to senior manager. The first time I had managers reporting to me. Learned that my job had stopped being about ops and started being about whether the people running ops trusted each other
4. 2020 — Promoted to Director. COVID hit four months later. The 11 supply-chain disruptions without a customer-commitment miss is the thing I'm most proud of in my career, and the thing that nearly broke me
5. 2025 — Got merged out in the leadership reorg. Spent five weeks figuring out what I want next; here

THE SKILL ARC
- 2007-2013: doing ops — the work itself
- 2013-2017: managing ops — process and team
- 2017-2020: scaling ops — multi-site, multi-shift, building leaders
- 2020-2025: ops as customer-trust function — every commitment we made, we kept
- Going forward: the version of all of this where I'm in an org that treats ops as a strategic function, not a cost center

WHAT I CARE ABOUT NOW (clear)
- Ops as a strategic function (not relegated to the back of the org chart)
- Building leaders who can run their own pieces
- Working in a place where the values get tested in the hard moments and hold
- Not having to fight to be in the room when ops decisions get made

THE TRANSITION CLAIM
After 13 years in manufacturing ops, I'm looking for the next director-level role where ops is treated as central to the business strategy, not as overhead. I'm open to manufacturing, supply chain, logistics, or distribution — what matters more than the industry is how the org treats the function. Why now: my last role got eliminated in a reorg that consolidated ops under finance. I'm looking for an environment where that wouldn't have been the move.

ONE-LINER (interview-ready, 30 seconds)
"I'm an operations director — 13 years in manufacturing ops, the last 4 owning a $32M budget across four facilities. I'm proudest of running our supply chain through 11 disruptions in 2020 without a customer-commitment miss. I'm looking for my next role somewhere ops gets treated as a strategic function, not a cost center — which is why I'm having this conversation."

VARIATIONS

For networking conversations (less formal):
"Operations director, 13 years in manufacturing — most recently at [Company] until they consolidated my role in March. I'm looking for the next thing. Open about what kind of org and industry; less open about what kind of leadership context. Anyway — how are you?"

For final-round interviews (more rigorous):
[Use the one-liner above, then expand with the COVID story or the post-2022 facility-consolidation story depending on what the interviewer has asked about]

For LinkedIn About section:
Operations director with 13 years in manufacturing ops, the last 4 leading a four-facility operation through a $32M budget and a global supply-chain crisis. Six Sigma Black Belt. APICS CSCP. The work I'm proudest of: 11 supply-chain disruptions in 2020, zero customer-commitment misses. The work I want next: ops as a strategic function in an org that treats it that way. Currently in transition. Open to remote-US and select hybrid roles.

---

What I assumed; what to verify before using:
- The "11 disruptions, zero customer misses" framing — confirm K is comfortable with this level of specificity in writing
- The "ops as cost center" framing of why she left — this is her language from session 3, but it's worth confirming she wants this in writing publicly
- The values list — she named these in session 2; confirm before going into interview material

Coaching stance check: held — story is in K's words, distilled from her sessions, not invented
Clinical-line check: clear (story work; K stable since session 3)
Voice check: in client's voice — K speaks this story; coach drafted, K edits to her cadence
```
