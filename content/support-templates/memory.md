# Memory — Customer Support Templates

## Domain context

A support person spends their day inside a queue. Tickets come in via email, chat, social, or sometimes phone. The platform is Zendesk, Intercom, Help Scout, Front, or for solo founders, plain Gmail. The job is: read the ticket, figure out what the customer needs, decide what you can do, write the response. Repeat 30-100 times a day.

The hard responses are the ones where you're saying no — denied refunds, "we can't add that feature," "your account was suspended." The easy ones are "shipped today, here's the tracking link." Most fall in between. Tone matters more than people think — the same content delivered cold vs. warm produces a totally different customer reaction.

Solo founders doing their own support are usually warmer than they need to be, and slower than they should be. Support teams at scale usually default to formal and end up sounding like a robot. Both are leaving relationship value on the table.

There's also a quiet rule the best support people understand: you're not just resolving a ticket, you're building (or eroding) the customer's perception of the company. A perfectly-handled refused refund can save a relationship. A poorly-handled granted refund can still lose one.

## Vocabulary the AI should know

- **Ticket**: a customer-initiated support conversation. Has a status (open, pending, solved, closed).
- **Macro**: a saved response template in Zendesk. Intercom calls them "saved replies." Help Scout calls them "saved replies" too.
- **First response time (FRT)**: how long until the customer gets *any* reply. The most-watched metric in support.
- **Resolution time**: ticket creation to "solved" status. Less honest than FRT — agents close prematurely to game it.
- **CSAT**: Customer Satisfaction. Usually a 1-5 or 1-7 survey sent after a ticket closes. Aim for 90%+ "very satisfied."
- **NPS**: Net Promoter Score. 0-10 scale. Promoters (9-10), passives (7-8), detractors (0-6). Score = %promoters - %detractors.
- **Detractor recovery**: outreach to customers who gave 0-6 NPS to find out what's wrong and try to fix it.
- **Escalation**: bumping a ticket to a senior rep, manager, or specialized team. Usually has a 24-48 hour internal SLA.
- **SLA**: Service Level Agreement. The promised response/resolution time, usually enterprise-contracted.
- **Refund window**: how many days after purchase a refund is automatically eligible. Industry norms: 14 days (SaaS), 30 days (DTC), 365 days for Costco.
- **Chargeback**: when a customer disputes a charge through their bank instead of asking the merchant. Costs the merchant a fee (~$15-25) on top of the refund.
- **Stripe Dashboard**: where most modern SaaS processes refunds. One click, money back in 5-10 days.
- **Upsell from support**: offering an upgraded plan during a support interaction. Only appropriate when the customer is happy AND the upgrade solves their actual problem.

## Common workflows

- **Process a refund request**: read the ticket → check the order date and refund policy → check the customer history (first-time? long-time? past issues?) → decide: full, partial, denied → if granted, process in Stripe/Shopify → reply with the resolution and reason → if denied, offer 2-3 alternatives.
- **Handle a lost order**: confirm the order shipped → check tracking → if "delivered" but customer says otherwise, ask for photo of the front porch → if genuinely lost, replace or refund → reach out to the carrier on the back-channel if it happens often.
- **Acknowledge an escalation**: reply within 1 hour with "I've escalated this to [name/team]" and the realistic timeline → never promise a fix you can't confirm → set the expectation for the next update time.
- **NPS detractor recovery**: see a 0-6 score → reply within 24 hours from a real person (not "thanks for your feedback!") → ask one specific question → listen → propose a fix or compensation if appropriate.
- **Upsell-from-support (rare-but-right)**: customer is happy with the resolution → their actual need is on a higher tier → mention it once, briefly, with the math → don't push.

## What to avoid / common mistakes

- "We sincerely apologize for any inconvenience this may have caused." Bottom-tier support cliché. Specific acknowledgement always beats generic apology.
- Leading with the apology, burying the resolution. Customers want to know what you're doing, then why.
- "Per our policy..." without explaining the reason. If the reason makes sense, give the reason. If it doesn't, change the policy.
- Vague timelines: "shortly," "soon," "in due course." Be specific or be silent.
- "Please feel free to reach out if you have any questions" as the closing. Replace with the actual next step or a real sign-off.
- Multi-paragraph apologies before the substance. Two sentences max on the apology side.
- Form-letter sign-offs: "the team at [Company]," "Customer Happiness Team." Use a real name.
- Mixed tone — starting formal, sliding into warm halfway. Pick one.
- Upselling when the customer is unhappy. Reads as cynical, kills the relationship.
- "I hope this email finds you well." They wrote in with a problem. Acknowledge the problem.

## Tone / register

A great support writer matches the brand voice without losing their own. They write at the customer's reading level — shorter sentences, fewer commas, plain words. They never use jargon the customer didn't use first. They are warm without being saccharine, professional without being cold, honest without being curt. They say "I" when they mean I, and "we" when they mean the company.

The internal vocabulary: tickets, macros, FRT, CSAT, escalations, chargebacks, refund window. The external vocabulary: "your order," "your account," "the issue," "what happened." Never use internal jargon in customer-facing copy.

Good support people are slightly more direct than the brand voice suggests they should be. They've learned that customers prefer a short clear "no" with a path forward over a long polite "we regret to inform you."
