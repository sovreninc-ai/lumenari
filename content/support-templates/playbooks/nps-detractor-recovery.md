# NPS Detractor Recovery

A customer just gave you a 0-6 on the NPS survey. Most companies treat this as a metric. The smart ones treat it as a 90-second window to keep a customer who was about to leave silently.

This playbook is for the outreach that happens after a detractor score lands in your queue. It's not a sales motion. It's a "what's wrong" conversation that sometimes becomes a save.

---

## The rules

1. **Reply within 24 hours.** After that, the customer has moved on and your reply reads as "we noticed you said something negative."
2. **Real person, real name.** "Customer Happiness Team" is the death of the response.
3. **No defensiveness.** They didn't ask for a debate. They asked to be heard.
4. **One specific question.** Not "tell us how we can improve." That's a survey, not a conversation.
5. **Listen when they reply.** Most of the value is in the reply, not your opening.
6. **No upsell. Ever.** Not in the opening, not in the follow-up. Detractor outreach is the wrong channel for sales motion.

---

## The opening message — three tones

The customer gave a score of 3 to a SaaS product they've used for 18 months. No comment was attached. You don't know what's wrong yet.

### Formal

> Subject: Following up on your recent feedback
>
> Hi Marcus,
>
> I saw your recent survey response, where you scored us a 3 out of 10. After 18 months as a customer, that's not the experience I'd want anyone leaving with.
>
> I'd appreciate the chance to understand what's not working. If you have a minute to share, what's the single biggest thing that would have made the last 18 months better?
>
> No pressure to reply if it's not the right time. I read every response personally.
>
> Best regards,
> Sarah Chen
> Customer Support Lead

### Friendly

> Subject: Saw your survey — what happened?
>
> Hi Marcus,
>
> Caught your NPS response — a 3 after 18 months stands out, and not in the way we'd want.
>
> If you've got a minute, what's the single biggest thing that would have made these 18 months better? Doesn't have to be polished — bullet points, one line, whatever's easy. I read everything that comes back.
>
> Sarah

### Warm

> Subject: Saw your survey — what's going on?
>
> Hi Marcus,
>
> You gave us a 3 on the survey after sticking around 18 months. That stuck with me — long-time customers don't usually leave detractor scores by accident.
>
> If you've got 30 seconds: what's the biggest thing we'd have to fix to make it not a 3? Doesn't have to be a list. One sentence is fine. I just want to know what we missed.
>
> Sarah

## What "one specific question" means

The question is calibrated. Too broad → vague answers. Too narrow → you might miss the real issue.

**Too broad** (don't):
- "How can we improve?"
- "What did you think?"
- "Any feedback you'd like to share?"

**Too narrow** (don't):
- "Was it the pricing change in March?"
- "Did the recent UI redesign frustrate you?"

**About right**:
- "What's the single biggest thing that would have made the last [N months] better?"
- "If you had to pick one thing that's holding the product back for you, what is it?"
- "What were you hoping for that you didn't get?"

## The reply — three responses to "what's wrong"

The customer wrote back. Now the conversation actually starts. Three common shapes:

### Reply pattern 1: "Pricing went up and the value didn't"

The customer is right. Don't pretend they're wrong. Validate, then talk about value alignment.

#### Friendly

> Hi Marcus,
>
> That's fair, and the pricing change wasn't pitched well to existing customers — I think you're not the first to feel that.
>
> A couple of things I can offer:
>
> - **Grandfather your old rate** for the next 12 months — we did this for some long-time customers and yours qualifies
> - **Move to a lower tier** with the features you actually use
> - **A 30-minute call** to look at how you're actually using it — sometimes the value is there but not the awareness
>
> Just tell me what would help and I'll set it up.
>
> Sarah

The save here isn't always "give them the old price." Sometimes it's just being honest that the price change felt bad and offering an off-ramp that respects them.

### Reply pattern 2: "Specific feature is broken / missing / slow"

The customer is reporting a real product issue. Three things to do, in order:

1. Confirm you heard them correctly
2. Tell them the actual status of the thing they raised (in progress / on the backlog / not happening, and why)
3. If you can route them to a workaround or beta, do it

#### Friendly

> Hi Marcus,
>
> Got it — bulk import has been slow when you're working with 5,000+ rows. You're not the only one — that's a known issue and it's actively being worked on. Realistic timeline is 4-6 weeks for the fix.
>
> In the meantime, two workarounds that have helped other customers:
>
> - Use the CSV import API endpoint instead of the UI — same data, 10× faster
> - Split your file into chunks under 2,000 rows
>
> Happy to walk you through the API approach if it's useful. I'll also flag your account to get notified the day the fix ships.
>
> Sarah

Never say "we'll let the team know." That phrase is what a chatbot says. Say "I've flagged this with the engineering team" or "this is already on the roadmap, here's the realistic timing."

### Reply pattern 3: "Your support has been terrible"

The customer is complaining about *you* (or your colleagues). Don't get defensive. Don't blame the previous agent.

#### Friendly

> Hi Marcus,
>
> Thanks for telling me straight — I'd rather hear it.
>
> I looked back at your last three tickets and I can see what you're describing — the response times stretched past where they should be and the answers were less than direct. That's on us, not you.
>
> If you're open to it, I'd like to be your direct point of contact for the next 60 days. Reply to this email with anything you need and I'll handle it personally. After 60 days, if support is back to where it should be, we go back to the normal queue.
>
> No pressure either way.
>
> Sarah

The "direct point of contact for 60 days" move is one of the highest-leverage saves in support. Costs you almost nothing. Means everything to the customer.

## When the customer doesn't reply

Most won't. That's fine. The outreach still matters.

If you want to follow up once (and only once), do it 5-7 days later:

> Hi Marcus,
>
> Just circling back in case my last note got buried. No need to reply if there's nothing to share — I just wanted you to know the door's open.
>
> Sarah

That's it. One follow-up. Then move on. Multiple "checking in" emails after a detractor score signal you're chasing the metric, not the customer.

---

## Internal — what to track when the customer DOES reply

Build a habit. Every detractor reply gets tagged:

- **Root cause category**: pricing, missing feature, broken feature, support quality, onboarding, other
- **Save outcome**: saved, churned, neutral
- **What was offered**: discount, plan change, escalation, dedicated rep, nothing

After 90 days, look at the data. The pattern almost always shows that one root cause is generating most of the detractor scores. Fix that one and your NPS moves more than any individual save.

---

## The mindset

Detractor recovery isn't a metric-management exercise. It's the rare moment when a customer tells you what's actually wrong before they walk away silently. Most never tell you at all.

A 30% reply rate is good. A 50% reply rate means the question is well-calibrated and the brand has earned the trust to be answered honestly. A 5% reply rate means the email reads like a robot wrote it.

When in doubt: be shorter, be more specific, be more human.
