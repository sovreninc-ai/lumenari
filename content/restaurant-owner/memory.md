# Memory — Restaurant Owner / GM Pack

## Domain context

An independent full-service restaurant runs on a knife edge. Food cost target sits around 28-32% of sales, labor 28-35%, rent 6-10%, and what's left after utilities, insurance, marketing, and credit card fees is the operator's pay and reinvestment. A 30-100 seat room turns 1.5-3 times on a busy night and once on a slow one. The owner is also frequently the chef, the expediter, the HR department, and the person calling the produce guy at 5 AM to chase a missing case of strawberries.

The rhythm is broken into prep, service, and after. Prep happens 9 AM to 3 PM — ordering, butchery, sauces, station setup, family meal at 4 PM. Service hits at 5 or 5:30 and runs to 10 or 11. After is breakdown, deep clean, and the owner's paperwork window — payroll, ordering, the email to the linen company, the response to the bad Yelp review. Sunday or Monday is usually dark or short, which is when menu changes, supplier reviews, and event proposals happen. None of it gets written when the operator is rested. The AI is most useful at the post-service hour, turning a string of facts into something printable, sendable, or postable.

Success looks like: a Friday night where the kitchen ran clean, the FOH didn't burn out, the table turn hit the target, comps stayed under 2%, and one regular sent a friend in for next week. Failure looks like: a 1-star review that names a server, a supplier that's now refusing to ship until last month's bill is paid, and a private dining inquiry that got lost in the inbox for nine days.

## Vocabulary the AI should know

- FOH: front of house — servers, hosts, bartenders, bussers, management on the floor
- BOH: back of house — kitchen, dish, prep, expediter
- 86: a menu item is unavailable for the rest of service ("we're 86 on the branzino")
- Comp: complimentary item or removal from check, usually owner/manager-approved
- VIP: not just celebrities — any guest the FOH should treat with extra attention (regular, industry friend, food press, ownership family)
- Cover: one diner. "We did 180 covers last night."
- Turn: how many times a table seats new guests in a service. 2x on a Saturday is healthy, 3x is great.
- POS: point of sale system (Toast, Square, Aloha, Resy, OpenTable for resos)
- Top: a table by size. "I have a 4-top open at 7:30."
- Walked: guest left without being seated (lost cover, often a wait that ran too long)
- Two-top, four-top: table for 2, table for 4
- Family meal: staff meal served before service, usually 4-4:30 PM
- Pre-shift: the FOH meeting before service where specials, 86s, VIPs, and any service notes are covered
- 86 list / weeds list / dupe: items unavailable / kitchen is overwhelmed / printed order ticket
- ServSafe: US food safety certification baseline; in Canada it's typically Food Safety BC, AHS Food Handler, or equivalent provincial certification
- Health inspector / AHJ visit: surprise inspections that grade or pass/fail the restaurant publicly
- Mise: short for mise en place — everything in its place, station prep before service starts
- Cost-out: pricing a dish by adding up the cost of each component, dividing by target food cost
- COGS: cost of goods sold
- 28-day plate cost: how often menus are repriced when ingredient costs shift seasonally
- Tip pool / tip share: how gratuities are split. Laws vary by state and province — Ontario, BC, and Alberta each have specific rules; in the US it's state-by-state and there's been a lot of federal rule churn since 2018

## Common workflows

- Weekly menu update: chef-owner sits down Sunday with the upcoming week's market sheet, plans 2-4 specials, writes copy for board or insert, sends to GM for approval. Trigger → ingredient availability → cost-out → copy → print. 1-2 hours.
- Supplier order: standing weekly orders for produce, protein, dairy, dry goods, linen. Plus daily call-ins for what was missed or 86'd. Trigger → check par sheet vs. inventory → place order → confirm delivery window → receive and check in → flag any shorts or damages.
- Review response: Yelp, Google, OpenTable, Resy notifications come in. Owner triages: 5-star gets a same-day thank-you, 3-4 star gets a "we hear you" within 24 hours, 1-2 star gets held for 4-12 hours then a careful response. Trigger → read fully → check internal facts (was that server working, did that dish actually fire, is this the guest who had the issue) → draft → sleep on it → post.
- Private dining inquiry: email comes in for a 12-40 person event (rehearsal dinner, corporate, birthday). Trigger → respond within 24 hours with availability and pricing structure → if interested, send a proposal with menus, beverage options, AV, deposit terms → contract → COBC (confirm one business day before).
- Staff scheduling change: someone calls out at 3 PM for a 5 PM service. Owner texts the group, finds coverage, updates the POS. Sometimes it's a no-show mid-service and the response is immediate triage on the floor, then a real conversation the next day.
- Pre-shift huddle: every service starts with a 5-minute meeting — what's on the special, what's 86'd, who the VIPs are tonight, any service notes (large party at 8, server in training, equipment issue).

## What to avoid / common mistakes

- "Succulent," "to-die-for," "explosion of flavor," "elevated," "curated," "passionate about food," "hand-crafted with love." This is the cliche pile. Real menu copy uses ingredients and techniques.
- Making up provenance. "Local farm" without naming the farm, "house-made bread" when you buy it from a local bakery (better: name the bakery), "organic" without certification. Health departments and food writers both punish this.
- Apologizing in writing in a way that admits fault. "We're so sorry our food made you sick" is a discoverable document. "We take food safety seriously and would like to talk about this directly" is not.
- Treating Yelp like Twitter. The owner-response is read by every prospective guest researching the restaurant. Tone is permanent.
- Allergen language that sounds safe but isn't. "Gluten-free" implies a celiac-safe environment. Most full-service kitchens cannot guarantee that. The right language is honest: "made without gluten ingredients; ask your server about cross-contact."
- Generic supplier emails. A vague complaint produces a vague credit. Cite PO #, line item, quantity, the actual issue.
- Treating staff text threads like an HR memo. Crews respond to direct, brief, named-ask messages. Not paragraphs.
- Treating dietary restrictions as marketing rather than safety. "We can accommodate" is good. "100% safe for celiacs" is a lawsuit.

## Tone / register

A real chef-owner sounds like someone who has had less sleep than you. Direct. Specific. Sentence fragments are normal. They mention ingredients and producers by name when it matters and don't when it doesn't. They use service slang when they're talking to staff and translate it for guests when they have to. They acknowledge a complaint without groveling. They thank a regular by remembering what they drank last time. They never use "guest experience," "elevated dining," or "culinary journey." They say "the room," "the menu," "the food," "the team." That's the voice.
