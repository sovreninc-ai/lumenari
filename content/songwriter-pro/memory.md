# Memory — Songwriter / Lyricist Pack

## Domain context

Songwriters work in one of several lanes — staff writers signed to publishers (mostly Nashville and LA), indie songwriters who release their own catalog, artist-songwriters writing for themselves, and sync writers focused on TV/film/ad placements. The job is producing songs at volume: a working Nashville staff writer might write 100-150 songs a year. Most of those don't get cut. The ones that do are the result of being in the right co-write room with the right artist's needs at the right time.

The work rhythm is co-write-driven. A typical day in Nashville is 11 AM-3 PM co-write, lunch in the middle, then maybe another session at 4 PM. Remote co-writes via Zoom doubled during the pandemic and stuck. A song is usually drafted in one session, demoed within a week, pitched to artists/publishers/A&R within a month. Sync writers operate differently — they write to brief, often working from a music supervisor's spec sheet for a specific scene or campaign.

Success looks like a cut (an artist records your song), which leads to a release, which leads to streams and radio plays, which leads to royalties. The biggest checks come from major-label cuts that get radio singles. Sync placements can be one-time fees ($1k-$50k) plus streaming royalties. Indie artist-songwriters build slower but own more of the catalog. Most working songwriters need 3-5 cuts in a good year to make a living from the publishing side; sync writers might need 30-50 placements.

## Vocabulary the AI should know

- **Cut**: an artist records and releases your song
- **Hold**: an artist has reserved the song but hasn't cut yet (and might never)
- **Single**: the song the label/artist is pushing to radio
- **A&R**: artist and repertoire — the people at labels who decide what gets recorded
- **Pub/Publisher**: music publisher who reps the songwriter; collects royalties, pitches songs
- **Plug**: when a publisher pitches a song to an artist or A&R
- **Demo**: recorded version of the song used to pitch
- **Master demo**: high-quality demo that could double as a release
- **Hook**: the memorable lyrical + melodic anchor that recurs (usually the chorus)
- **Title hook**: the song's title is the hook line
- **V/C/B**: verse / chorus / bridge — basic structural shorthand
- **Pre-chorus**: section between verse and chorus that builds tension into the chorus
- **Tag / outro**: the final repeated phrase or section
- **NORMS**: a song's expected structural conventions for its genre
- **Sync**: synchronization licensing — song placed in TV, film, ad, video game
- **Sync brief**: the spec sheet a music supervisor sends describing what they need
- **PRO**: performing rights organization — ASCAP, BMI, SESAC; pays public-performance royalties
- **Split sheet**: document recording each writer's % contribution to a co-write
- **Country radio format**: 3:00-3:30, hook in first minute, structurally conservative
- **Country B-side / album cut**: longer, more narrative, less radio-driven
- **Cliche rhymes**: heart/start, fire/desire, soul/whole, night/right — the rhymes that mark a lyric as generic

## Common workflows

- **Concept brainstorm (theme → angle → POV)**: User provides a theme + genre + tempo → AI produces 3-5 angles ranked by specificity, each with a POV recommendation. User picks the angle that's most theirs. Output: a starting point that isn't generic.

- **V/C/B structure prompt**: User provides angle + hook idea + genre → AI produces verse/pre-chorus/chorus/bridge structure with syllable count guidance, story arc across verses (verse 1 sets situation, verse 2 deepens or complicates), chorus emotional resolution, bridge as new angle. Output: a structural skeleton to lyric-draft against.

- **Near-rhyme generator**: User provides a target word/vowel sound + genre → AI produces 8-15 near-rhymes ranked by singability and freshness. Cliche rhymes flagged and excluded. Output: a rhyme palette that doesn't force the song into the same place every song goes.

- **Hook-building exercise**: User provides angle + tone → AI produces 5-7 candidate hook lines. Each rated by syllable count, title-worthiness, alignment with angle, cliche check. Output: a hook to build the rest of the song around.

- **Full lyric draft**: User provides angle + hook + structure + reference tracks → AI produces a full lyric draft with verse 1, pre-chorus, chorus, verse 2, chorus, bridge, final chorus. Each line flagged for singability concerns and any cliche risks. Output: a draft to take into the co-write or the studio.

- **Sync / brand pitch email**: User provides track title + tonal references + sync target → AI produces a short pitch email (subject line + 3 sentences + sign-off) suitable for music supervisor or brand team. Output: a pitch that survives a supervisor's 30-second scan.

## What to avoid / common mistakes

- **Cliche rhymes.** Heart/start, fire/desire, soul/whole, night/right, dance/chance — the forbidden list. A song that hits two or more of these is doing the work no one wants done.

- **Lyrics that scan but say nothing.** AI default. Every line technically fits the meter and rhymes properly, but you can't tell what the song is about because nothing is specific.

- **Choruses that repeat the title without earning it.** "I love you, I love you, I love you, I love you" can work if the melodic variation justifies it. Without that, it's filler.

- **Bridges that restate the verse.** A bridge introduces a new angle, a confession, a reversal, or a shift in time. If the bridge is just verse content with different words, the song wasted a section.

- **Generic relationship songs.** A song so vague it could be about anyone leaving anyone — it could be a karaoke standard but it won't get cut by a specific artist who needs a specific song.

- **AI-tell rhyme positions.** "Embrace," "in this moment," "feel alive," "set me free," "let it go," "find my way" — these aren't just tired phrases, they're the rhyme positions AI defaults to. Replace with specifics.

- **Lyrics that don't sing.** Too many hard consonants in a row ("the cracked picture frame split sharply"), vowels that close off the high notes (long-u, short-i in belt positions). AI doesn't hear this. The writer has to.

- **Forced perfect rhymes.** Stretching to hit a perfect rhyme often produces an awkward word choice. Near-rhymes are usually better than awkward perfect rhymes.

## Tone / register

Real songwriters sound like people who hear music while they're talking about lyrics. They reference cuts and writers ("Lori McKenna writes the kind of verse I want to write here"), talk in terms of feel ("we want a 6/8 thing, like Sleeping at Last but more pissed"), and use specific structural language ("the post-chorus needs an ear-worm — Charlie Puth ear-worm, not Bieber ear-worm"). They know the difference between writing FOR an artist (specific, fits their range, fits their story) and writing TO an artist (generic — you're hoping it'll land somewhere). They use plain language for emotional content and technical language for structural content. They're often funny about how brutal the rejection rate is. When they like a lyric, they say specifically: "the line about her finishing his sentences — that's the hook for me." When they don't, they say what they'd cut.
