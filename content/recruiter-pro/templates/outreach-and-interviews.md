# Outreach and Interviews

Three parts: outreach templates by seniority and role family, interview question banks (screening + behavioral + technical), and the warm-but-clear rejection email copy that protects your reputation.

---

## Part 1 — Outreach by seniority

A Junior responds to different signals than a Staff Engineer. The mistake most recruiters make is sending the same template to everyone.

### Universal rules

- Three lines maximum in the opener. Senior people close DMs in 4 seconds.
- Lead with WHY YOU SPECIFICALLY. If you can't name a real reason, you don't have one.
- Include the comp band. Hiding it screams "we underpay."
- One clear ask. "15 minutes next week" beats "would love to connect."
- No "I came across your profile and was impressed." Banned phrase.

### Entry / Junior outreach

Tone: encouraging, clear, growth-focused. They're often nervous about whether they qualify.

```
Subject: Junior Engineer role at [Company] — would you take a look?

Hi [Name],

I'm hiring junior engineers at [Company] — we make [one-line product]. Saw your project on [specific thing], and the way you wrote up [specific detail] is the level of clarity we want on the team.

Role pays USD $90-110K base + equity, fully remote in US/Canada. Mentorship from two senior engineers; first 6 months is mostly pairing.

If you're open to chatting, here's the calendar: [link]. Otherwise, no worries — just glad you're shipping.

— [You]
```

What works: specificity on the project, comp band stated, mentorship signal, light close that doesn't pressure.

### Mid outreach

Tone: professional, role-specific, comp-honest. They want to know if this is a real step or a lateral.

```
Subject: Mid Frontend Engineer at [Company], $130-150K + equity

Hi [Name],

Reaching out about a Mid Frontend Engineer role at [Company]. You've been at [current employer] for 2 years on [specific product/team], and the role I'm hiring for is similar scope with two differences worth a look: [difference 1, difference 2 — could be team size, technology, ownership area].

USD $130-150K base + 0.02-0.05% equity, fully remote. Hybrid 2x/week if you're in [city].

Want 15 mins next week? Calendar: [link].

— [You]
```

What works: names where they currently work and for how long; the "two differences worth a look" makes it specific; band stated.

### Senior outreach

Tone: peer-to-peer. They've seen 100 recruiter messages this month. Don't be number 101.

```
Subject: Senior Backend role, $180-220K, working on [specific problem]

Hi [Name],

I'm hiring a senior backend engineer at [Company]. The work is [specific problem statement — what the team is actually building], and your write-up of [their blog post / talk / OSS contribution] is the closest thing to the right mental model I've found.

USD $180-220K base + 0.05-0.15% equity. Remote-first US/Canada. The team is [headcount + 1-line description].

15 mins next week if you're open: [calendar]. Even if not, I'd be curious what you think of [specific technical thing] — happy to chat without a job in the mix.

— [You]
```

What works: the closing offer to chat without a job. Senior engineers respect recruiters who treat them as peers, not pipeline.

### Staff / Principal outreach

Tone: strategic, problem-focused, comp-honest about the ceiling. They're rarely actively looking. The opening has to do real work.

```
Subject: Staff Engineer role at [Company] — interested in your take

Hi [Name],

I'm working with the engineering leadership at [Company] on a Staff Engineer role. The reason I'm writing specifically: you've worked through the [specific technical problem — e.g., "monolith-to-services migration at a similar scale," "real-time sync layer for collaborative editing"] and that's exactly the problem on the table here.

The role is comp'd USD $280-340K base + meaningful equity (0.3-0.7%). Remote-first. Direct reporting line to the CTO; one of three staff engineers on a team of 35.

If the problem space is interesting, I'd love 30 mins. If not — could I send you the JD and ask if you'd recommend anyone in your network? Calendar: [link].

— [You]
```

What works: names the technical problem. Names comp with a real range. Offers a graceful out (referral instead of interview).

## Part 2 — Outreach by role family

### Engineering / Technical

See above. Engineering candidates respond to:
- Technical specificity (the problem, the stack, the scale)
- Engineering culture signals (review process, deploy frequency, on-call expectations)
- Honest comp bands
- Concise, peer-toned messages

Don't lead with: "exciting opportunity," "passionate team," your funding round, your investor names.

### Design (Product / Brand)

Designers want to know who they'll work with and how decisions get made. They've been burned by "fast-paced startups" where design is decoration.

```
Subject: Senior Product Designer at [Company] — design has a seat at the table

Hi [Name],

I'm hiring a Senior Product Designer at [Company]. Saw your work on [specific project] — the way you handled [specific decision] is the kind of thinking we need.

A few things that might matter to you:
- Design reports to the CEO, not engineering
- Two product designers and one design researcher already on the team
- Real research budget; we run usability tests every 2 weeks
- USD $170-200K base + equity, remote in US/Canada

If you're open to a 20-min chat, here's my calendar: [link].

— [You]
```

What works: signals that design isn't an afterthought. Names the team structure. States real research budget.

### Sales (AE, SDR, Sales Leadership)

Sales candidates want to know the comp plan, the territory, and the product they'll sell.

```
Subject: Senior AE at [Company] — selling to [vertical], $250K+ OTE

Hi [Name],

I'm hiring a Senior AE at [Company]. We sell [product] to [vertical/buyer], deal sizes USD $30-150K ACV. You've been at [current company] for [time] selling [similar/adjacent] — the territory I'm hiring for is open and uncapped.

Comp: USD $130K base + $130K variable, 50/50 split, accelerators above quota. Quota is $1.1M annual based on AE attainment data over the last 18 months.

15 mins next week? Calendar: [link].

— [You]
```

What works: comp structure transparent. Quota stated with attainment context. Names territory and ICP.

### Operations / G&A (Finance, People, Legal)

Ops candidates want to know what they'll actually OWN. They've been burned by "build everything from scratch" with no support.

```
Subject: People Ops Manager at [Company] — what you'd actually own

Hi [Name],

I'm hiring a People Ops Manager at [Company], 60-person Series B SaaS. The role is genuinely "build the People function from a real but incomplete starting point" — we have an HRIS (Rippling), basic policies, and a culture worth building on, but no dedicated People person yet.

Your 4 years at [current company] putting in the systems before scaling to 200 is exactly the muscle this role needs.

USD $140-170K base + equity, remote with quarterly in-person leadership offsites.

Open to a 20-min chat? [Calendar]

— [You]
```

What works: honest about the starting state. Maps their experience to the actual need.

## Part 3 — Interview kits

### Screening (15-20 min, recruiter-led)

Goal: confirm baseline fit, gauge interest, check comp expectations. Never make a screen longer than 20 minutes.

| Question | What good looks like | Red flag |
|---|---|---|
| Walk me through your current role in 3 sentences. | Concise, names the SCOPE not just the title. | Rambling, defensive, blames last employer. |
| What's drawing you to look at this role specifically? | A real reason tied to the JD or the team. | "Open to anything." "Just looking." |
| What are you targeting for comp? | A direct number or range, not coy. | "Whatever's competitive" / refusal to engage. |
| When could you realistically start? | A clear answer with notice period factored. | Vague ("a few months"). |
| Any reason this WOULDN'T be a fit for you? | Honest about a concern; engaged enough to ask. | "No reason at all!" — usually means they haven't read the JD. |

### Behavioral (45-60 min, hiring manager + peer)

STAR-friendly: Situation, Task, Action, Result. Listen for specifics. Drill on the ACTION step, not the situation.

#### Universal behavioral

```
Q: Tell me about a project where the scope or goal changed mid-stream. What did you do?

What good looks like:
- Names a specific project, real numbers/dates
- Describes the trigger for the change (data, stakeholder, market)
- Explains their decision process, not just their conclusion
- Acknowledges what they got wrong or had to redo

Red flags:
- Generic, no specifics
- Blames the changer entirely (no self-reflection)
- "Everything went smoothly" — projects don't go smoothly; that's a tell
```

```
Q: Walk me through a time you disagreed with a peer or your manager. How did it resolve?

What good looks like:
- Real conflict, not "we had different opinions"
- They engaged directly (not went around the person)
- Outcome is mature: resolved or agreed to disagree productively

Red flags:
- "I always agree with my manager"
- "I just did what they said"
- Vendetta-tone — they're still angry about it
```

```
Q: Describe a time you took on something well outside your scope.

What good looks like:
- Specific instance with a real outcome
- They sought permission or alignment (not heroics)
- They went back to their lane afterward (not "now I run that area too")

Red flags:
- "I'm always taking on extra work"
- Heroics narrative — the place was on fire and they alone saved it
```

#### Engineering-specific behavioral

```
Q: Tell me about a production incident you owned. What happened, what did you do, and what did you change after?

What good looks like:
- Specific incident, technical depth in the description
- Describes the IR process (paged, triaged, fixed, root caused)
- Post-mortem changes — what they shipped to prevent recurrence

Red flags:
- "I've never had an incident" (you have; you weren't on call or you weren't notified — neither is a great sign for a senior)
- Blames infra/another team entirely
- No post-mortem learning
```

#### Sales-specific behavioral

```
Q: Walk me through a deal you lost that you should have won. What did you miss?

What good looks like:
- Specific deal, specific reason
- Names what they'd do differently
- Doesn't blame the prospect

Red flags:
- "We didn't lose deals we should have won"
- Blames marketing/product for lost deals only
- Refuses to engage with the loss
```

#### Design-specific behavioral

```
Q: Tell me about a design decision you defended and a design decision you rolled back.

What good looks like:
- Both have real receipts (research, data, customer interviews)
- They distinguish between "I was right and stood ground" and "I was wrong and changed course"
- Comfortable with both as outcomes

Red flags:
- Can only name decisions they defended ("never wrong")
- Decisions feel taste-based, not data-informed
```

### Technical / role-specific (60-90 min)

#### Senior Full-Stack Engineer (sample)

Probe areas (45-60 min total across these):

1. **Code review** — share a 200-line PR (real one, anonymized), ask them to review it out loud. Listen for: what they catch, what they miss, how they communicate concerns.
2. **System design** — "Design a feature that lets users tag any object in our app with arbitrary labels and search across them." Listen for: schema design, indexing thinking, caching, scale tradeoffs.
3. **Debugging** — present a real failure ("after our 2am deploy, users reported X. Here are the logs/metrics. Walk me through how you'd investigate."). Listen for: hypothesis generation, what they'd check first, how they prioritize.
4. **Scope a project** — "We want to add SSO to our app. PM has given you one paragraph. Talk me through how you'd scope this." Listen for: questions they ask, how they think about phasing, risks they surface.

NEVER use: take-home assignments over 2 hours, LeetCode for senior engineers, brainteasers, "why are manhole covers round."

#### Senior Account Executive (sample)

1. **Pitch the product** — give them a 2-page product brief and 15 min prep, then ask them to pitch you (the buyer persona). Listen for: discovery vs. pitch ratio, how they handle objections.
2. **Mutual close plan walkthrough** — show them a deal scenario, ask how they'd build the mutual close plan. Listen for: stakeholder mapping, deal tempo, risk surfacing.
3. **Pipeline math** — "If your quota is $X and your average deal is $Y, how do you plan the quarter?" Listen for: math literacy, conversion-rate awareness, activity-to-pipeline thinking.

#### Senior Product Designer (sample)

1. **Portfolio walkthrough** — 30 min. Pick ONE project. Listen for: decision-making process, research integration, what they'd change.
2. **Live critique** — show them a real Figma file, ask them to critique. Listen for: hierarchy thinking, accessibility awareness, how they give feedback.
3. **Whiteboard exercise** — "Design an onboarding for [our app]. You have 30 min." Listen for: questions they ask, sketches, why they made each choice.

## Part 4 — Reference-check questions

5 questions max. Calibration, not interrogation.

```
1. How did you and [candidate] work together, and for how long?

2. What was [candidate] hired to do, and how did that role change over time? (This reveals scope and growth.)

3. Tell me about their single biggest contribution while you worked together. What made it work? (Listen for specifics — vague answers here are a soft signal.)

4. If [candidate] joined a team like [target team description], where would they need the most support in the first 6 months?

5. Would you hire them again? If yes, same role, more senior role, or different role?
```

What you DO NOT ask:

- "Were there any issues we should know about?" (invites bias, vague answers)
- "How would you describe their personality?" (low signal, high bias)
- "Are they a hard worker?" (Yes, they'll say yes.)
- Anything that probes protected categories

## Part 5 — Rejection emails

Three tiers based on how far they got.

### Tier 1 — Resume only, no interview

```
Subject: Re: [Role title] application

Hi [Name],

Thanks for your application to the [Role] role at [Company]. We've gone through this round, and we're moving forward with other candidates whose background was closer to the specific needs of this requisition.

I'd encourage you to apply again for future roles — we hire continuously, and your profile is a fit for the function generally. I'll keep an eye out for relevant roles and reach out if I see one.

Thanks for the time you put into applying.

— [You]
```

### Tier 2 — One interview, didn't move forward

```
Subject: Update on the [Role] interview process

Hi [Name],

Thanks for the time on our call last week. I wanted to follow up directly rather than leave you waiting.

We've decided not to move forward to the next round. The specific thing that came up in the conversation was [ONE concrete reason — e.g., "the experience scale we need (B2B at $50M+ ARR) is just deeper than your last two roles have given you opportunity to hit"]. That's not a knock on the work you've done; it's a fit-to-this-role thing.

A few things that stood out positively: [genuine compliment tied to something specific from the conversation]. If a different role opens up at [Company] that fits your profile better, I'd like to come back to you — okay if I do?

Thanks for the time.

— [You]
```

What works: ONE specific reason, tied to the conversation. Genuine positive note. Door left open with permission.

### Tier 3 — Final round, didn't get the offer

```
Subject: Following up on the final round

Hi [Name],

I want to start by saying I appreciate the time you put into this process. Four conversations is real time, and I know you took it seriously.

We've made an offer to another candidate. I want to be direct about why, because you deserve more than a non-answer:

The deciding factor was [genuine reason — usually relative strength of another candidate on a specific dimension that matters most for the role]. It was a close call, and a few of the panel feedback notes specifically named [genuine positive thing about you].

If it would be useful, I'm happy to:
- Pass on the full panel feedback (with permission of the interviewers)
- Refer you to [Person at another company / role] who I know is hiring for a similar profile
- Stay in touch for future roles at [Company]

Either way, thanks for trusting me with this process.

— [You]

P.S. [Personal sign-off about something real from the conversations.]
```

What works: direct about the reason, genuine, offers actionable follow-up. Most candidates who get this email become a referral source later — not despite the rejection, because of how it was handled.

### What you never write in a rejection

- "We've decided to go in a different direction." (Non-answer.)
- "It's just not a fit." (Vague.)
- "We've decided to pause the role." (Lying. They'll find out.)
- "We'll keep your resume on file." (Nobody keeps anything on file.)
- "Best of luck in your future endeavors." (Form-letter signal.)
