# Memory — LinkedIn Content Creator Pack

## Domain context

B2B operators, founders, and consultants use LinkedIn as the primary top-of-funnel for warm pipeline. Unlike consumer creators, the math is different: 50 of the right viewers can produce a $50k engagement; 50,000 of the wrong viewers produce nothing. The user is not trying to become a LinkedIn celebrity. They're trying to be the first person a specific kind of buyer thinks of when a specific kind of problem comes up.

The work rhythm is irregular. Posts often get drafted on Sundays for the week, batched 3-5 at a time, then scheduled or posted manually as the week unfolds. Comment engagement on other people's posts happens in 10-15 minute windows — between meetings, before standup, end of day. DMs that come from posts get handled the same day if at all possible.

Success looks like 1-3 high-intent inbound DMs per week from people in the target buyer profile, plus a steady increase in the right kind of follower (not raw count). Vanity metrics — likes, impressions, follower count — are misleading. The right metric is "did anyone who could become a customer reach out this week."

## Vocabulary the AI should know

- **ICP**: ideal customer profile — the specific buyer the user is targeting (e.g., "Series A SaaS founders selling to mid-market")
- **Hook**: the first 1-2 lines of a post, visible before the "see more" cutoff; determines whether anyone reads the rest
- **Dwell time**: how long readers spend on a post — LinkedIn's primary ranking signal post-2023
- **Broetry**: the LinkedIn style where every sentence is its own line, stacked vertically; widely mocked but still used
- **Carousel**: multi-slide PDF post format; high reach in 2022-2024, declining in 2025 unless substance is real
- **Selfie post**: post with the user's photo, often face + arms-crossed or face + whiteboard; reach is high, conversion is mixed
- **Engagement bait**: low-effort post asking for likes/comments ("Agree?" "What would you add?"), now algorithmically punished
- **POD**: engagement pod — group of accounts that auto-engage with each other's posts; bannable, easy to spot
- **Lead magnet**: free resource (PDF, template, checklist) offered in exchange for a comment or DM
- **Inbound DM**: unsolicited message from a viewer of your content, the highest-quality pipeline signal on LinkedIn
- **Creator mode**: LinkedIn account setting that shifts the profile toward content discovery (vs. networking)
- **Top-of-funnel**: posts designed for reach and brand awareness, not direct conversion
- **Bottom-of-funnel**: posts designed for buyers actively in-market; specific, technical, sometimes long
- **Sales Navigator**: LinkedIn's paid prospecting tool; relevant for follow-up after a post lands
- **Newsletter**: LinkedIn's native subscription product; different from posts, higher commitment from readers

## Common workflows

- **Weekly content plan (3 pillars)**: User shares a domain + 3 content pillars (e.g., "fractional CFO work — hiring decisions, board prep, runway") → AI generates 5 post angles per pillar (15 candidates) → user picks 3-5 to draft this week → AI drafts each in selected style. Output: a week of queued content in 30 minutes.

- **Single-post draft from a bullet**: User pastes 3-5 bullets in their own rough words ("we lost the deal because procurement asked for SOC 2 in week 3 — pricing meant nothing once that came up") → AI drafts the post in one of three styles (authority, visibility, pipeline) → user picks the best draft and tightens it. Output: one ready-to-post draft in 5 minutes.

- **Comment-on-others framework**: User pastes a post they want to comment on + their ICP + relationship goal ("warm them up — not pitching yet") → AI generates 3-5 candidate comments ranked by substance, with one flagged as "don't post — sounds like pitch" if applicable → user picks one, edits to their voice. Output: relationship-building comments that don't smell like outreach.

- **DM follow-up from a post engagement**: User pastes the post they shared + the specific comment or share that prompted the DM + what they want to offer → AI drafts a 4-line DM that references the specific exchange, adds one line of substance, and makes one clear offer. Output: a DM that doesn't read like a sequence.

- **Repurpose a long-form piece**: User pastes a blog post, newsletter, or call transcript → AI extracts 3-5 LinkedIn post angles with different hooks → user picks the strongest one to develop. Output: turning one piece of substance into a week of posts.

## What to avoid / common mistakes

- **Writing for other creators, not buyers.** A post that gets 800 likes from LinkedIn creators teaching LinkedIn is a failure if the user sells to logistics directors. The right post might get 40 likes — but three of them might DM.

- **Hook-stacking that doesn't deliver.** Hooks like "I lost $250k last week and here's what I learned" are bad if the post then pivots into generic advice. The hook has to actually match the substance of the post.

- **Fake intimacy.** "Storytime: I cried in my Tesla yesterday" — every operator can smell this. If a real moment is part of the post, it's specific, brief, and serves the point. If it's a manufactured opener, cut it.

- **Generic case studies.** "I worked with a Series A founder who..." → no specifics, no numbers, no names of decisions. Real case studies have at least one piece of evidence the AI couldn't have invented.

- **Overuse of one-liner paragraphs.** Broetry stacking signals "I read a LinkedIn growth Twitter thread." Use it for emphasis, not for every post.

- **CTAs that ask for engagement.** "What would you add?" "Agree?" "Comment your take." These produce shallow engagement and are now ranked down. End with a thought, not a question, unless you genuinely want responses on a specific angle.

## Tone / register

Real B2B operators sound like people who have skin in the game. They reference specific deals, specific numbers, specific decisions. They use jargon when jargon is faster (CAC, ICP, ACV) and explain it when they're talking past the buyer. They have opinions and they say them — but they say them as observations, not as performances. They don't humble-brag. They don't manufacture vulnerability. They're often a little dry, sometimes funny in a deadpan way, and they assume the reader is smart enough to follow without hand-holding. When they're wrong, they say so flatly and move on. The voice is closer to a Slack DM with a smart peer than a TED talk.
