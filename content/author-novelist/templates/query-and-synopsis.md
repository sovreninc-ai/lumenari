# Query Letter and 1-Page Synopsis Templates

The two documents agents request. Most queries get rejected in 30 seconds; the synopsis gets read when the query earns it. Both have specific conventions. Both reward specificity and punish summary.

---

## Part 1: The query letter

### Structure (250-400 words, single page when printed)

**1. The hook (1-3 sentences)**

The specific element that makes this book this book. Not a genre tag, not a summary, not a thematic gesture. The thing that makes an agent stop scrolling.

Examples of hooks that work:
- "When a forensic accountant discovers her dead husband had a second wife, she has 48 hours to choose between the truth and the inheritance both women need." (specific situation + stakes + concrete choice)
- "On the morning her husband's funeral begins, FBI cryptanalyst Maren Cole receives an encrypted message in his handwriting — sent that morning." (named character + impossible event + immediate question)

Examples of hooks that fail (do not produce):
- "A young woman discovers she's the chosen one in a world full of magic." (generic, could be any fantasy)
- "When tragedy strikes, [protagonist] must learn to love again." (no specifics)
- "In a world where..." (worldbuilding opener — show, don't tell us the world)

**2. The pitch paragraph (150-200 words)**

Introduce protagonist, situation, inciting incident, stakes, and a hint of the twist or choice the book turns on. Do NOT reveal the ending. End with the central choice or stakes line — but as a stakes line, not a rhetorical question.

The structure inside the paragraph:
- Sentence 1: Protagonist + situation when the book opens
- Sentence 2-3: The inciting incident — the thing that makes today different
- Sentence 4-5: What's at risk and what the protagonist has to decide
- Sentence 6 (optional): A hint of the twist or the harder choice underneath the obvious one
- Final sentence: The stakes, framed as a choice the protagonist faces

What to cut: backstory, worldbuilding, secondary characters who aren't the antagonist or love interest.

**3. The bio (1-3 sentences)**

Relevant credentials only:
- Previous publications (small press counts; literary magazines count for literary writers)
- MFA if you have one — name the program
- Day job IF it informs the book ("I worked as a federal prosecutor for eight years; this book draws on three real cases")
- Why you wrote this specific book (sparingly — only if it adds credibility)

What to cut:
- "I've been writing since I was six"
- "I live with my husband, two children, and a golden retriever"
- "This is my first novel" (the agent assumes this unless told otherwise)
- Unrelated credentials ("PhD in chemistry" if the book is a contemporary romance)

**4. The housekeeping (1 line)**

Single line, end of letter:

> "THE [TITLE] is a [word count]-word [genre] novel that will appeal to readers of [Comp Title 1] and [Comp Title 2]."

Or for upmarket / literary:

> "[TITLE] is a [word count]-word [genre] novel in the tradition of [Comp 1] and [Comp 2]."

Rules:
- Title in caps or italics (each agency has slight preferences — caps is standard)
- Word count rounded to nearest 1,000
- Genre named precisely (not "thriller" but "domestic suspense," not "fantasy" but "epic fantasy")
- 2-3 comps, ALL from the last 5 years, ALL midlist or breakout (not mega-bestseller)
- Comps should match category (adult comps for adult, YA for YA)

### Worked example: Upmarket domestic suspense

**Subject: Query — THE OTHER WIFE — Upmarket Domestic Suspense**

Dear [Agent Name],

When forensic accountant Maren Cole opens her dead husband's safe deposit box, she expects insurance papers. She finds a second marriage license — and the woman on it is still very much alive.

Maren's husband Daniel died in a single-car crash on a stretch of I-94 he had no reason to be driving. The insurance payout is enough to save the house and keep her two kids in their schools. But the woman on the second license — Sarah, a hospice nurse in Madison — has the same payout coming. They cannot both inherit, and the policy was issued the week before Daniel died.

Maren has forty-eight hours to file her claim, contest Sarah's, or walk away from money she desperately needs. She drives to Madison expecting to find a stranger. She finds someone who knew Daniel longer than she did, and who has her own theory about the crash. The two women begin to compare the lives they thought they'd been living — and discover the only person who can confirm or deny what really happened that night is the one person who isn't there to ask.

I'm a CPA with twelve years in forensic accounting, including four years as an expert witness in fraud cases. THE OTHER WIFE draws on the legal mechanics of contested inheritance and the way two competing claims actually unfold in real life. My short fiction has appeared in Glimmer Train and Ploughshares.

THE OTHER WIFE is a 92,000-word upmarket domestic suspense in the tradition of Lisa Jewell's THE FAMILY UPSTAIRS and Catherine Steadman's MR. NOBODY. Thank you for your time and consideration.

Best,
[Writer name]
[Email]
[Optional: website / Twitter]

## Part 2: The 1-page synopsis

### Structure (~500 words on a single page, single-spaced)

The synopsis is a complete plot summary. It exists so the agent can verify the writer can resolve the story. The ending goes on the page. No cliffhangers.

**Conventions:**
- Present tense (regardless of manuscript POV)
- Third person (regardless of manuscript POV)
- POV character names and antagonist in CAPS at first mention only
- Single page single-spaced if possible; 2 pages OK if agency requests
- No subheadings — flow it as continuous narrative
- All major plot turns named: inciting incident, midpoint, climax, resolution

**Shape inside the page:**
- Paragraph 1: Setup — protagonist, world, want, the situation when the book opens
- Paragraph 2: Inciting incident and first plot point
- Paragraph 3: Rising action, complications, midpoint
- Paragraph 4: All is lost, dark night, breakthrough
- Paragraph 5: Climax and resolution — including who the antagonist is, what they wanted, and how it ends

### Worked example: 1-page synopsis of THE OTHER WIFE

MAREN COLE, a Minneapolis forensic accountant, is widowed at thirty-six when her husband DANIEL dies in a single-car crash on I-94. The insurance payout — $850,000 — is enough to keep their two children in their private school and the house she grew up in. Three days after the funeral, Maren opens Daniel's safe deposit box and finds a marriage license dated four years before their own wedding. The bride is SARAH WELLS, a hospice nurse in Madison, Wisconsin. Sarah is alive.

Maren drives to Madison expecting to confront a fraud. Instead she finds Sarah's life sitting on the dining room table — the same handwriting on the same kind of grocery lists, photos of Daniel at Sarah's mother's birthday last spring. Sarah is also widowed. She believes Daniel divorced her after the affair he confessed to ten years ago. The divorce papers are notarized but never filed. Both marriages are real. Both payouts have been issued. Sarah has forty-eight hours to contest, and so does Maren.

In the days that follow, the two women begin to compare the chronology of Daniel's two lives. He was a sales rep with a "regional" territory; the territory turns out to be a story he told both wives to cover four-day stretches in the other woman's bed. Maren learns Daniel had been ill for months — a diagnosis he never shared. Sarah learns Daniel had been emptying their joint account into a third account neither wife knew existed.

The third account is the midpoint discovery. It contains $340,000 and is in the name of a woman named JESSICA PARK — Daniel's college girlfriend, now a divorce attorney in Chicago. Jessica isn't a third wife. She's the executor of Daniel's actual will, the one neither of his wives knew he'd written six weeks before his death. He had ALS, undiagnosed long enough to mask, terminal within a year. He'd been arranging his exit.

The crash wasn't an accident. Daniel drove off I-94 deliberately, into a stretch he'd scouted, at a speed designed to end it cleanly. The third account was meant for both women, divided by the will Jessica was holding. He couldn't tell either of them. He could only leave them the choice he'd never been able to make.

Maren and Sarah agree to honor the will. They split the third account and the insurance — each takes half, by mutual signed agreement, in defiance of the policy's exclusivity. The fraud, they decide, was Daniel's, not theirs. In the final scene, Maren returns to Minneapolis. She tells her children their father loved them, and that he was a more complicated man than she had known. She does not tell them about Sarah. Not yet.

## Part 3: Housekeeping checklist before sending

Before sending any query, run through:

- [ ] Personalized greeting (agent name, correctly spelled)
- [ ] Hook is specific, not a genre tag or thematic gesture
- [ ] Pitch paragraph names protagonist, situation, stakes, and a choice
- [ ] Pitch paragraph does NOT reveal the ending
- [ ] Bio is relevant to this book (not "I've been writing since I was six")
- [ ] Title in caps, word count rounded to nearest 1,000
- [ ] Genre named precisely
- [ ] Comps: 2-3, all from the last 5 years, all midlist or breakout, all in the same category
- [ ] No "Will [protagonist] discover the truth before it's too late?"
- [ ] No "embark on a journey"
- [ ] Synopsis: present tense, third person, ending on the page
- [ ] Sample pages: agency's exact requested format (5 pages / 10 pages / 50 pages — read the submission guidelines, not your friend's)
- [ ] One agent at a time per query email (no BCC to ten agents)

If anything fails the checklist, fix it. Querying agents see thousands of queries — a clean one moves you from the 90% pile to the 10% pile.
