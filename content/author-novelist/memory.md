# Memory — Author / Novelist Pack

## Domain context

Fiction writers work on a manuscript over months or years, often in genres with very specific conventions (mystery's fair-play rule, romance's HEA/HFN guarantee, SFF's worldbuilding load). They split their time between drafting (generating new pages), revising (cutting, restructuring, sharpening), and — for traditionally published writers — submitting query letters and synopses to literary agents, then to editors at acquiring houses. Self-publishing writers spend that submission time on cover design, blurbs, KDP setup, and launch planning instead.

Most writers have a day job. Drafting happens early morning (5-7 AM), late at night (9-11 PM), or in weekend sprints. The work is solitary and the feedback loop is long — agents respond in 4-12 weeks if they respond at all; editors take longer. Most writers are also in critique groups or work with beta readers.

Success looks like different things at different stages. For querying writers: full manuscript requests from agents (the "full request"), then an offer of representation, then a book deal. For self-publishing writers: copies sold, reviews, a series that builds an audience. For literary writers, a strong debut might be 5,000 hardcover copies sold and rave reviews; for commercial genre writers (romance, thriller), success is bigger numbers and series velocity.

## Vocabulary the AI should know

- **Comp titles (comps)**: comparable recent books that signal what category, audience, and style this book fits; industry standard is 2-3, all from the last 5 years
- **Query letter**: 1-page pitch sent to literary agents to request a full manuscript read
- **Synopsis**: 1-2 page summary of the entire plot, including the ending — a separate document from the query
- **Pages**: the first 5/10/50 pages of the manuscript, often sent with the query
- **Full request / partial request**: agent requesting the full manuscript or the first 50-100 pages after reading the query
- **R&R**: revise and resubmit — an agent's request for revisions before deciding on representation
- **Offer of rep**: literary agent's formal offer to represent the writer
- **On submission**: when an agented manuscript is being read by editors at publishing houses
- **MS or ms**: manuscript
- **POV**: point of view — first person, third limited, third omniscient, second person
- **Beat sheet**: structural outline mapping scenes to story beats (Save the Cat, three-act, etc.)
- **HEA / HFN**: Happily Ever After / Happy For Now — required ending types in romance subgenres
- **MFA**: Master of Fine Arts — graduate writing degree, often cited in bios
- **Pitch contest**: structured online event (PitMad, PitchWars, etc.) where writers pitch to agents
- **Wound / Lie / Truth**: character arc framework — the past hurt, the false belief, the eventual reckoning
- **Save the Cat**: Blake Snyder's 15-beat structural framework, widely used in commercial fiction

## Common workflows

- **Character bible (one page per major character)**: User provides name, role, premise → AI produces a one-page bible with situation/want/need/wound/lie/truth/voice notes/contradiction/physical anchor/backstory beats. Output: a character document the writer can consult while drafting to keep voice and arc consistent.

- **Three-act or Save the Cat beat sheet**: User provides premise + protagonist + genre → AI produces a 15-beat (Save the Cat) or 9-beat (three-act with pinch points) outline mapped to the specific story. Each beat names what happens, what the protagonist understands or doesn't, and the promise to the reader. Output: a structural skeleton to draft against or to diagnose a stuck draft.

- **Query letter draft**: User provides premise + protagonist + stakes + genre + word count + comps → AI produces a query letter with hook, pitch paragraph, bio, and housekeeping line. Output: a draft to revise into the writer's voice and test on the agent who reps similar books.

- **1-page synopsis**: User provides query letter + complete plot in bullets → AI produces a 1-page synopsis (~500 words, present tense, third person, POV names in CAPS first appearance, ending fully revealed). Output: the document agents request alongside the query.

- **Character arc audit**: User pastes draft of a chapter or scene → AI returns notes on whether the protagonist's want/need/wound/lie/truth is showing up on the page, with specific revision suggestions (not "show don't tell"). Output: targeted revision notes that connect craft to arc.

- **Comp title brainstorm**: User provides premise + tone + audience → AI suggests 5-8 candidate comp titles from the last 5 years, with one-line rationale for each. User picks 2-3 they've read and that legitimately compare. Output: a starting list — the writer confirms by actually reading.

## What to avoid / common mistakes

- **Pitching by summarizing.** A query that walks chronologically through the plot has lost. The hook is the specific element that makes this book different; the pitch paragraph sets up the central choice or stakes without revealing how they resolve.

- **Mega-bestseller comps.** "Like Harry Potter for adults," "Gone Girl meets Where the Crawdads Sing." Agents see thousands of queries with these comps. Comps should be midlist or breakout books in the same category, from the last 5 years, that the writer has actually read.

- **Synopses that hide the ending.** A synopsis exists so the agent can verify the writer can resolve the story. Cliffhanger synopses get auto-rejected. The ending goes on the page.

- **"Show don't tell" as feedback.** It's a slogan, not a revision. Every "show don't tell" note can be replaced with a specific instruction: "The grief is in the narration; try giving her three small physical actions that signal it instead."

- **AI-generated final prose without flagging.** If the writer asks for a draft of a scene, the AI produces it but flags that this is generative material to be heavily revised in the writer's voice. Default deliverables are structure and notes, not finished prose.

- **Soft antagonists.** AI defaults to making villains misunderstood. If the writer's antagonist is cruel, AI should write them cruel. If the antagonist murders someone, the verb is "murders," not "is responsible for the death of."

## Tone / register

Real novelists sound like people who read widely and have opinions. They use the precise word over the showy word. They distinguish between literary terms ("close third," "free indirect discourse") and craft notes ("the scene is sitting in his head — get him into the room"). They reference books and authors specifically — "her sentences move like Lauren Groff's" rather than "lyrical prose." They're honest about what's working and what isn't, including in their own drafts. When they like something, they say so specifically: "that turn on page 84 — I didn't see it coming and now I can't unsee it." When they don't, they say what they'd cut. They don't use "narrative arc," "compelling characters," "page-turner" — those are jacket-copy words. They use actual descriptions of what the prose is doing.
