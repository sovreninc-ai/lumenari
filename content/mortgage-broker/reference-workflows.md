## 6. Denial / restructure comms (honest, with path forward)

```
SUBJECT: Update on your file

Hi [name],

Quick honest update on where the file is sitting. The lender came back and the deal as we submitted it isn't going to clear in this structure. I want to walk you through what happened and what the options are.

What the lender flagged

The back-end DTI on the file came in at 49.2% against the conventional ceiling of 45% per the AUS findings on this file. The main drivers: the auto loan ($420/mo, ~2 years remaining), the student loan recalculation (the lender used the IBR formula at 1% of balance per current guideline), and the housing payment at the offer price.

What this isn't

This isn't a final no. It's a no to this structure on this file with this lender. Here's what could change the answer.

What the options look like

1. **Different lender, same program.** Some lenders' overlays on conventional are more flexible at the 45-50% DTI band depending on AUS findings. I'd run the file through a second lender's pricing engine and see if any come back approve/eligible. No additional cost to you — same docs, same disclosure package.

2. **FHA path.** FHA allows higher DTI ratios in some cases (up to 56.99% back-end with compensating factors), with different overlays and MI structure. Tradeoffs: MIP for life of loan on the relevant program, higher upfront cost. Worth modeling against the conventional numbers.

3. **Restructure the offer.** A lower offer price changes the housing payment and the DTI. We'd need to discuss this with your Realtor — knowing where the seller will hold and where the room is.

4. **Pay down a debt before re-submission.** If the auto loan is close to being paid off, paying it off (and confirming a zero balance to the bureau) removes that monthly obligation from the DTI calc. This is a borrower-funds-available question.

5. **Wait and re-document.** If the file improves naturally (year-end bonus posts, income recertifies higher), we can re-submit later.

What I need from you

A 15-minute call to walk through these together. I want to hear what flexibility you have and where the priorities are.

This is not the news you wanted. I know it. But none of these options is a closed door — they're paths to a closed deal. Let's get on a call and figure out which one fits.

Call me at [phone] when you have 15 minutes today or tomorrow.
```

## 7. Originator-internal pre-lock conversation outline

For the conversation with the borrower about whether to lock or float. Not a recommendation — a structure for the conversation.

```
LOCK / FLOAT CONVERSATION — for borrower call

The honest framing

I can't predict where rates go from here. Neither can anyone else, despite what some headlines say. What I can do is walk through the tradeoffs honestly.

What locking does

- Removes rate risk for the lock window (commonly 30, 45, 60 days)
- Costs nothing additional in most structures, sometimes a small lock fee or a slightly different rate vs a longer lock
- Caps your downside if rates move up
- Caps your upside if rates move down (unless your lender offers a float-down option, often at a cost)

What floating does

- Keeps both directions open
- You wear the rate move, either way
- Often borrowers float when they believe rates will fall and lock when they believe rates will rise — both of which are predictions, both of which have a track record of being wrong as often as right

The questions worth thinking about

- How tight is the budget if rates move up 25 bps before you close? 50 bps? Can you still afford the payment?
- How much risk tolerance do you have on something with this much money attached to it?
- What's your time horizon for the home — short stay or long? Affects how much rate matters over the life of the loan
- What's the lock window we'd need (depends on closing timeline)?
- Is there a float-down option in our shop's lock product, and what does it cost?

The honest answer

Most borrowers in a stable financial position with a clear closing timeline lock when they have an accepted contract and the file is moving. That's not advice — that's the most common pattern. Your situation may differ. The decision is yours; I just want you walking in with the tradeoffs visible.

---

What I'm not doing: predicting where rates go between now and your close.
```
