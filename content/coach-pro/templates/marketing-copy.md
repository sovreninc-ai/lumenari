# Marketing Copy

Templates for Instagram captions, weekly newsletter, website service pages, and re-engagement of lapsed clients. Anti-pattern enforcement is baked in — no "transform your life," no guaranteed outcomes, no "passion" language.

---

## The standing rule

Every piece of marketing copy this kit produces:

- Describes the work, not the outcome
- Matches practitioner type — coaching language vs. clinical language
- Stays inside scope of practice
- Closes with a clear next step
- Avoids the banned-word list: transform, unlock, passion, rock-star, 10x, your best self, level up, journey (as a noun), elevate, empower
- Uses "client" or "person I'm working with" — not "customer"

If a draft slips into any of the above, re-prompt with: "Rewrite — you used a banned word. Replace and keep the meaning."

## Instagram caption template

### The prompt

```
You are writing an Instagram caption for a [practitioner type] post.

Theme or topic: [paste — could be a recent client insight (anonymized), a common pattern you see, a piece of writing you wanted to share, a workshop announcement]

Caption type: [educational / behind-the-scenes / question-to-audience / workshop promo]

Rules:
- 80-200 words
- Hook in the first line (curiosity, contrarian, or specific observation)
- One clear takeaway or invitation
- Voice: warm but specific; not motivational-speaker
- No banned words (transform, unlock, passion, etc.)
- End with a question or a clear next step (DM, link in bio, save the post)
- If using hashtags, keep to 4-6 specific ones
```

### Output structure (example — life coach, educational)

```
The most common thing I see in first sessions: a client says "I just want clarity," and what they actually mean is "I want permission to stop pretending the answer isn't already obvious."

Clarity is rarely the missing piece. The piece that's missing is the willingness to call what's already clear by its name.

If you've been spinning on the same decision for three months, the work isn't analytical. It's about whether you're ready to act on what you already know.

A 50-minute session won't give you the answer — you have it. It might give you the room to admit it.

If that lands, save the post. If it doesn't, I'm probably not the right fit and that's fine.

#coaching #clarity #decisionmaking
```

That's the bar. Specific, opinionated, scope-respecting, no banned vocabulary, no false intimacy.

## Weekly newsletter intro template

### The prompt

```
You are writing the intro paragraph of a weekly newsletter for a [practitioner type]'s solo practice.

This week's topic: [paste]
Recent client pattern I'm noticing (anonymized): [paste, optional]

Rules:
- 100-180 words for the intro
- Voice: warm, specific, slightly under-explained (trust the reader)
- No banned words
- Open with a hook — observation, question, or short story
- One takeaway by the end of the intro
- Hand off to the body of the newsletter with a clear transition
```

### Output structure (example — personal trainer)

```
Three clients this week told me the same thing in different words: "I'm fine, I'm just tired."

Fine and tired aren't a state. They're a signal that something's been deferred for too long. For one client it was sleep — he'd been chronically under 6 hours for a month and called it normal. For another it was strength — she'd cut her sessions back during a busy work stretch and the strength loss caught up with her. For the third it was nutrition.

"Fine" is almost always one of those three.

This week, a few questions to ask yourself + a 10-minute drill for each of them. Skim the rest below.
```

## Website service page template

### The prompt

```
You are writing the main service page on a solo [practitioner type]'s website.

About my practice:
- Practitioner type: [life coach / personal trainer / licensed therapist with credential]
- Jurisdiction: [where I'm licensed if applicable]
- What I actually do: [paste 2-3 sentences]
- Who I work with: [audience]
- Fee: [if displaying] / "Inquire" / "Pay-what-works"
- Format: [in-person / video / phone / mix]

Rules:
- 350-500 words total
- Sections: (1) hero — one sentence, who you help with what; (2) what working with me looks like — specifics, not generalities; (3) what it costs + format; (4) who this isn't for; (5) next step
- Voice: specific, warm, not salesy
- No banned words
- "Who this isn't for" section is required — it builds trust and pre-qualifies
- End with one CTA
- Include a scope-of-practice line in section 4
```

### Output structure (example — licensed therapist)

```
# Therapy for adults who feel like they've "tried everything"

I'm [Name], a [credential] in [jurisdiction]. I work with adults who've done some therapy before and are looking for something more grounded, present-focused, and specific.

## What working with me looks like

Sessions are weekly or biweekly, 50 minutes, video or in-person.

The first session is mostly listening — I ask a lot of questions about what's been hard, what's worked before, and what you're hoping to do differently. By the end of it, you'll know whether I'm a fit.

After that, sessions are part conversation, part skill-building. I draw from [list two or three modalities you're trained in — CBT, ACT, IFS, EMDR, etc.] depending on what's useful. You won't get a script. You'll get a way of working that's specific to you.

Between sessions, I'll occasionally suggest something to try — a noticing practice, a journaling prompt, a conversation to have. You're the one doing the work; my job is to make it specific enough to actually move.

## What it costs + format

[$X] per 50-minute session. Sliding scale available — ask. Sessions are video (Zoom or Doxy) or in-person at my office in [neighborhood]. I'm out-of-network for insurance; I provide receipts you can submit for partial reimbursement.

## Who this isn't for

I'm not the right fit if:
- You're in active crisis and need higher-intensity care (DBT-IOP, partial hospitalization). I can help with a referral.
- You're looking for medication management. I'm not a prescriber.
- You want a coach who'll push you toward specific goals on a timeline. That's not how I work.

If any of that's a yes, I can probably point you to someone better suited.

## Next step

[Book a free 15-minute consultation] — we'll talk briefly and you'll know if it feels right.

*Therapy is regulated in [jurisdiction]. I'm licensed under [board / college]. If you're in crisis, contact 988 (US/Canada) or your nearest emergency department.*
```

## Re-engagement email for a lapsed client

For clients who've been gone 4+ weeks and you're not sure whether to reach out.

### The prompt

```
You are drafting a re-engagement email for a lapsed [coaching / training / therapy] client.

Relationship history:
- Number of sessions: [N]
- Last session: [date / how long ago]
- How it ended: [neutral / abrupt / mutual pause / unclear]
- Practitioner type: [coach / trainer / therapist]

Rules:
- 80-150 words
- Warm, no-pressure
- Acknowledge the time gap without making it weird
- Offer a low-friction next step (a single question, a quick check-in)
- Never imply they "should" return or that they're falling behind
- Respect their autonomy — they have the right not to come back
- Include disclaimer footer
```

### Output structure (example — life coach)

```
Hi A.,

It's been about six weeks since our last session. I'm not chasing — just checking in.

If life pulled you in a different direction, that's completely fine. If you've been thinking about picking sessions back up but haven't found the moment, that's normal and I'm here whenever.

If you want to chat for 10 minutes about whether it makes sense to pick up where we left off, or whether we should start with something different, just reply to this email and I'll send a couple of times.

Either way, hope you're well.

— [Coach Name]

*Coaching is not a substitute for medical or mental-health care. If you're in crisis, contact 988 (US/Canada) or your local emergency line.*
```

## What good marketing copy doesn't do

- **Promise outcomes.** "You'll feel better." "You'll lose 15 pounds." Outcomes vary. The copy describes the work.
- **Pretend the practitioner is for everyone.** A clear "who this isn't for" section is more trustworthy than a vague "I work with anyone who's ready."
- **Use clinical language without the credential.** A life coach saying "we'll process the trauma" creates legal exposure and sets clients up for disappointment.
- **Default to "passion."** The word is meaningless in marketing copy. Describe what you actually do.
- **Manufacture intimacy.** "I see you." "You're not alone." If the practitioner hasn't earned the line with the reader, it reads as fake.
- **Skip the disclaimer.** Every public-facing piece includes one. Edits are fine; deletions need a reason.
