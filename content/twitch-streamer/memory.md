# Memory — Twitch / Live Streamer Pack

## Domain context

Twitch streamers operate on a different rhythm than YouTubers or podcasters: their "content" is the live stream itself, often 3-6 hours, 4-6 nights a week. Off-stream work (graphics, panels, social, sponsor outreach, community management) happens in the cracks — typically a 1-2 hour block before going live or on the one off-day they take. Most working streamers are solo: no manager, no editor, no agent unless they've crossed roughly 2,000 CCV consistently.

The platform's economics are unforgiving below mid-tier. Twitch's sub split is 50/50 by default for most streamers, climbing toward 60/40 or 70/30 for some Partners. Bits pay roughly $0.01 per bit cheered. Ads are inconsistent — programmatic CPMs are low ($2-6) and unreliable. Real money comes from sponsorships ($500-5,000+ per deal for streamers in the 500-5,000 CCV range), Patreon or Ko-fi memberships, and direct merch sales. Sponsors care about engagement and audience match far more than follower count — a 1,500-CCV streamer with a hyper-engaged FPS community is a better partner for a peripherals brand than a 10,000-CCV variety channel.

Success at the working-streamer level is: consistent stream schedule (audiences punish missed streams more than they reward big ones), a Discord that's active even when the stream is offline, sub growth slightly outpacing churn, and one or two recurring sponsor partners who renew because attribution works.

## Vocabulary the AI should know

- CCV: concurrent viewers, the live-audience count at a given moment
- ACV / AVG: average concurrent viewers across a stream or month — the metric sponsors actually care about
- Peak CCV: the highest CCV during a stream, usually mid-stream not at start
- Hours watched: total viewer-hours across the stream — a strong engagement signal
- Sub points: Twitch's measure for sub-only emotes and rewards; tied to total subs
- T1 / T2 / T3 subs: Twitch's three sub tiers at $4.99 / $9.99 / $24.99 monthly USD
- Prime sub: a free sub funded by Amazon Prime; the streamer still gets the split
- Bits: Twitch's cheering currency; ~$0.01 per bit to the streamer
- Affiliate / Partner: Twitch's two monetization tiers — Affiliate is automatic at 50 followers + 8 hours streamed + 7 days, Partner is a manual application
- Raid: when one streamer ends their stream by sending their audience to another stream, live
- Host: passive routing of an offline channel to point at another stream (mostly deprecated in favor of raids)
- Lurkers: viewers who watch but don't chat — the silent majority of any stream
- Mods: volunteer moderators in chat — usually the most engaged community members
- Sub goal / follow goal: a stream's running target for new subs or follows
- Stream key: the unique key that connects OBS / Streamlabs / encoder software to the channel
- StreamElements / Streamlabs / Sammi / Lumia: the most common tools for overlays, alerts, and stream automation
- Just Chatting: Twitch's catch-all category for non-gaming content — now one of the platform's biggest

## Common workflows

- **Bio rewrite for a new game/category pivot**: trigger = streamer is shifting focus (e.g., variety → ranked Apex grind) → steps = streamer gives new direction, voice adjectives, what's the arc → output = 4-6 line bio with one-line who-you-are, schedule, current arc, one inside thing, and links
- **Schedule announcement (new schedule or pivot)**: trigger = schedule change or relaunch → steps = streamer gives the new schedule + why it changed → output = three versions — Twitter/Bluesky (under 280 chars), Discord (100-200 words), Twitch panel update (plain text with explicit time zone and schedule-slip policy)
- **Sponsor outreach cold pitch**: trigger = streamer has a brand in mind → steps = streamer gives brand, why they fit, recent thing about the brand to reference, and current metrics → output = personalized email with audience-match opener, conservative metrics block, specific ask, soft close
- **Community return-from-break post**: trigger = coming back from 1+ week off → steps = streamer gives reason (or "I just needed rest"), what's next, what to expect → output = 100-200 word Discord post with 280-char Twitter version that links to it

## What to avoid / common mistakes

- Sponsor pitches that lead with follower count instead of audience match and engagement
- Writing "we" when the streamer is solo with no co-host or team
- Using "vibes only," "chill vibes," "good vibes" in a bio — vibe-as-substitute-for-personality
- Bios that are just a list of games and PC specs ("AMD 7950X3D, 4090, 64GB DDR5") with no actual human in them
- "Going live in 5 minutes!!!" as the entire schedule tweet — no reason to click
- Apology-toned community posts when nothing wrong happened (taking a break, losing a game)
- Generic "level up your gameplay" or "epic moments" copy — instant AI-tell
- Listing every peripheral and sponsor in the bio — turns the bio into a sponsor reel

## Tone / register

A real working streamer sounds like someone who's been in chat with their viewers for thousands of hours. Sentences are short, sometimes fragmentary. They use specific shorthand from their community when it earns its place but don't overplay it for outsiders. They say "see you Tuesday" not "join us as we embark on an epic journey." They name the actual game, the actual goal, the actual thing happening — not "exciting content." They write the way they'd tell a regular in their Discord what's up, not the way an agency would write copy. When they're funny, they're dry or absurd, rarely loud. When they're earnest, they're brief.
