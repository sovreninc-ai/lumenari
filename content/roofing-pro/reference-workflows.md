## 3. Customer education — deductible and depreciation

```
Hi Sarah and Marcus,

You asked at our walk-through how the insurance payout is going to work. It's confusing the first time — let me walk through it.

How your policy pays this claim:

1. The adjuster scopes the damage and writes a number. That's the Replacement Cost Value (RCV) — what it costs to fully replace the damaged roof in today's dollars. Let's call that $24,500 illustratively.

2. Your insurance company sends you an initial check for the Actual Cash Value (ACV). ACV = RCV minus depreciation for the age of the roof. Your roof is 15 years old. On a 25-year shingle, that's substantial depreciation — call it $7,500 withheld. So initial ACV check = $24,500 - $7,500 = $17,000.

3. You owe the deductible. Your policy shows a $1,000 hail deductible. The insurance company subtracts that from their payment, not from the total. So your first check is actually $17,000 - $1,000 = $16,000.

4. We do the work. We submit the final invoice showing the actual replacement was performed. Your insurance company then releases the Recoverable Depreciation — that $7,500 they held back. They send that as a second check directly to you (or, in some cases, payable to both you and us).

5. Total math, illustratively:
   - Scope RCV: $24,500
   - Your deductible (out of pocket): $1,000
   - Insurance pays in total: $24,500 - $1,000 = $23,500
   - Your total to us for the work: $24,500
   - The $1,000 difference between what insurance pays and what we charge is your deductible. That's your contribution to the loss, by law.

WHAT I CAN'T DO, AND WON'T:

- I cannot waive your deductible. In Alberta (and in most jurisdictions), that's insurance fraud — a criminal offense for the contractor, not the homeowner. Any contractor who tells you "we'll cover your deductible" or "free roof" is breaking the law and you should walk away.

- I cannot promise the insurance company will approve everything I bid. I can document our scope and submit supplement requests for items I believe are missing (and we'll be doing that — I've identified several supplements I'll be requesting). The final scope decision is between you and your carrier.

- I cannot act as your public adjuster. If the insurance company denies items I supplemented, or if you get into a real fight over scope or coverage, you'd want to work with a licensed public adjuster (Alberta has them) or a property insurance attorney. I'm not licensed to negotiate the claim on your behalf — I can document, advocate, and recommend, but the negotiation is your call. Happy to refer you to a PA or attorney I trust if it comes to that.

WHAT I DO:

- Thorough inspection report (you have a copy; the adjuster has one)
- Meet the adjuster on the roof when they scope (already scheduled for Tuesday)
- Submit supplement requests for items the adjuster's first scope may miss (likely — I've reviewed and identified several)
- Pull permits and perform the work to current Alberta Building Code
- Submit final invoice and documentation that triggers the depreciation release
- Register the manufacturer warranty (we're GAF Master Elite, which qualifies you for their Golden Pledge system warranty if you install the matched GAF system — we'll discuss)

If anything in this is unclear, call me. This is a confusing process the first time and I'd rather over-explain than have you frustrated three weeks from now wondering why your check is smaller than the bill.

— Tom Tarasov
Tarasov Roofing & Exteriors

## 6. "My claim was denied" follow-up

```
Subject: Following up on the carrier's response

Hi Marcus,

I got your email yesterday with the carrier's denial letter. Genuinely sorry — that's a frustrating outcome, especially after the documentation we put together.

Let me give you my honest read.

What I see in the denial:
The carrier is calling the damage "cosmetic" and stating the roof "retains its functional integrity." This is an increasingly common denial pattern in the last few years, especially on hail claims where the damage hasn't yet caused a leak. Some carriers have added explicit cosmetic damage exclusions to their policies. Whether yours has one is a question you'll need to ask the carrier directly or check your policy.

What's true:
- The damage is real. The mat exposure on multiple slopes is documented and is, in my professional assessment, exactly what hail damage looks like.
- The roof will likely leak within 2-5 years if the damage isn't addressed. Mat-exposed shingles fail faster than weathered-but-intact ones.
- The denial does not change my inspection assessment. It's a coverage decision by the carrier, not a damage assessment.

What I can do and what I can't:

I can:
- Re-submit my inspection report and the supplement letter with a request for a re-inspection
- Provide a written response to the specific points in the denial letter (with you as the addressee, you forwarding to the carrier)
- Refer you to a licensed public adjuster in Alberta who handles denials like this

I cannot:
- Negotiate the claim on your behalf. That's PA or attorney work.
- Promise the re-inspection will reverse the decision. Sometimes it does, often it doesn't.
- Tell you whether to pursue this further through legal channels. That's a decision between you, a PA, or an attorney.

Your three real options:

1. Accept the denial. Let the roof go for now, plan to replace on your own dime in 2-4 years when it starts to fail. Manageable but you're paying out of pocket for what should have been a covered loss.

2. Engage a licensed public adjuster. Their fee is typically 10-15% of the recovery. If they can get the claim approved, you net more than option 1. If they can't, you've spent a consultation fee for nothing. Most PAs in Alberta will look at your file at no charge to assess whether it's worth taking.

3. Engage a property insurance attorney. Higher cost, more aggressive posture, suitable for cases where bad faith may be in play. Most attorneys offer a free consultation.

I have a PA I trust in Calgary and a property attorney I've worked with. Happy to make the introduction with no kickback to me either way.

Whatever you decide, I'm in your corner on the inspection facts. If a PA or attorney needs me to walk through the report or be deposed (rare but possible), I'll do it.

Talk it over with Sarah. Call me when you've thought through which way to go. No rush from my side.

— Tom
