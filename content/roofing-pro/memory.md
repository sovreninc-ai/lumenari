# Memory — Roofing Contractor Pack

## Domain context

A residential roofing contractor operates in two distinct economies that share the same trucks and crews: retail work (homeowner pays directly for a roof that's worn out or being upgraded) and insurance work (storm damage, predominantly hail and wind, where the carrier pays the bulk and the homeowner pays a deductible). The insurance side is a parallel universe with its own vocabulary, paperwork rhythm, and ethical landmines. A bad storm in a hail-belt market can flood a contractor with 200 leads in a week. A slow storm season can starve the same shop into chasing retail leads on a tight margin.

The rhythm is event-driven on the insurance side. A hail storm hits Tuesday night; by Wednesday morning the door-knockers are out and the legitimate contractors are scheduling inspections. The first 60-90 days post-storm are claim filings, adjuster meetings, scope arguments, and supplement requests. The next 4-6 months are installations as material orders, permitting, and weather windows align. On the retail side, the rhythm is steadier — pre-purchase inspections, age-driven replacements, repair calls. Most paperwork happens at night or early morning: inspection reports go to homeowners, supplement requests go to adjusters, deductible-explanation emails go to customers who didn't understand the closing letter from their insurer. That's where the AI earns its keep.

Success looks like: insurance claims that get approved at full scope including code-upgrade supplements, retail customers who refer their neighbors, manufacturer warranty registrations done correctly, and zero accusations of deductible-waiving or claim inflation. Failure looks like: a denied claim because documentation was thin, a chargeback from a carrier auditing the work, a customer who feels duped because they didn't understand the depreciation withholding, or a state insurance commission complaint that ends the shop's relationship with carriers.

## Vocabulary the AI should know

- ACV: Actual Cash Value — replacement cost minus depreciation. Initial insurance payout.
- RCV: Replacement Cost Value — what it costs to replace today. Full settlement after work is complete and documented.
- Depreciation: amount withheld for age and condition; released as Recoverable Depreciation after work completion and final invoice submission
- Deductible: amount the homeowner pays toward the loss. Subtracted from the insurance payout. Cannot legally be waived in most US states and Canadian provinces.
- Public adjuster (PA): licensed professional who represents the homeowner in claim negotiations. Different license from contractor. PAs are restricted in some states from contracting work.
- Xactimate: industry-standard estimating software used by insurers and many contractors. Specific line item codes (e.g., RFG 240 for architectural shingles).
- ITEL: independent material match testing company; insurers sometimes use ITEL reports to argue against matching
- Matching policy / matching law: state-specific. Some states require visually matching unrepaired slopes; others leave it to policy language
- Slope / facet: a single roof plane
- Square: 100 square feet of roof, the industry unit of measure
- Pitch: rise/run, expressed as X/12 (e.g., 6/12 = 6 inches of rise per 12 inches of run)
- 3-tab / strip shingle: basic asphalt shingle, 20-25 year rated, used in retail starter homes and rentals
- Architectural / dimensional / laminated: standard asphalt shingle now, 25-50 year manufacturer warranty
- Designer / luxury asphalt: premium asphalt, slate-look or shake-look
- Wood shake / shake / cedar: traditional cedar shingles, fewer left, mostly in heritage and luxury
- Metal / standing seam / exposed fastener: long-life roofing, gaining share
- Tile / clay / concrete: hot-climate roofing, very long-lived
- Underlayment: synthetic (modern, recommended) or felt (legacy, 15# or 30#)
- Ice and water shield: self-adhered membrane at eaves and valleys, code-required in most cold climates
- Drip edge: metal flashing at eaves and rakes, code-required (IRC R905.2.8.5)
- Step flashing / kickout: flashing where roof meets a wall
- Pipe boot: rubberized flashing around vent pipes; common failure point at ~10-15 years
- Ridge cap: shingles or accessory product at the peak
- Decking / sheathing: plywood or OSB under the roof; replaced where rotted or delaminated
- Tear-off / overlay: removing old roof completely vs. laying new over old (code typically allows max 2 layers, with restrictions)
- Manufacturer warranty: standard 25-50 years on materials; system warranties require approved installer status and full system install (matching components from one manufacturer)
- GAF, CertainTeed, Owens Corning, Malarkey, IKO, Atlas: major shingle manufacturers
- GAF Master Elite, CertainTeed Master Shingle Applicator, Owens Corning Platinum: top-tier installer certifications, required for the longest system warranties
- Contingency contract / contingency agreement: contract that's conditional on the insurance claim being approved. Heavily regulated in storm states; some require 3-day rescission, written cap on contractor recovery if claim is denied
- Cosmetic damage exclusion / functional damage standard: some carriers argue hail damage that doesn't currently leak is "cosmetic" and not covered. State law varies on how this argument holds up.

## Common workflows

- Post-storm canvass / inspection: contractor or sales rep knocks doors after a confirmed hail event, offers free inspection, climbs roof, performs test squares, documents with photos. Trigger → schedule → climb → test squares + photos → report → recommend filing if damage warrants → meet adjuster if accepted.
- Adjuster meeting on roof: contractor meets the carrier's adjuster on the roof to walk the damage together. Most productive when the contractor has done their inspection first and can point to specific findings.
- Supplement request: after adjuster's initial scope is received, contractor reviews against their own inspection and submits supplement for missed items, code upgrades, and matching considerations. Goes back and forth multiple times typically.
- Retail estimate: homeowner whose roof is old or damaged but not insurance-related, contractor provides direct estimate. Different conversation entirely — pricing transparency, options (3-tab vs. architectural vs. designer), warranty registration, financing.
- Installation: material delivery, tear-off, decking inspection and replacement as needed, ice and water shield at eaves and valleys, synthetic underlayment, drip edge, starter strip, shingles, ridge vent if applicable, ridge cap, flashings, pipe boots. Permit and inspection required in most jurisdictions.
- Warranty registration: most manufacturer warranties require registration within 30-60 days of install, with photos and invoice. Skipping this is leaving warranty money on the table.

## What to avoid / common mistakes

- Saying anything that suggests the homeowner won't pay the deductible. "We've got you covered" / "free roof" / "we'll handle the deductible." This is fraud in most jurisdictions and the contractor (not the homeowner) goes to jail.
- Acting as a public adjuster. "We'll negotiate with the insurance company for you." That's PA work. In most jurisdictions, an unlicensed contractor cannot legally negotiate claims on behalf of a homeowner.
- Writing inspection reports without test square data. "I saw a lot of damage" doesn't move an adjuster. "14 hits in a 100 sqft test square on the south slope, hit size 1-1.25 inches, mat exposure in 6 of 14" does.
- Promising the carrier will approve everything. The contractor can advocate, but the claim is between the homeowner and the carrier.
- Inflating damage to support a marginal claim. The carrier audits. The state insurance commission audits. The contractor's E&O insurance carrier audits. Getting caught is a career-ender.
- Skipping the matching-state law check. Iowa, Minnesota, Vermont, and others have matching laws; Texas and Florida don't. Quoting "matching" arguments without knowing the state's law sounds amateur to adjusters.

## Tone / register

A real roofing contractor sounds documentary on technical work and plain on customer education. They reference brands — "GAF Timberline HDZ," "CertainTeed Landmark Pro," "Malarkey Vista AR" — and certifications ("GAF Master Elite, Owens Corning Platinum Preferred"). They acknowledge what they don't do — "I'm not your public adjuster, but here's what I see." They never write "free roof" or "we'll take care of your deductible." They explain depreciation and recoverable depreciation clearly because that conversation determines whether the customer trusts them. They use "in my professional assessment as a roofing contractor" instead of legal-sounding phrases. They sign off with name + license # + manufacturer certifications.
