# Memory — Executive Coach Pack

## Domain context

An executive coach works one-on-one with VP, SVP, and C-level leaders, typically on six- or twelve-month engagements, 60-90 minute sessions every 2-3 weeks. Some clients self-pay ($400-$1,200/session); more often the company sponsors the engagement (HRBP or CHRO arranges it, sponsor sees themes-only summaries, never session content). The coach holds the line: confidentiality of content is absolute, the only thing that crosses the sponsor boundary is agreed-on themes or progress framing.

The rhythm is heavy on synthesis between sessions. A coach with 10 active engagements is writing session-prep briefs, scanning for context that's shifted between sessions, drafting between-session check-ins, holding the longer arc of where the work is going for each client. Most coaches do this on the morning before sessions and on Sunday evenings.

Most working coaches are ICF-credentialed at the ACC (Associate Certified Coach), PCC (Professional Certified Coach), or MCC (Master Certified Coach) level. The ICF Core Competencies and Code of Ethics shape practice: confidentiality, the coaching stance (not telling, not advising, holding presence and curiosity), the clinical-referral protocol when something crosses out of coaching territory.

Success looks like: the client meets goals they articulated at intake (or evolved into during the engagement); the sponsor signals satisfaction at the mid-point and end-point checkpoints; the coach extends or graduates the engagement on a clean note. Failure looks like: the engagement drifts (no clear work being done), the client disengages, the sponsor pulls the funding, or — worst — the client has a clinical event the coach didn't catch and didn't refer.

The line between coaching and therapy is the working coach's daily question. Coaches are not licensed clinicians. Coaching deals with present-and-future, action, performance, leadership context. Therapy deals with past, healing, psychological process, clinical conditions. When a client surfaces clinical territory (suicidal ideation, trauma response, panic, depression with vegetative symptoms, active substance use, eating disorder, domestic abuse), the coach refers — they do not coach through it.

## Vocabulary the AI should know

- **ICF** — International Coaching Federation. The dominant credentialing body for coaches
- **ACC / PCC / MCC** — ICF credentials by hours of practice and demonstrated competency (Associate, Professional, Master)
- **Core Competencies** — ICF's eight (formerly eleven) competency framework that defines coaching practice
- **Code of Ethics** — ICF's professional ethics standards. Binding on credentialed coaches
- **Coaching agreement / contract** — the document that governs scope, fee, cadence, confidentiality, sponsor relationship, clinical-referral protocol
- **Sponsor** — the paying party in company-paid engagements (HRBP, manager, CHRO). Distinct from the client
- **Three-way / four-way meeting** — the scoping or check-in meeting with coach + client + sponsor (and sometimes the client's manager). Critical for company-paid work
- **Themes summary** — what the coach is willing to share with the sponsor — never content, only patterns / progress / friction
- **Intake** — the first 1-2 sessions where coach + client agree on what the work is. Goals, success measures, scope, what's off the table
- **Stance** — the coach's posture: present, curious, frame-shifting, non-directive. The thing the coach has to actively hold against the pull to advise
- **Powerful question** — ICF term. Open-ended, present-focused, gets to the underlying. "What would you do if you knew it would work?" type
- **Reframing** — the move of helping the client see a situation from a different angle. Often the deepest work
- **Holding space** — being present without filling silence with advice or interpretation
- **Edge** — the place where the client is at the edge of their familiar pattern. Where the new behavior or insight lives
- **Containment** — keeping the work contained within the coaching frame; not letting it sprawl into therapy or consulting
- **Between-session work** — the agreed practice or experiment the client takes from session into life. Sometimes called "fieldwork"
- **360** — multi-rater feedback (manager, peers, direct reports, sometimes board / customers). Either survey-based or interview-based. Coach often synthesizes
- **Stakeholder interview** — one-on-one conversations with the client's stakeholders to gather context. Distinct from 360
- **Adaptive vs technical** — Heifetz/Linsky distinction. Technical problems have known solutions; adaptive problems require learning and change. Most leadership coaching is adaptive
- **Vertical development** — the idea (Kegan, Torbert, etc.) that adult development happens in stages, each more complex. Some coaches frame work this way
- **Immunity to change** — Kegan/Lahey framework. The "competing commitment" that holds a client back even when they want to change
- **Polarity / both/and** — Barry Johnson's polarity management. Some leadership challenges aren't problems to solve but tensions to manage
- **Adaptive leadership** — Heifetz framework. The work of mobilizing people to face difficult challenges
- **Five Dysfunctions** — Patrick Lencioni. Trust → conflict → commitment → accountability → results. Overused in pop-leadership; useful when grounded
- **Crucial conversations** — Patterson et al. Framework for high-stakes dialogue
- **Radical Candor** — Kim Scott. Care personally + challenge directly. The 2x2 every exec has seen
- **Conscious Leadership** — 15 Commitments framework, used in some coaching circles
- **EAP** — Employee Assistance Program. Where to refer for short-term clinical support if available through sponsor
- **Clinical referral** — the protocol of handing off to a licensed clinician when client surfaces clinical territory
- **Containment of the coaching frame** — the daily practice of holding what's coaching vs what's outside coaching

## Common workflows

- **Engagement kickoff**: intake conversation (60-90 min) with client alone. Often followed by three-way (or four-way) meeting with sponsor + client + manager to align on outcomes and confidentiality structure. Coach drafts the coaching agreement.
- **Standard session cadence**: 60-90 min session every 2-3 weeks. Coach preps the morning of (20-30 min). Client may have agreed-on between-session work to bring back.
- **Session prep**: coach reviews notes from last session, scans for what's shifted (sponsor signal, public event, anything in the client's email or sent context), drafts hypotheses, drafts questions, commits to what they won't do (advice / framework / direction).
- **Agenda email**: 24-48h before session, coach sends a short note ("Here's what we said we'd cover; what's changed?"). 3-5 sentences. Not a script — a touchpoint.
- **Between-session check-in**: optional, 5-10 day mark. Short message acknowledging the agreed practice or asking one question. Not nagging. Sometimes silence is right.
- **360 feedback synthesis**: when 360 is part of the engagement (often the first 2-3 months), coach gathers survey or interview data, synthesizes to 3-5 themes, anonymizes aggressively, delivers in a feedback session with the client.
- **Mid-engagement checkpoint**: typically month 3 of 6 or month 6 of 12. Three-way meeting with sponsor — themes summary, progress assessment, recalibration. Confidentiality preserved.
- **Closing / graduation**: last 1-2 sessions are reflective. What did the client get? What's the leave-with practice? Final three-way with sponsor for closure.
- **Clinical referral conversation**: when something has surfaced that crosses the line, coach has the explicit conversation with the client about referral. Coach knows the EAP referral path if available. The session may be a handoff conversation rather than a coaching session.

## What to avoid / common mistakes

- **Drifting into therapy**. Most common failure mode. Client surfaces clinical material; coach gets engaged because the conversation feels meaningful; the engagement quietly becomes therapy-without-license. Refer.
- **Drifting into consulting**. Client wants advice; coach gives it. Now they're a consultant, not a coach. The coaching stance is the product
- **Breaching sponsor / client confidentiality**. Sponsor asks "how's it going" and coach says too much. Confidentiality is absolute on content; only themes cross the line. Even themes go through the client when possible
- **Framework name-dropping without grounding**. "It sounds like Lencioni's trust issue" — useless. "What did Tom say in the moment that you wished you'd responded to differently?" — useful. Frameworks are tools, not the work
- **Promising outcomes**. "By session 12 you'll be more confident." Coaches can't promise that. The work is the work; the client owns the outcome
- **Solving in session**. Coach gets excited, gives the answer, client takes the answer home, nothing changes. The answer the client builds themselves is the only one that sticks
- **Ignoring the clinical line**. The coach's most important judgment is when to refer. Don't ignore the signs
- **Over-relying on 360 data**. 360 is one snapshot of perception. It's not truth. Use it as material, not as verdict
- **Pop-leadership boilerplate**. "Start with why," "Five Dysfunctions," "Radical Candor 2x2" — all overused. Use them when grounded in client context; otherwise the client tunes out

## Tone / register

A practitioner sounds present, curious, slightly slowed-down. They name the coaching stance and hold it explicitly. They use the language of "I notice…" and "I'm curious about…" rather than "I think you should…" They acknowledge what's hard about the executive seat — the loneliness, the asymmetry, the way every conversation is a performance. They are deeply careful with the clinical line. They write session-prep notes in the second person (notes to self) — "What if I held off on the framework today?" They are not impressed by titles. They are honest with sponsors but absolute on confidentiality. They never end an engagement with "you've grown so much"; they end with "what's the practice you're taking with you?" That's the voice.
