Real worked examples. Steal whichever ones map to your practice. Names and identifying detail are placeholders.

## 1. Session prep brief — VP Engineering client, mid-engagement

```
SESSION PREP — M — Session 7 of 12 — 2026-05-17

WHERE WE LEFT OFF
Last session ended on M's experience of "shutting down" in the most recent board meeting when the CEO interrupted her mid-update. She described the pressure as "physical now, like I can't breathe in the moment."

WHAT'S SHIFTED
She emailed Wednesday: "Board met again this week. Same thing but worse." No detail. No ask.

CLINICAL CHECK — FLAG
The "physical, can't breathe" + "worse this week" framing is panic-symptom-adjacent. Before drafting the full session as coaching, I want to pause and assess.
- Has she described this anywhere outside the meeting context? (Coaching-side, situational stress under specific trigger)
- Or is it generalizing — also showing up at home, sleep, etc.? (More clinical-adjacent)
- The first I'd hold as coaching. The second I'd want to surface a clinical referral conversation.

I will ask early in session 8 whether this shows up only in the board context or more broadly. If "more broadly," I shift the session into clinical-handoff territory — name what I'm noticing, ask if she has anyone in clinical care, mention the EAP if not. I do not coach through panic symptoms.

ASSUMING IT STAYS SITUATIONAL (BOARD CONTEXT ONLY), HERE'S THE SESSION-PREP

HYPOTHESES I'M HOLDING
- I notice the "shutting down" pattern has been the central thread for 4 sessions. The conversation has been useful but circular — she names it, we explore it, she returns the next session having done little different in vivo
- I'm curious whether the work is no longer about the pattern itself but about why she's not yet willing to act differently in the room. The cost of changing the pattern is higher than the cost of the pattern, in her current calculus
- I'm watching for whether this is also about her relationship with the CEO specifically — and whether that's coachable or whether we're at the edge of what coaching can move

QUESTIONS I'M CURIOUS TO ASK
- "What's the specific moment in last week's meeting where you'd locate the shutdown? Walk me through 60 seconds."
- "If you'd done something different in that 60 seconds, what would it have cost you that the silence didn't?"
- "What's the version of you that responds differently in that moment? What does she know that you in the room didn't have access to?"
- "We've been holding this thread for 4 sessions now. What would you want different about how we're working on it?"
- "What would tell you, two months from now, that this work was worth it?"

WHAT I'M WATCHING FOR
- If she pivots to a different topic, what does she pivot to? That's data
- If she stays with the board topic but the conversation feels different, what's shifted in how she's holding it?
- If the panic-adjacent description comes back up, I move into clinical-handoff mode immediately

WHAT I WON'T DO
- I won't give her the answer about how to respond to the CEO. I have a hypothesis (set the frame at the start of the meeting, ask one question that invites the interruption to be the inquiry rather than the dismissal) but it's mine, not hers
- I won't reach for the executive-presence framework I'm tempted to use. She's read all of them. The framework isn't the work
- I won't smooth over the "physical, can't breathe" piece — I'll explicitly ask about it early in the session
- I won't extend the engagement preemptively because I'm worried about the pattern not moving. We have 5 more sessions; the work has time

CLINICAL-LINE NOTE
If the panic symptoms generalize beyond the board context, the next session converts to a clinical-handoff. EAP referral path through the CHRO sponsor (with M's permission only). I keep coaching open for after she has clinical support if she wants to continue, but I do not coach through panic symptoms.

---

What I assumed; what to verify or challenge before using:
- That the panic-adjacent framing is situational. The session opener confirms or shifts this
- That M wants to keep working on the board-meeting thread. Worth asking if she'd like to shift focus
- That she's open to the meta-question ("we've held this thread for 4 sessions — what do you want different about how we're working on it?"). Some clients hate that question; she's said before she likes it

Coaching stance check: held — questions are open and present-focused; no advice; explicit "what I won't do" includes the framework I'm tempted to deploy
Clinical-line check: flagged at the top; protocol stated; will reassess in session
Confidentiality check: notes are for me alone; sponsor sees no content from this prep
```

## 2. Agenda email — sent 36 hours before session

```
Subject: Friday at 9 — M

Hi M,

See you Friday at 9.

Last session we ended on the board meeting and what was happening for you in the interruption moment. Curious what's been live for you since, and whether you want to come back to that thread or to something else that's surfaced. You mentioned the board met again this week — bring whatever you want from it.

If anything has come up between now and Friday that you'd want to use the time on, even if it feels like it's coming from left field, bring it.
```

## 3. Between-session check-in — 8 days after a session

Context: client agreed to try one specific behavior change in the week. Coach considered drafting a check-in. The right call here was actually one short acknowledgment, not a question.

```
Subject: no need to reply

Thinking of you in the week before the board meeting. You've got this.

— [Coach first name]
```

That's it. No question. No "how's it going." No advice. Sometimes silence is right; this is one notch above silence.

## 4. 360 feedback themes synthesis — anonymized

Context: 360 conducted for "E," CFO at a 600-person manufacturer. 11 raters: CEO, 4 peers (other C-level), 4 direct reports (VP Finance, VP Accounting, VP Treasury, Controller), 2 board members. Mixed survey + 5 interviews.

```
360 FEEDBACK THEMES — E — 2026-04-22

CONTEXT
11 raters across 4 categories: CEO (1), C-level peers (4), direct reports (4), board members (2). Mixed methodology — quantitative survey for 7, semi-structured interview for the remaining 4. Range of tenure with E: 18 months to 6 years.

THEME 1: Operational excellence is unquestioned
- Consistent across categories: depth of expertise, accuracy of analysis, command of the numbers. CEO + board: "best CFO I've worked with"-tier signal. Peers: trust the work entirely. Direct reports: high learning value, high expectations
- Variance: minimal. This theme is robust
- One anonymized example: a recent acquisition review where E flagged a customer-concentration risk no one else had caught; the deal didn't close on those terms

THEME 2: Strategic voice is uneven
- Mixed across categories. CEO + 2 of 4 peers see E as a strategic partner — present in long-horizon decisions, contributes the financial frame proactively. 2 of 4 peers + 1 board member see E as primarily executional — the strategic frame comes from the CEO, E translates it into financial terms
- Variance: significant. Where the data is split (see below)
- One anonymized example: in a recent quarterly board prep, E presented the operational read crisply but, per one rater, "didn't volunteer a point of view on the question of whether we should be running this business for cash or growth right now"

THEME 3: Team development is a gap
- Direct reports raise this most. Themes: high standards, accuracy demanded; but limited feedback on growth direction, limited investment in their visibility outside finance, limited reach for stretch assignments
- Variance: 2 of 4 direct reports score this as a primary growth need; 2 of 4 see it as secondary. The split tracks with tenure (more tenured = less concerned)
- One anonymized example: one direct report said they've been at the same level for 28 months and never had an explicit conversation about what would change that

THEME 4: Conflict style — measured to a fault, possibly
- Peers and CEO mention this from different angles. CEO: "E will go along with the call even when I can tell she doesn't agree — I want to know when she doesn't." Peers: "Conflict avoidant in cross-functional discussions; defers to operating execs even when finance has the stronger frame"
- Variance: direct reports don't see this — for them, E is direct and clear. The disagreement-avoidance shows up only with peers and the CEO
- One anonymized example: a recent cross-functional decision about a market expansion where, per the CEO, E "didn't push back on the timeline I'd already set, even though I think she had concerns"

WHERE THE DATA IS SPLIT
- Theme 2 (strategic voice): half the senior stakeholders see E as strategic, half as executional. This is a real split, not a smoothing situation
- Theme 3 (team development) tenure-tracking — newer reports see growth investment as a need, longer-tenured see it as adequate
- Theme 4: shows up with peers and CEO but not with direct reports — likely because the avoidance is direction-specific (up + lateral, not down)

WHAT THE DATA DOES NOT SAY
- It doesn't say E should be more aggressive in board meetings. It says half the senior peers want her strategic frame more visible
- It doesn't say E is a poor manager. It says her direct reports want more explicit growth investment, and the demand varies by tenure
- It doesn't tell us why E avoids conflict up-and-lateral. That's the work in session
- It doesn't tell us what E wants for herself out of the next 24 months. That's also the work

NO RECOMMENDATIONS
Synthesis ends here. The readout session is where E and I build what to do with this.

---

What I assumed; what to verify or challenge before using:
- Quote paraphrases are mine — confirm with raw transcripts they don't drift from what was said
- Tenure-tracking on Theme 3 is an inference from 2 data points — flag it as inference, not finding, in the readout
- The "direction-specific avoidance" (Theme 4) is also an inference. Hold loosely

Coaching stance check: held — themes are descriptive, no recommendations included, dissent surfaced not smoothed
Clinical-line check: clear (no clinical signals in 360 input or coach observation)
Confidentiality check: all raters anonymized; no identifying quotes; tenure-tracking phrased to protect direct-report identity
```

## 5. Leadership-framework one-pager — Heifetz adaptive vs. technical

Context: client "K," SVP Product at a 900-person fintech, wrestling with a cross-functional initiative that keeps stalling. Coach has used this framework before with K; she asked for a one-pager to bring to her staff meeting.

```
FRAMEWORK NOTE — Adaptive vs. Technical Work — for K — 2026-05-09

THE SPECIFIC SITUATION
K is leading a cross-functional initiative — moving Product, Eng, and Customer Success onto a shared roadmap process — that's been "almost done" for 4 months. Surface symptoms: meetings end without decisions, the artifact (the shared roadmap document) keeps getting re-discussed, owners shift, the work isn't moving.

THE FRAME (Ron Heifetz, Marty Linsky — "Leadership on the Line," "The Practice of Adaptive Leadership")
- Technical problems have known solutions. The expertise exists somewhere; the work is to apply it. Example: standing up a new dashboard, choosing between two SaaS vendors, fixing a documentation gap
- Adaptive problems require people to learn, change, and let go of something they value. Solutions are not yet known; the work is to mobilize people to face the challenge. Example: shifting how Product and Eng decide together, building trust between two functions that have history, redefining what "good roadmap" means across teams
- The most common leadership failure: treating an adaptive challenge as if it were technical. You can't process-design your way out of an adaptive problem. The fix isn't the right meeting structure; it's the work the people have to do to change how they relate to each other and to the decision

HOW IT MAPS TO K'S SITUATION
The shared roadmap process is being run as a technical problem — get the right document, get the right meeting cadence, get the right RACI. The reasons it keeps stalling suggest adaptive territory underneath: Eng leadership has to give up unilateral roadmap authority they've held for years; Product has to accept that Eng input is co-equal not advisory; CS has to learn to bring customer signal in a form that the other two can hear and act on. None of those are document problems. They are loss problems — people losing something they had — and they require the people involved to do the adaptive work, not just K to deliver the right artifact.

WHERE THE FRICTION WILL BE
The temptation to keep iterating on the document. Every time the meeting ends without progress, the urge will be to fix the document one more time. The actual work K may need to do is name out loud, in the room, that this is an adaptive challenge — that the document isn't the problem, the loss is — and to hold the room in the discomfort of that. That's a posture shift, not a process shift. It will feel slower and like she's "not delivering." She'll be tempted to retreat to the technical fix.

QUESTIONS THE COACH MIGHT EXPLORE
- "What would it cost Eng leadership to give up the unilateral roadmap call? Have you ever named that out loud?"
- "If you stopped editing the document and instead spent your next 1:1s with the three of them asking what they're each losing, what would happen?"
- "Where do you feel the pull to make this technical again? Is that pull yours, or the room's, or both?"
- "If you held the room in the adaptive frame and the meeting ended without a decision again, what would that mean to you — and what would it mean to them?"

---

What I assumed; what to verify or challenge before using:
- That K's stalling is adaptive, not technical. Could be both. The framework is a lens, not the truth
- The "Eng losing unilateral authority" framing — confirm with K that this is the actual loss, not my inference

Coaching stance check: held — framework is grounded in the specific situation, friction is named honestly, questions explore rather than direct
Clinical-line check: clear
Confidentiality check: framework one-pager is for K's use; no sponsor breach; no identifying detail about Eng or CS leads
```

## 6. Contracting language — mid-engagement re-contracting

Context: six-month engagement, currently month 3. CHRO sponsor wants a check-in. Coach is preparing the conversation outline.

```
MID-ENGAGEMENT RE-CONTRACTING — three-way meeting prep — [Client] — 2026-05-15

PURPOSE
Mid-point check-in. Three-way conversation with client + CHRO sponsor + coach. ~45 minutes. Standard practice at month 3 of a 6-month engagement.

CONFIDENTIALITY REMINDER (verbal, by coach, at the open)
"Quick reset before we start: today is a themes-and-progress conversation. Session content stays in our coaching work. What I'll share is the themes that have emerged and the progress signal the client and I have agreed to share. Everything specific stays in the room with us."

AGENDA

1. Coach opens — confidentiality frame (above), purpose of mid-point (recalibration, not evaluation)

2. Client shares first — what they're noticing about the work; what's shifted; what they want more of from the engagement and the sponsor
   - The client leads this. The coach has prepped the client on what they're comfortable sharing in advance
   - Client speaks to themes, not session content. ("I'm noticing I'm more direct in board meetings" — yes. "Last session we explored the imposter feeling I have around the new role" — no.)

3. Sponsor shares — what they're seeing from their vantage; whether the outcomes they hoped for are visible; whether anything has shifted in the business that affects the work
   - Sponsor often shares more than they should. Coach can redirect: "What you're describing sounds like specific feedback to [client] — let's hold that for after this meeting"

4. Coach closes the loop on themes — what's emerging in the work
   - "Here's what's been most active in our sessions, in language [client] and I have agreed to share: [theme 1, theme 2]. Both of these are areas where I'd want to keep the work focused for the next 3 months unless [client] tells me different."

5. Recalibration questions
   - "Is the original engagement scope still right, or has anything shifted?"
   - "Are there outcomes the sponsor wants more visibility into?"
   - "Anything to add or change for the next 3 months?"

6. Close with explicit next-step ownership
   - Coach: continues weekly sessions, next checkpoint at month 6
   - Client: continues between-session work
   - Sponsor: any visible support needed (e.g., providing room for client to take on a stretch)

WHAT THE COACH WILL NOT DO
- Share session content
- Diagnose the client to the sponsor
- Validate or invalidate sponsor's read of the client
- Promise specific outcomes for months 4-6
- Let the meeting drift into a performance review

CLINICAL-LINE CHECK
If at any point in the conversation the sponsor describes something that suggests clinical territory (e.g., "they seem really down lately"), coach surfaces gently: "That's worth a separate conversation. Want me to follow up with [client] after this?" Don't process clinical signal in a three-way meeting.

---

What I assumed; what to verify or challenge before using:
- That the client has been prepped on what's appropriate to share in advance (pre-meeting 15-min call)
- That the sponsor understands the themes-only frame (re-confirm in the email setting up the meeting)
- That neither party expects this to be a performance evaluation

Coaching stance check: held — meeting is recalibration, not evaluation; coach holds the confidentiality line
Clinical-line check: protocol stated for surfacing clinical signal if it arises
Confidentiality check: themes-only frame confirmed; no content crosses to sponsor
```

---

These are working examples, not templates. Real engagements will vary. The structures hold; the specifics belong to your client.
