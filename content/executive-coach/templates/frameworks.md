# Leadership Framework One-Pagers — Executive Coach Pack

Four coach-facing one-pagers on frameworks executive coaches reach for between sessions. These are NOT to deploy on a client sight-unseen. They are the coach's reference material — the framework, its origin, when it's useful, when it's NOT, the questions it invites, and the friction the client will hit.

**Reminder: coaching is NOT therapy. Frameworks below are leadership development tools, not therapeutic interventions. If the client's challenge sits in clinical territory, consult a licensed therapist for clinical concerns and refer.**

---

## How to use these one-pagers

Before deploying any framework with a client:

1. Confirm the specific situation. Frameworks are useless in the abstract. "Delegation in K's specific seat, with K's specific team, this week" is the work
2. Cite the source. Don't pretend you invented it
3. Identify the friction. Where will THIS client most resist the framework's central move? That's the coaching point
4. Hold loosely. The framework is a lens, not a verdict

The reference-workflows.md has a worked example of Heifetz Adaptive Leadership applied to a real situation. Use that as the model for any other framework application.

## 1. Heifetz Adaptive Leadership

### Origin
Ron Heifetz, Marty Linsky, Alexander Grashow. Foundational texts: "Leadership Without Easy Answers" (Heifetz, 1994), "Leadership on the Line" (Heifetz + Linsky, 2002), "The Practice of Adaptive Leadership" (Heifetz + Linsky + Grashow, 2009). Origins in Harvard Kennedy School.

### What it is, in two sentences
A distinction between technical problems (known solutions, expertise applies) and adaptive challenges (no known solution, requires people to learn and change). Most leadership failures come from treating adaptive challenges as if they were technical.

### When it's useful
- Cross-functional initiatives that keep stalling despite good plans
- Change efforts where the resistance isn't about competence (people know what to do, they're not doing it)
- Situations where the leader keeps "fixing" something and it keeps coming back
- Team or culture work where the issue is loss-of-something-valued, not lack-of-something-needed
- When the client feels stuck and is reaching for "better process" or "better tools" as the solution

### When it's not useful
- When the problem really is technical (the dashboard is broken; the vendor is wrong; the runbook is missing). Don't pathologize execution
- When the client needs immediate action and adaptive reflection is a luxury (acute crisis)
- When the client has already over-intellectualized and needs to do, not analyze

### The move the framework makes
Asks: "Is this technical, or adaptive, or both?" And: "If adaptive, what are people being asked to give up? What are they losing? What learning is required?"

The adaptive question — "what are they losing?" — is the central diagnostic. Loss is the resistance.

### Questions the framework invites
- "What's the loss for each group in the situation?"
- "Where are you treating an adaptive challenge as technical right now?"
- "What's the difference between you fixing this and the people doing the work of changing?"
- "If you stopped delivering the answer, what would happen in the room?"
- "Who needs to do the learning here? Is it you, them, or both?"
- "What's the work only they can do?"

### Where clients resist
- The technical fix feels productive; sitting in adaptive uncertainty doesn't
- "Holding the room" feels like not leading
- Naming the loss out loud feels confrontational
- Adaptive work is slower than technical work and the client feels accountable for speed

### One-line summary you might share with a client
"Some challenges are about not knowing the answer; some are about people not being willing to live with the answer. The second kind doesn't get solved by a better plan."

## 2. Immunity to Change (Kegan + Lahey)

### Origin
Robert Kegan + Lisa Lahey. "Immunity to Change" (Kegan + Lahey, 2009). Kegan also: "In Over Our Heads," "The Evolving Self." Lahey: "How the Way We Talk Can Change the Way We Work." Roots in Harvard Graduate School of Education adult development research.

### What it is, in two sentences
A framework for uncovering the "competing commitment" — the unstated, often unconscious commitment that prevents a person from making a change they genuinely intend. The change isn't happening because something else is being held more tightly.

### When it's useful
- Behavior change that's stuck despite the client's stated commitment
- Pattern of recurring "I should but I don't" cycles
- The client is frustrated with themselves and can't explain why they keep falling back
- Leadership development work where insight isn't translating into action
- When the client says they want X but consistently chooses Y

### When it's not useful
- The change is new and the client hasn't tried it yet. Immunity to Change is for stuck change, not unattempted change
- The barrier is genuinely external (the structure prevents the change, not the person)
- The client is in acute distress and self-reflection of this depth is too much load

### The move the framework makes
The 4-column map:
- **Column 1: Commitment** — What do you want? The stated change goal.
- **Column 2: Doing / Not Doing** — What are you doing (or not doing) that's working against Column 1?
- **Column 3: Competing Commitment** — What other commitment is your behavior in Column 2 serving? What are you protecting?
- **Column 4: Big Assumption** — What belief or assumption holds Column 3 in place?

The work is at Column 4 — the assumption that, if surfaced and tested, can shift everything.

### Questions the framework invites
- "What's the commitment you've named you want to live? What are you doing that's working against it?"
- "If you stopped doing that thing, what would you be losing? What protection would you be giving up?"
- "What belief about yourself, others, or the world holds that competing commitment in place?"
- "What if that belief weren't fully true? What if it were only partly true?"
- "How might we test the big assumption, in a low-stakes way, in the next two weeks?"

### Where clients resist
- The work is internal and the client wants external solutions
- Surfacing the competing commitment can feel embarrassing ("I claim X but I'm actually committed to Y?")
- The big assumption is often something the client has held since childhood; testing it feels risky
- The framework asks for slow, careful work in a culture that rewards speed

### One-line summary you might share with a client
"Maybe you're not failing to change. Maybe you're succeeding at something else you haven't named yet."

## 3. Polarity Management

### Origin
Barry Johnson. "Polarity Management: Identifying and Managing Unsolvable Problems with Solvable Problems" (Johnson, 1992; updated multiple times). Polarity Partnerships continues the work.

### What it is, in two sentences
A framework for recognizing when a challenge isn't a problem to solve but a polarity to manage — two interdependent values that both need attention, like centralized vs. decentralized, stability vs. change, individual vs. team. Treating a polarity like a problem (picking one pole as the answer) creates the opposite problem.

### When it's useful
- The client is in a "this OR that" debate that keeps cycling
- The leader has picked a side (centralization, focus, structure) and is now experiencing the downside, and is tempted to flip to the other side
- Cultural or organizational pendulum swings that keep happening
- Decision-making frustration where neither option works
- When the client says "we just need to pick one and commit" but can't sustain the commitment

### When it's not useful
- When it really is an either/or decision (acquire or not; promote this person or not)
- When the client is using "it's both" to avoid making a real choice
- When time pressure requires picking a side and seeing what happens

### The move the framework makes
- Maps the polarity into 4 quadrants: upper-left (upside of Pole A), upper-right (upside of Pole B), lower-left (downside of Pole A overdone), lower-right (downside of Pole B overdone)
- Identifies the early warnings that you've overdone one pole (the lower-left or lower-right symptoms)
- Asks: how do you tap the upside of both poles, and what are the action steps when you sense yourself sliding to the downside of one?

### Questions the framework invites
- "Is this a problem to solve or a polarity to manage?"
- "What's the upside of the pole you're currently in? What's the upside of the opposite pole?"
- "What are the downsides you're experiencing right now? Are they the downsides of overdoing your current pole?"
- "What's the early warning sign that you've overdone this pole?"
- "What action step do you take when you sense yourself sliding to the downside?"
- "How do you tap the upside of the OTHER pole without flipping to it entirely?"

### Where clients resist
- It feels like "fence-sitting" — a way out of decision-making
- Leaders are trained to pick and commit; managing tension feels weak
- The framework asks for ongoing attention; clients want to solve and move on
- Cultural pressure for "clarity" punishes both/and thinking

### One-line summary you might share with a client
"Some challenges aren't problems with answers — they're tensions you steer, ongoing. Picking a side just creates the next problem."

## 4. Somatic Noticing

### Origin
Strozzi Institute (Richard Strozzi-Heckler). "The Anatomy of Change," "The Leadership Dojo," "In Search of the Warrior Spirit." Wendy Palmer, Stuart Heller, and others have related lineages. Roots in Aikido, somatic experiencing, and embodied leadership practice.

### What it is, in two sentences
A practice of attending to the body's signals — sensation, breath, posture, gesture — as a source of information about what the client is actually experiencing and committing to, beyond the words they're using. The body holds patterns the mind can rationalize past.

### When it's useful
- Client's words and behavior don't match (saying "I'm fine with this" while body says otherwise)
- Stress patterns that show up physically (shoulders rising, breath shallow, jaw tight) and the client doesn't notice
- Presence work — when the client needs to be more fully in the room
- Embodied practices for new leadership behaviors (the "felt sense" of saying no, holding ground, taking up space)
- When the conversation is going in circles and the client's body is signaling something

### When it's not useful
- When clinical territory is active (panic attacks, dissociation, trauma triggers). This is where somatic noticing becomes therapeutic and outside coaching scope. **Refer to a somatic-trained therapist.**
- When the client is uncomfortable with body-focused practice and the coach is forcing it
- When body-talk would feel performative or coachy in a culture that wouldn't welcome it
- When the coach is not somatically trained — don't deploy somatic frameworks you can't hold

### The move the framework makes
- Invites the client to notice their body in real-time during the conversation
- Surfaces the gap between stated commitment and physical signal
- Sometimes uses simple practices: lengthening the spine, three slow breaths, feeling feet on the ground, noticing where tension is held
- Distinct from breathwork-as-relaxation; this is awareness as information

### Questions the framework invites
- "What are you noticing in your body right now?"
- "When you say [X], where do you feel that in your body?"
- "What shifts physically when you imagine doing the thing we're discussing?"
- "If your body could speak about [Y], what would it say?"
- "What posture or breath would help you walk into the meeting Friday?"

### Where clients resist
- Body-focused work can feel foreign in corporate contexts
- Some clients have body-relationship history that makes this fraught (eating disorders, trauma history, chronic pain) — do not proceed without checking
- "I'm not a body person" — sometimes a real preference, sometimes resistance
- Coach must be comfortable in this work; awkward deployment is worse than not deploying

### One-line summary you might share with a client
"Your body knows things you haven't named yet. Sometimes listening to it is the work."

### Boundary note — IMPORTANT

Somatic work sits closer to the coaching-therapy line than other frameworks. If a client's body content includes:
- Panic symptoms (chest tightness, can't breathe, dissociation)
- Trauma activation (flashbacks, freezing, somatic memory)
- Chronic body pain that's central to their experience
- Body-image content tied to eating-disorder patterns

…the coach is not the right container. **Consult a licensed therapist for clinical concerns**, ideally one with somatic experiencing or trauma-informed training. The coaching version of somatic noticing is leadership-presence-focused. The clinical version of somatic work is therapy.

## Other frameworks worth knowing (one-line each)

- **Goldsmith Stakeholder-Centered Coaching** — feedforward instead of feedback; explicit stakeholder check-ins; behavior change with witnesses. Useful for "how others experience me" work
- **Situational Leadership (Hersey-Blanchard)** — match leadership style (directing / coaching / supporting / delegating) to follower readiness. Useful for managing diverse teams
- **Lominger / Korn Ferry Leadership Architect** — 67 competencies grouped into factors and clusters. Useful as a vocabulary, less so as a framework
- **Hogan Personality Inventory (HPI / HDS / MVPI)** — bright side / dark side / values. Requires certification to administer
- **EQi-2.0** — emotional intelligence assessment with 15 sub-scales. Requires certification
- **CliftonStrengths (Gallup)** — 34 strengths, work with top 5 or 10. Strengths-based development lens
- **5 Behaviors of a Cohesive Team (Lencioni)** — trust → conflict → commitment → accountability → results. Useful when grounded, pop when unmoored
- **Crucial Conversations (Patterson et al.)** — framework for high-stakes dialogue. Useful for specific scenarios

## What you do NOT do

- Deploy a framework you haven't internalized
- Cite a framework you've never read primary sources on
- Use a framework as a substitute for the coaching work
- Use somatic frameworks if you're not somatically trained
- Use clinical-adjacent frameworks (parts work, internal family systems, attachment frameworks) — those are therapy
- Diagnose clients via framework ("she's a 3 on the Enneagram")
- Make a framework into the answer when the work is the question

---

Things to verify before deploying any framework with a client:
- You've read the original source (or a credible derivative), not just absorbed it through cultural osmosis
- The specific client situation matches the framework's actual use case
- The friction you'd predict is real for this client (not your projection)
- The questions you'd ask are open, generative, and not leading toward the "right answer" the framework implies
- You're holding the framework loosely as a lens, not deploying it as a verdict
- Clinical-line check: nothing in the client's situation requires therapeutic intervention rather than coaching
