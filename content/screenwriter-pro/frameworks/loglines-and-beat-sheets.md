# Loglines and Beat Sheets — Framework Reference

The frameworks the kit operates on. Use this as the underlying structure for logline pressure-tests, feature beat sheets, TV pilot beat sheets, and character arc audits.

---

## Part 1: The logline framework

A logline does four jobs in 1-2 sentences:

1. **Protagonist** — specific, characterized in 2-3 words ("forensic accountant," "estranged daughter," "off-duty Marine")
2. **Inciting incident or central situation** — the thing that makes today different from yesterday
3. **Goal and obstacle** — what they're trying to do, what stands in the way
4. **Stakes or central irony** — what happens if they fail, OR the twist that makes the premise specific

### The logline pressure-test

When a writer gives you a premise, generate three variants:

**Tight version (~25 words, one sentence)**
The elevator pitch. Compressed.

> Example: "A junior forensic accountant discovers her firm is laundering money for an FBI informant — and that exposing it will destroy the only career she ever wanted."

**Open version (~40 words, two sentences)**
The version that lets you breathe and characterize.

> Example: "When a junior forensic accountant at a Big Four firm uncovers a money-laundering scheme inside her own office, she traces it to a senior partner she trusted. The deeper she digs, the more she realizes the FBI has known for two years — and that they need it to continue."

**Twist-forward version**
Leads with the central irony or the thing that makes it not a generic premise.

> Example: "The FBI has been protecting the money launderer for two years. A junior forensic accountant just walked into the trap they set."

The writer picks the version that sounds most like them. None of the three is "the right one." The right one is the one the writer can defend in a room.

### Loglines to refuse (forbidden patterns)

- Genre tags as loglines: "A psychological thriller about identity"
- No protagonist: "In a world where memories can be stolen, no one is safe"
- No obstacle: "A woman tries to find her sister" (try means nothing; what's the obstacle?)
- No stakes: "An accountant uncovers a fraud" (what happens if she succeeds or fails?)
- Pure premise: "What if everyone could read minds?" (cool, but where's the movie?)

## Part 2: Save the Cat 15-beat sheet (features)

Blake Snyder's framework. Reliable for commercial features. Page ranges below assume a 110-page script.

| # | Beat | Page | What it does |
|---|------|------|--------------|
| 1 | Opening Image | 1 | The before-state of the protagonist's world. Visual. |
| 2 | Theme Stated | 5 | Someone says the theme out loud (usually not to the protagonist). |
| 3 | Setup | 1-10 | We meet the protagonist and the world they're stuck in. |
| 4 | Catalyst | 10-12 | The thing that breaks the protagonist's normal life open. |
| 5 | Debate | 12-25 | The protagonist resists the call. The question: will they go? |
| 6 | Break into Two | 25 | They commit. New world. New rules. |
| 7 | B Story | 30 | A relationship subplot that carries the theme. |
| 8 | Fun and Games | 30-55 | The "promise of the premise" — the trailer moments. |
| 9 | Midpoint | 55 | A false victory or false defeat that raises the stakes. |
| 10 | Bad Guys Close In | 55-75 | Things deteriorate. The protagonist is losing ground. |
| 11 | All Is Lost | 75 | The lowest point. Often involves death (literal or metaphorical). |
| 12 | Dark Night of the Soul | 75-85 | Protagonist grapples with the loss. |
| 13 | Break into Three | 85 | A new idea. The synthesis of A and B story lessons. |
| 14 | Finale | 85-110 | The climax. The protagonist confronts the antagonist using what they learned. |
| 15 | Final Image | 110 | The after-state. Mirrors the opening image, transformed. |

When generating a Save the Cat beat sheet, produce for each beat:
- The beat name and page range
- What happens at this beat in this story (1-2 sentences with specifics)
- The promise this beat makes to the reader

### Worked example: Save the Cat for THE LAUNDRY (forensic accounting thriller)

1. **Opening Image (p1):** MAYA RIVERA (28, junior forensic accountant) arrives at her Big Four firm at 6:45 AM. Earliest in. Last to leave. Her father's prison release date is on a sticky note in her bottom drawer.
2. **Theme Stated (p5):** Her mentor: "The only audits that matter are the ones nobody asks you to run."
3. **Setup (p1-10):** Maya is up for senior promotion. She's the best in her class. She's also visibly worn down — and we learn her father went to prison for tax evasion ten years ago.
4. **Catalyst (p11):** She finds a discrepancy in a client account — a chain of car washes that's depositing four times its plausible revenue.
5. **Debate (p12-25):** Her senior partner DAVID HONG signed off on the audits. She raises it gently. He shuts her down. She tells herself she's wrong. She runs the numbers again.
6. **Break into Two (p25):** She makes a copy of the audit files and takes them home. New world: she's auditing the firm itself.
7. **B Story (p30):** ELENA, an old friend now at the FBI, agrees to meet for coffee. The relationship reopens. The B story carries the theme of trust.
8. **Fun and Games (p30-55):** Maya runs a shadow investigation from her apartment. Trail leads to the client — TONY CARRASCO, who owns 14 car washes across Chicago. She tails him. She finds the second set of books.
9. **Midpoint (p55):** Elena confirms what Maya already suspects: the FBI has known about Carrasco for two years. He's their informant. Maya is now working against the federal government.
10. **Bad Guys Close In (p55-75):** David Hong notices the access logs on the client files. He confronts Maya. The firm starts to isolate her. Carrasco's people start watching her apartment.
11. **All Is Lost (p75):** Elena is reassigned. Maya is suspended pending an internal review. Her father's release date is in six days. The promotion is gone.
12. **Dark Night of the Soul (p75-85):** Maya nearly destroys the files. She visits her father in prison. He tells her he's proud of her for the first time in ten years — and asks her not to be him.
13. **Break into Three (p85):** Maya doesn't go to the FBI. She goes to the SEC. The SEC has jurisdiction the FBI can't override.
14. **Finale (p85-110):** Maya hands over the files to a senior SEC investigator. David Hong is indicted. Carrasco is arrested on tax charges that bypass the FBI's informant agreement. The firm collapses.
15. **Final Image (p110):** Maya at 6:45 AM, alone in a small office on the 12th floor of the SEC building. Same sticky note. Different drawer. The promotion she wanted is on a different track now.

## Part 3: Hour drama pilot — 5-act structure

Standard for network hour dramas; streaming and cable often use this as a baseline. Page ranges assume a 58-page pilot.

**Teaser (p1-8):**
- Cold open. Hook the audience in the first 90 seconds.
- Often a tense scene that doesn't make sense until later
- End with the protagonist or the central question

**Act 1 (p8-20):**
- World establishment
- Introduce the protagonist, their want, and the world they operate in
- Inciting incident — the case, the call, the discovery
- Act 1 out (p20-22): a question or escalation that earns the next act

**Act 2 (p20-32):**
- Investigation, complication, world deepens
- B-story setup with a runner character
- Midpoint reveal (p30-32): a twist that changes what the protagonist thought was happening
- Act 2 out: stakes raised

**Act 3 (p32-44):**
- Things get harder. Antagonist or obstacle escalates.
- A-story and B-story collide
- Personal cost for the protagonist
- Act 3 out: someone the protagonist trusted is compromised, or a piece of identity is challenged

**Act 4 (p44-58):**
- Climax and resolution of the episode's main conflict
- But not the SERIES conflict — that's what earns episode 2
- The "kicker" or button: a final scene that opens the door to next week
- For a pilot specifically: prove the show, not just the episode

When generating a hour drama pilot beat sheet, produce for each act:
- The act number and page range
- What happens in this story at this act
- The act-out — the question, escalation, or twist that earns the next act
- For Act 4 specifically: how this episode resolves AND how the series continues

## Part 4: Half-hour single-cam pilot structure

Faster cuts. Three acts.

**Cold open / teaser (p1-4):**
- 1-2 pages
- A scene that establishes voice and shows the protagonist in their problem
- Often a self-contained comic premise that pays off the title

**Act 1 (p4-16):**
- The episode's A-story sets up
- B-story introduced (often a supporting character runner)
- Act-out around page 14-16

**Act 2 (p16-28):**
- A and B stories converge
- The protagonist makes the worst possible choice (or the best one, with consequences)
- Resolution that doesn't reset the world — half-hour comedy is incremental change

**Button (p28-32):**
- The capper. Often the funniest moment.
- For a pilot: leaves a runner thread for episode 2

---

## Part 5: Limited series pilot structure

Hybrid. Feature-style opening + episode-2 hook.

- Opens like a feature's Act 1 (we meet the protagonist, the inciting situation hits)
- First plot point arrives around the act-out (p40-50 in a 55-page pilot)
- Ends not with resolution but with the question that drives the season
- The pilot should make the next 5-7 episodes feel inevitable

## Part 6: Character arc audit template

For every protagonist, run this:

```
Name: 
Situation in scene 1:

WANT (external, drives the plot):
NEED (internal, drives the arc):
WOUND (the pre-script event still shaping them):
LIE (the false belief they hold because of the wound):
TRUTH (what the story forces them to confront):

What they're wrong about in scene 1:
What they understand by FADE OUT:

Two scenes where WANT and NEED collide:
1. (Scene where they choose the WANT at the cost of the NEED)
2. (Scene where they finally choose the NEED)

Three actions that show character without dialogue:
1.
2.
3.
```

Worked example for Maya Rivera (THE LAUNDRY):

```
Name: Maya Rivera
Situation in scene 1: First in the office, last to leave, up for promotion.

WANT: Get promoted to senior, secure the career she's built.
NEED: Stop measuring herself against the absence of her father.
WOUND: Father went to prison for tax evasion when she was 18. She testified.
LIE: If she works hard enough and clean enough, she'll never be like him.
TRUTH: The system she's defending is the same system that prosecuted her father — and she has to choose whether to serve it or correct it.

What she's wrong about in scene 1: That working inside the firm proves she's nothing like her father.
What she understands by FADE OUT: That her father's mistake was small and the system's was large, and that her job is to audit the system, not herself.

Two scenes where WANT and NEED collide:
1. Page 25: She makes the copy of the audit files. WANT says "leave it alone, take the promotion." NEED says "look."
2. Page 85: She goes to the SEC instead of the FBI. WANT is already lost. NEED chooses correction over self-preservation.

Three actions that show character without dialogue:
1. Page 7: She replaces her morning coffee with a glass of water mid-pour when her phone rings — her father.
2. Page 33: She wipes the audit files from her work laptop and writes them to a personal drive. Doesn't say a word.
3. Page 88: She drops her firm ID in the SEC building's lobby trash can on her way out.
```

The three actions are the spine of how the audience reads the character. If you can't write three, the arc isn't on the page yet.

## How to use this reference

1. Pick the format and the framework that fits the project.
2. Read the structure plus the worked example.
3. Paste your premise + protagonist details + comps into the AI with the framework name.
4. The AI returns a draft. You revise into your voice and your specifics.

If your premise can't generate three logline variants, it's not ready. Go back to the premise.
