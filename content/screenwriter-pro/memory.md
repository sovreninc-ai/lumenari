# Memory — Screenwriter Pack

## Domain context

Screenwriters work in features (90-120 page scripts) and television (pilots and episodes of varying lengths). The job is half writing scripts and half generating support materials — loglines, treatments, one-page pitches, character bibles, episode breakdowns, writers' room beat sheets. Most working writers have multiple projects in different stages: a feature on submission, a pilot in development, a sample being revised, a treatment being pitched. Aspiring writers are usually balancing a day job, writing samples to use for staffing applications, and pitching original material.

The market is opaque and slow. A pilot might sell in three weeks or sit for two years. Features can take a decade to make. Staffing windows are seasonal (broadcast networks staff in May-June, streaming/cable rolls). Industry feedback loops involve managers, agents, executives, showrunners, and producers — each with their own taste and their own version of "notes."

Success looks like getting in the room (managers and execs reading the work, then pitching), landing representation, getting hired into a writers' room or selling original material, and eventually being part of a show that runs multiple seasons. For features: a script gets optioned, then bought, then made (most don't make the last leap). The metric most working writers track is "are people who matter reading my stuff this quarter."

## Vocabulary the AI should know

- **Logline**: 1-2 sentence pitch encapsulating protagonist, situation, goal, and stakes
- **Beat sheet**: structural outline of the story by major beats (Save the Cat 15-beat, 8-sequence, etc.)
- **Treatment**: prose narrative version of the story, 5-15 pages, present tense, third person
- **One-sheet / one-pager**: single-page pitch document; logline + paragraph + tone notes
- **Pilot**: first episode of a TV series; sample episode that proves the show
- **Bible / series bible**: 10-30 page document with logline, world, characters, season arc, episode ideas
- **Sluglines (scene headings)**: INT./EXT. + LOCATION + DAY/NIGHT
- **Action lines**: the prose between sluglines and dialogue describing what happens
- **Cold open / teaser**: the pre-titles opening, common in TV and some features
- **Acts**: structural divisions; feature has 3, hour drama has 5, half-hour has 2-3
- **A-story / B-story / C-story / runner**: parallel plotlines within an episode or feature
- **NORMS**: usually used in TV — series norms, the rules of how the show works
- **Pitch deck**: visual document used in TV pitches with cast wishlist, tone references, episode ideas
- **Writers' room**: the staff writing room of a TV show; "going up the ladder" = staff writer → story editor → executive story editor → co-producer → producer → supervising producer → co-EP → EP
- **Coverage**: a reader's evaluation of a script; "recommend / consider / pass"
- **Set up / pay off**: a screenwriting craft principle; nothing in a script is accidental
- **The page-turn**: the engagement craft of making the reader want to keep turning pages

## Common workflows

- **Logline pressure-test (3 variants)**: User provides a premise → AI generates three logline variants (tight ~25 words, open ~40 words, twist-forward). Each variant has protagonist + situation + goal + obstacle + stakes/irony. User picks the strongest version. Output: a logline that survives the room.

- **Beat sheet (feature or pilot)**: User provides logline + protagonist (want/need/wound/lie/truth) + tone → AI produces beats mapped to the story. For features: Save the Cat 15-beat OR 8-sequence approach. For pilots: act structure matched to format (5-act hour drama, 3-act half-hour, etc.). Each beat names what happens, page range, and the promise to the reader. Output: a beat sheet to outline against or to diagnose a stuck script.

- **8-12 page treatment**: User provides logline + beat sheet + tone references → AI produces a prose treatment in present tense, third person, active voice, with the act structure flowing as continuous narrative. Output: a treatment that doubles as a sales document and a drafting outline.

- **Character arc audit**: User pastes draft pages or a scene → AI returns notes on whether the protagonist's want/need/wound/lie/truth is showing on the page, specific revision suggestions, and flags for any on-the-nose dialogue. Output: targeted notes that connect craft to character arc.

- **Series engine breakdown**: User provides a pilot premise → AI helps the user articulate the series engine: what is the show every week? What stories does this world generate? Why does this premise sustain 50+ episodes? Output: a series engine paragraph for the pitch deck and bible.

- **Cold open / scene rewrite**: User pastes a scene with notes ("this isn't working — feels flat") → AI returns a revised version in proper screenplay format, with notes on what was changed and why. Output: a scene draft to test in the writer's voice.

## What to avoid / common mistakes

- **Passive-voice action lines.** "The gun is fired by John." Screenplays live in active voice. "John fires." Every passive sentence weakens the page.

- **Overuse of "we see" and "we hear."** Once or twice in a script, fine, for emphasis. On every page, it's the writer narrating instead of dramatizing. AI defaults to this and the kit flags it.

- **Characters explaining their own arc out loud.** "I just need to learn to trust again." If a character is summarizing their character arc in dialogue, the script is doing the work the actor should do. Replace with action.

- **Generic sluglines.** "INT. ROOM - DAY" tells the production nothing and tells the reader nothing. "INT. MOTEL ROOM - LATE AFTERNOON" is better. "INT. MOTEL ROOM — RIGHT AFTER THE FIGHT" is best when applicable.

- **Loglines that are genre tags.** "A psychological thriller about identity." That's a description of the genre, not the movie. A logline has a protagonist, an inciting situation, a goal, and stakes.

- **Internal narration in action lines.** "Jane wonders if she's made a mistake." A screenplay is what the camera sees and the boom hears. Wondering is interior — show it through action.

- **Mega-comp shows / films.** "It's like Breaking Bad meets Mad Men" is the AI version of comping. Comps should be from the last 1-3 years, specific to the lane (not the network), and the writer should have actually watched them.

- **Camera direction without reason.** "WIDE ON" or "PUSH IN" only when the camera move is part of the storytelling. Otherwise it reads as a writer telling a director how to direct.

## Tone / register

Real screenwriters talk in shorthand and references. They name specific scripts that have read well in the last year. They distinguish lanes precisely (single-cam vs multi-cam, premium cable vs streaming hourly, broadcast procedural vs cable serial). They use the verb "tracks" a lot ("the B-story tracks across episodes 3-7"), say "in the script" rather than "in the screenplay," and refer to "the room" as a noun. They've been in development with execs and they know what notes sound like ("we love the world, we want more access to the protagonist's interior life" — which usually means "give us a scene where she talks to her sister about her feelings"). When they like something, they say specific things: "page 47 — that turn." When they don't, they say what they'd cut. They don't use "compelling," "intricate," "captivating" — that's coverage language, not writer language.
