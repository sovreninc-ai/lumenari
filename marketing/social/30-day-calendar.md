### Day 2 — [Day 2] — The core thesis: structure beats cleverness

**Tweet (≤270 chars)**

Hot take: prompt engineering is the wrong frame.

The win isn't a cleverer sentence. It's giving the model a structured role, context, examples, and constraints.

That's a kit, not a prompt.

**LinkedIn (200-400 words)**

I've watched smart people lose entire afternoons trying to "engineer" a better prompt.

The truth is, prompt cleverness has diminishing returns past about prompt #3. What actually moves the output is structure: a defined role, the right context, real examples, and explicit constraints on format and tone.

That's not a prompt. That's a kit.

The shape I've landed on after building 100 of them looks like this:

1. **Role definition** — who the AI is acting as, and the seniority level
2. **Context blocks** — the user fills in the parts only they know (industry, region, customer type)
3. **Reference examples** — 2-3 samples of what good output looks like
4. **Output contract** — the exact shape, length, and tone of the response
5. **Edge case handling** — what to do when the input is incomplete or weird

Drop that into Claude or ChatGPT and the difference is not subtle. A real estate agent doing a market analysis goes from a generic "here's a polite paragraph" to a comp-by-comp breakdown with pricing recommendations and a buyer-side narrative.

This is also why I'm betting on SKILL.md as a portable standard. A markdown file with sections is not glamorous, but it's the lowest-common-denominator format that works across every model and every editor.

If you're still copy-pasting one-line prompts in 2026, you're leaving most of the model on the table.

### Day 16 — [Day 16] — The decision to bet on SKILL.md

**Tweet (≤270 chars)**

Picking SKILL.md as the canonical format was a bet.

It wasn't standard yet. Claude was the only model deeply supporting it. I bet other tools would converge on a markdown-with-sections format because it's the lowest-common-denominator that scales.

So far, looks right.

**LinkedIn (200-400 words)**

A founder decision I want to break down: why I picked SKILL.md as the canonical format for Lumenari.

When I started building, three formats were on the table:

1. **JSON system prompts** — structured, parseable, but unfriendly to humans editing them
2. **YAML config files** — popular in the dev tooling world, but verbose and quirky
3. **Markdown with sections (SKILL.md)** — readable, portable, easy to author, less precise

Markdown won. Here's the reasoning.

**Authoring matters more than parsing.** Most kits get edited by humans, not machines. A format that's easy to read and edit beats a format that's easy to parse, especially when the consumer (an LLM) is fine with either.

**Lowest-common-denominator portability.** Every model can read markdown. Every IDE can render it. Every git workflow handles it natively. The moment you reach for JSON or YAML, you've added a tooling tax.

**Convergence signal.** When I was making this call, Anthropic was leaning hard into SKILL.md natively. Cursor was experimenting. The signal wasn't that markdown was the standard yet — it was that the smart tools were converging on it. Better to be early on the right side of a convergence than late.

**The downside I accepted.** Markdown is loose. It can't enforce structure the way a JSON schema can. A poorly-written SKILL.md is just a poorly-written doc. I mitigate this with internal linting and a strict template, but the format itself doesn't enforce.

Six months in, the bet is looking right. SKILL.md is becoming the de-facto format for portable AI skills, and Lumenari was early. The Custom GPT, optimization pack, and memory.md formats we ship for non-Claude models are all derived from the canonical SKILL.md, which keeps the maintenance tax bounded.

If you're building anything in the AI skills space and trying to pick a format, my recommendation is the same one I made for myself: pick the one that's easiest to author, accept the looseness, and trust that the ecosystem will converge to the readable thing.

### Day 20 — [Day 20] — The lesson from a kit that flopped

**Tweet (≤270 chars)**

**Why I built it:** the wizard kept getting "trading" and "investing" queries, and I figured a thoughtful kit could differentiate from the firehose of pump-and-dump prompts on Twitter. The plan was: education-first, risk-management-heavy, no signal generation.

**What actually happened:**

1. **The audience didn't want a thoughtful kit.** Buyers expected price predictions and entry/exit signals. The kit pointedly did not produce those. Refund requests rolled in within 48 hours from people who said the kit was "too cautious" or "didn't tell me what to buy."

2. **The signal-to-noise was bad.** Even when I trained the model heavily on risk frameworks, the output still got used as if it were advice. I could see in the support emails that buyers were treating the kit's "consider these factors" output as "the kit said to buy this."

**The decision:** pulled the kit, refunded everyone who'd bought it (32 people), and moved on. Total time invested: about 14 hours building, 6 hours supporting, 2 hours refunding.

**The lesson:** not every wizard signal should become a kit. Sometimes the signal means "this audience exists" and the right answer is "this audience is wrong for us." A curated catalog needs the discipline to say no, even after building a yes.

I'm not building anything in active trading, gambling, or unlicensed legal/medical advice. The Estate Planning kit and the Pharmacist kit are both heavily framed as "structured starting points for a professional conversation, not advice." That framing is the line.

If you build product, the kits you don't ship matter as much as the ones you do.
